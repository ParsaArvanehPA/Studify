Introduction: Toward a Science of Translating

Study Guide · Eugene A. Nida, Toward a Science of Translating, Ch. 1
Why translation needed a science — the enduring problems, the tensions, and the interdisciplinary foundations

1. What This Chapter Is Actually About

Here's the scene: It's 1964. Translation has been happening for thousands of years, but nobody has really studied it systematically. Nida's opening chapter is his manifesto: translation should be treated as a science — a descriptive science, like linguistics. The practice has "far outdistanced" the theory, and it's time to catch up. He surveys the problems (form vs. meaning, letter vs. spirit), lays out the field (using Jakobson's three types), and shows how insights from anthropology, psychology, linguistics, and philosophy can all contribute to understanding translation.
Know Nida's framing of translation as a "science" (what he means by that), the two fundamental tensions (form/meaning and letter/spirit), Jakobson's three types, and his interdisciplinary cast of characters (Sapir, Chomsky, Korzybski, etc.).

2. The Enduring Problems of Interlingual Communication

Nida opens with a sweeping image: from the multilingual scribes of ancient Babylon sending cuneiform tablets across the empire, to the electronic equipment used for simultaneous interpreting at the UN — the basic problems remain the same. What's different is the urgency: modern technology means we NEED effective understanding between peoples more than ever.

And yet, translation has always had its critics. Grant Showerman called it "meddling with inspiration." Harry de Forest Smith compared a translated literary work to "a stewed strawberry." Max Eastman declared that "almost all translations are bad." There have even been theological objections: the medieval Jewish *Masseketh Sopherim* said the day the Law was translated into Greek "was a hard day for Israel, like the day on which Israel made the golden calf."

"Nothing improves by translation except bishops." — but also: "A live sparrow is better than a stuffed eagle." (Fitzgerald)

3. The Fundamental Tensions That Never Go Away

Underneath all the complications of translation are two basic tensions that Nida wants to address head-on:
Form vs. Meaning: If you try to preserve the stylistic qualities of the original, you sacrifice meaning. If you stick strictly to the literal content, you lose the stylistic flavor. There's no perfect solution — every translation is a trade-off.
Letter vs. Spirit: Being faithful to the "things talked about" can destroy the spirit that pervades the original. But over-reproducing the "feeling" and "tone" risks playing loose with the substance.

And it gets worse — language is always changing, translators are caught between conflicting pressures, and as Julio Casares put it: "Translation is a customs house through which passes, if the custom officers are not alert, more smuggled goods of foreign idioms than through any other linguistic frontier."

4. Art or Science?

Is translating an art or a science? Nida says: both. The practice has far outdistanced theory — no one denies the artistic elements in good translating, but linguists and philologists are increasingly aware that the processes of translation are "amenable to rigorous description." When Nida says "the science of translating," he means the descriptive aspect — just as linguistics is a descriptive science. Those who insist it's "an art and nothing more" fail to probe beneath the surface; those who take the opposite view rarely study translating enough to appreciate the artistic sensitivity involved.

5. The Field of Translation (After Jakobson)

Nida uses Jakobson's three-way division to map the territory:

  • Intralingual translation — rewording within the same language. When you rephrase a complex sentence, that's a kind of translation.
  • Interlingual translation ("translation proper") — between two languages. But it's not just matching symbols word-for-word; you need to understand the entire utterance.
  • Intersemiotic translation ("transmutation") — from one sign system to another. A verbal message turned into flag signals. A speech turned into sign language.

But Translation Faces Real Restrictions

  • Cultural restrictions: Jiggs's corned beef and cabbage doesn't make sense in Southeast Asia (it becomes rice and fish), Turkey (stuffed cabbage), or Italy (stewed codfish). Translation happens between cultures, not just languages.
  • Stylistic restrictions (poetry): In poetry, form IS part of the meaning. Translating the sense without the form means you haven't translated the poem.
  • Medium restrictions (dubbing): Dubbing a film means matching timing, syllabic structure, and even facial movements. The medium imposes its own rules.
Why Bible translation? Nida's book draws heavily on Bible translation experience, and he's upfront about it. Bible translating surpasses all other types in: range of subject matter, linguistic variety (into 1,200+ languages), historical depth (3rd century BCE to present), cultural diversity, volume of manuscript evidence, number of translators, and accumulated data on principles. But the book is NOT restricted to Scriptural materials — it's concerned with "the entire range of translating."

6. The Interdisciplinary Foundations — Nida's Big Cast

What makes Nida's approach distinctive is how many fields he draws on. Translation, for him, can't be understood through linguistics alone:
  • Anthropological semantics — Wilhelm von Humboldt (language, thought, and culture are profoundly related)
  • Structural linguistics — Ferdinand de Saussure (the basis for dealing with semantic problems)
  • Anthropology — Sapir, Whorf, Malinowski, Goodenough (language as a guide to social reality; the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis)
  • General semantics — Korzybski ("the map is not the territory" — three key claims: words aren't reality, they don't include everything, and language contains symbols for talking about language — "metalanguage")
  • Symbolic logic — Russell, Wittgenstein ("Alle Philosophie ist Sprachphilosophie" — all philosophy is the philosophy of language)
  • Psychology — Gestalt psychology, Skinner, Miller/Galanter/Pribram (behavioral predispositions, feedback, the relationship between symbol and behavior)
  • Psychiatry — Jung, Fromm (the importance of symbols and their emotive significance)
  • Generative grammar — Noam Chomsky (1957) — this is crucial for Nida's later model. Language is NOT a fixed corpus of sentences but a "dynamic mechanism capable of generating an infinite series of different utterances." The translator needs to describe the mechanisms by which a message is "decoded, transferred, and transformed into the structures of another language."
The key Chomskyan insight for Nida: You can't just compare structures between languages. You need to explain how one can take a UNIQUE message in the source language and "create" an equally unique message in the target language. That requires looking at language in a generative manner — not just taxonomically.
You don't need to memorize every name in that list, but you should know that Nida is deliberately interdisciplinary. The quote from I. A. Richards is exam gold: translation may be "what may very probably be the most complex type of event yet produced in the evolution of the cosmos." Nida uses this to justify why translation needs a science — it's too complex to leave to intuition alone.

Exam Q&A · Check Yourself

Q: What does Nida mean by a "science of translating"?
A: The descriptive study of how messages are transferred from one language to another. Just as linguistics can be studied descriptively, so can translation processes. He's NOT saying translation is purely mechanical — he acknowledges the artistic sensitivity needed — but he insists the processes can be rigorously described.
Q: What are the two fundamental tensions in translation Nida identifies?
A: Form vs. Meaning (preserving style sacrifices meaning; preserving literal content loses style) and Letter vs. Spirit (being faithful to the "things talked about" can destroy the original's spirit; over-reproducing the "feeling" risks being unfaithful to the substance).
Q: What are Jakobson's three types of translation as used by Nida?
A: Intralingual (rewording, same language), Interlingual ("translation proper" between two languages), and Intersemiotic ("transmutation" between sign systems, e.g. verbal → flag signals).
Q: Why does Nida base so much of his theory on Bible translation?
A: Because Bible translating surpasses ALL other types in range of subject matter, linguistic variety (1,200+ languages), historical depth, cultural diversity, volume of manuscript evidence, and accumulated data on principles and procedures — making it the richest dataset for a general theory of translation.
Q: What key idea from Chomsky does Nida adopt, and why is it important?
A: The idea that language is a generative mechanism, not a fixed corpus. A translator can't just compare structures — they need to explain how a unique source message is decoded, transferred, and transformed into an equally unique target message. This goes beyond "mere lists of correspondences."
Q: Is translation an art or a science according to Nida?
A: Both. Those who insist it's "an art and nothing more" fail to probe the underlying principles; those who take the opposite view don't appreciate the artistic sensitivity needed. The "science" part is the descriptive, systematic analysis — the "art" is the creative execution.
