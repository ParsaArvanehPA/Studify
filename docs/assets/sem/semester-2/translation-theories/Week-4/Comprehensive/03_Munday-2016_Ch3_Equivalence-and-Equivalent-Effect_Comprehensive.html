Equivalence and Equivalent Effect

Study Guide · Jeremy Munday, Introducing Translation Studies (2016), Ch. 3
From Jakobson to Koller — the search for a systematic theory of equivalence, with case studies

1. What This Chapter Is Actually About

The big picture: The old "literal vs. free" debate was going nowhere — it was too vague, too subjective. So in the 1950s and 1960s, translation theorists tried to get more systematic. They started talking about meaning and equivalence. This chapter traces that journey: from Jakobson's linguistic take on equivalence, through Nida's famous formal/dynamic distinction and the principle of equivalent effect, to Newmark's semantic/communicative translation and Koller's five equivalence relations. Then there are case studies (Genesis 1, the Treaty of Maastricht) that show these concepts in action.
The three things you MUST know from this chapter: (1) Nida's formal vs. dynamic equivalence + equivalent effect, (2) Newmark's semantic vs. communicative translation, (3) Koller's five equivalence types. The Genesis case study is the best concrete example of formal vs. dynamic in action — bring it up in your essay.

2. Roman Jakobson: Meaning Between Languages

Key claim: "There is ordinarily no full equivalence between code-units." Russian syr isn't identical to English "cheese" — because syr doesn't include cottage cheese (that's tvarog). Translation substitutes whole messages, not separate code-units.

Jakobson draws on Saussure's distinction between signifier (the sound/written signal) and signified (the concept) — together they make the linguistic sign, which is arbitrary. The sign "cheese" could just as easily have been "bread" or anything else.

But here's where it gets theoretical: there's a tension between linguistic universalism (languages differ in surface form but share underlying ways of thinking) and linguistic determinism (the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis — language shapes thought). If determinism were absolute, translation would be impossible. But translation happens all the time, so...

Jakobson's resolution: "Languages differ essentially in what they must convey and not in what they may convey" — some languages force you to specify gender, aspect, etc. But "all is conveyable in any existing language." The exception? Poetry, where form and sense are fused — that requires "creative transposition," not translation.

3. Nida and the Science of Translating

The Chomsky Connection

Nida was inspired by Chomsky's generative-transformational grammar. His three-stage model: analysis → transfer → restructuring. The ST surface structure is analysed into kernels (basic structural elements) by a process of back-transformation, then transferred, then restructured into TT surface structure. Four functional classes: events (often verbs), objects (nouns), abstracts (adjectives/adverbs), relationals (prepositions, conjunctions).

Example: "will of God" — back-transformation = "B (object, God) performs A (event, wills)." "Creation of the world" = "B (the world) is performed by A (event, creates)." Nida claims all languages have 6-12 basic kernel structures and agree more on kernels than on surface structures.

Three Kinds of Meaning

Linguistic meaning: relationships between structures — "his house" (possession) ≠ "his journey" (he performs) ≠ "his kindness" (a quality of him)
Referential meaning: the denotative, dictionary meaning (son = male child)
Emotive/connotative meaning: the associations words carry ("Don't worry, son" = endearment or condescension, depending on context)

Nida developed techniques for analysing meaning: hierarchical structuring (animal → goat, dog, cow), componential analysis (plotting kinship terms by sex, generation, lineality — useful for languages with different kinship systems), and semantic structure analysis (separating out the different senses of "spirit": demons, angels, gods, ghost, ethos, alcohol — meaning is "conditioned" by context).

Formal vs. Dynamic Equivalence — THE KEY CONCEPT

Nida dumps the old "literal/free/faithful" vocabulary and replaces it with TWO basic orientations:
  • Formal equivalence (later "formal correspondence"): focuses on the message itself, in both form and content. ST-oriented. Typical of "gloss translations" with footnotes, giving the reader close access to the source language and customs. Used in academic/legal contexts.
  • Dynamic equivalence (later "functional"): based on the principle of equivalent effect — the relationship between the receptor and the message should be "substantially the same" as that between the original receptors and the message. Aims at "complete naturalness of expression" and "the closest natural equivalent." The message is tailored to the receptor's linguistic needs and cultural expectations.
Nida's four requirements for a translation: (1) making sense; (2) conveying the spirit and manner of the original; (3) having a natural and easy form; (4) producing a similar response. The priority rule: "correspondence in meaning must have priority over correspondence in style."

The Criticism

Equivalent effect has been heavily criticized. Van den Broeck and Larose: How do you MEASURE "effect"? How can a text have the SAME effect in two different cultures and times? Qian Hu (in a series of Meta articles, 1992-94) demonstrated the "implausibility" of equivalent response, especially when meaning is bound up in form. Gentzler attacks Nida from a deconstructionist perspective: dynamic equivalence is designed to convert receptors to Protestant Christianity. Ironically, some religious groups ALSO attack Nida — for being TOO free with the Word of God. The famous controversial example: "greet one another with a holy kiss" → "give one another a hearty handshake all round."

4. Newmark: Semantic and Communicative Translation

Peter Newmark felt that equivalent effect was "illusory." He proposed his own pair: semantic vs. communicative translation.
ParameterSemantic TranslationCommunicative Translation
FocusThought processes of the transmitter (author)TT reader, oriented to specific language/culture
CultureStays within SL cultureTransfers foreign elements into TL culture
Relation to STAlways "inferior" to ST; "loss" of meaningMay be "better" than ST; "gain" in force and clarity
TL formMore complex, detailed, tends to overtranslateSmoother, simpler, tends to undertranslate
Best forSerious literature, autobiography, important political statementsMost texts: technical, informative, publicity, popular fiction
Newmark's rule: where equivalent effect IS secured, literal translation is "not only the best, it is the only valid method." Where there's conflict, communicative translation wins — e.g., bissiger Hund → "beware of the dog!" not "dog that bites!"

5. Koller: Five Equivalence Relations

Werner Koller distinguished correspondence (contrastive linguistics, comparing language systems — langue) from equivalence (specific ST-TT pairs and contexts — parole). He then identified five types of equivalence, constrained by "double linkage" (the ST on one side, the receiver's needs on the other):
  • Denotative — the extralinguistic content ("content invariance"). First thing to try.
  • Connotative — lexical choices, near-synonyms. One of the hardest problems, often only approximate. Includes formality, social usage, geographical origin, stylistic effect, frequency, evaluation, emotion.
  • Text-normative — text-type norms. Different text types behave differently (linked to Reiss's work).
  • Pragmatic ("communicative") — oriented toward the receiver. = Nida's dynamic equivalence. Can override other requirements.
  • Formal ("expressive") — form, aesthetics, wordplay, rhyme, metaphor. NOT the same as Nida's "formal equivalence." Requires creativity.
Worked example: "I had wanted for years to get Mrs Thatcher in front of my camera. As she got more powerful she got sort of sexier." If translated denotatively into Arabic, "sexy" might convey "pornographic." Connotative (e.g., "attractiveness") is better but too direct for a magazine. Text-normative requires less direct phrasing. Pragmatic — the translator might choose "attractive femininity" or add "so to speak." Formal equivalence would need creative stylistic forms in the TL.

6. Case Study 1: Genesis 1 — Formal vs. Dynamic in Action

This is the best practical example of what Nida was talking about. Three English versions of the same Hebrew passage:

KJV (1611): "In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth. And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters."
→ Closest to formal equivalence. Retains the Hebrew metaphor "face of..." Uses "Spirit of God." Repeats "and" three times (formal syntactic device from Hebrew/Greek).

NEB (1970): "Now the earth was without shape and empty, and darkness was over the surface of the watery deep, but the Spirit of God was moving over the surface of the water."
→ Dynamic equivalence. Explicates "surface" and "watery deep." Adds "Now" for cohesion. Clear, explanatory.

NAB (1970): "The earth was a formless wasteland, and darkness covered the abyss, while a mighty wind swept over the waters."
→ Strongly dynamic. "Mighty wind" instead of "Spirit of God" is a major interpretative choice. "Formless wasteland." Cohesive "when" and "while."

What this shows: even within dynamic equivalence, there's a range. All three seek "equivalent effect" but the effect itself shifts — the KJV is now a formal, canonized archaism; the NEB is modern British; the NAB is modern American. And interestingly, Nida himself thought the KJV's threefold "and" needed adjustment to avoid "babyish" English.

7. Case Study 2: The Treaty of Maastricht — Equivalence in Law

EU law requires all language versions to be equally valid. This demands a high degree of formal equivalence. Compare:

English: "By this Treaty, the HIGH CONTRACTING PARTIES establish among themselves a EUROPEAN UNION..."

Portuguese: "Pelo presente Tratado, as ALTAS PARTES CONTRATANTES instituem entre si uma UNIAO EUROPEIA..."

Adjustments are minimal — Portuguese "presente" for "this," added "por." The structures track each other closely. But even here, problems arise: the English says "creating an ever closer union" (ongoing process), while the French reads "une union sans cesse plus étroite" (the goal is achieved by this treaty). Different interpretations in equally valid legal texts show the limits of formal equivalence.

8. Later Developments: Pym's Natural vs. Directional

Anthony Pym (2007) distinguishes two types of equivalence: Natural — identifying naturally-occurring terms in SL and TL (like you'd find in glossaries/term bases). Directional — rendering ST meaning in the TT (translation memories impose existing "directional" equivalents via exact and fuzzy matches). The rise of CAT tools has given a new twist to this distinction.
Equivalence continues to be criticized for circularity (Kenny: "equivalence defines translation, and translation defines equivalence"). Bassnett: translation involves far more than replacing lexical and grammatical items. The tertium comparationis (the invariant against which segments are measured) is inevitably subjective. Toury abandons prescriptive equivalence altogether — he takes equivalence as a given and studies the web of relations between ST and TT.

Exam Q&A · Check Yourself

Q: Distinguish Nida's formal and dynamic equivalence.
A: Formal equivalence focuses on the message itself in form and content, is ST-oriented, and often uses gloss translations with footnotes. Dynamic equivalence is based on equivalent effect — aims at naturalness and the "closest natural equivalent," tailoring the message to the receptor's needs.
Q: How do the KJV, NEB, and NAB versions of Genesis 1 demonstrate Nida's categories?
A: The KJV is closest to formal equivalence (retains Hebrew metaphors, "Spirit of God," threefold "and"). The NEB is dynamically oriented (explicates "surface," "watery deep"). The NAB is the most dynamic ("mighty wind" for "Spirit of God"). The case shows a spectrum within dynamic equivalence itself.
Q: What is the principle of equivalent effect and what are its main criticisms?
A: The relationship between receptor and message should be substantially the same as that between the original receptors and message. Criticisms: (1) impossible to measure "effect" (van den Broeck, Larose); (2) can't have the same effect across different cultures and times; (3) Gentzler: Nida's approach is covertly proselytizing (converting receptors to Protestant Christianity).
Q: Compare Newmark's semantic and communicative translation.
A: Semantic renders exact contextual meaning as closely as syntax allows, stays within SL culture, overtranslates (for serious literature). Communicative aims at an effect as close as possible to the original's, transfers foreign elements, tends to undertranslate (for most texts).
Q: List Koller's five equivalence relations with the Thatcher example.
A: Denotative (content; "sexy" → "sexy" = pornographic in Arabic). Connotative (near-synonyms; "attractiveness" is better but too direct). Text-normative (text-type norms). Pragmatic (receiver-oriented; "attractive femininity"). Formal (aesthetics/wordplay; would need creative TL forms). Hierarchically ordered by communicative need.
Q: What does the Maastricht Treaty example reveal about equivalence?
A: EU law requires high formal equivalence (all versions equally valid). Yet the English "creating an ever closer union" (ongoing process) and the French "une union sans cesse plus étroite" (goal achieved) conflict — showing formal equivalence has limits even in tightly controlled legal translation.
