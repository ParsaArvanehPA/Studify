The Notion of 'Problem' in Translation Studies

Study Guide · Gideon Toury, Descriptive Translation Studies – and beyond, Ch. 2 + Excursus A
Three kinds of "problem" and the significance of pseudotranslations

1. The Central Argument

Translation Studies rarely reflects on its own concepts. Toury isolates "translation problem" and shows it's not one concept — it's three different terms (problem1, problem2, problem3), each belonging to a different context of expert discourse. The chapter also includes a major discussion of pseudotranslations (fictitious translations) and why they matter.
The three problems (prospective/retrospective/processual) and their attributes are the core. The pseudotranslation excursus is frequently tested — know the Papa Hamlet case.

2. The Three Notions of 'Problem'

Problem1 — SOURCE-oriented, PROSPECTIVE, posited. It's about translatability, not actual translation. Utopian, abstract, potential. The "translator" is a theoretical persona with mythical capabilities (full mastery of languages, unlimited memory). Never actually "solved." Levy compared it to a "game with complete information."
Problem2 — TARGET-oriented, RETROSPECTIVE, reconstructed from actual translations. It's a concrete translation act that has already happened. Identified by mapping existing TTs onto their assumed sources via coupled pairs of replacing + replaced segments. The "solution" is whatever is there in the text.
Problem3 — PROCESS-oriented, PROCESSUAL, reconstructed step by step as the act unfolds. A dynamic notion that can change during the act. Requires more than the end-product — interim replacements, think-aloud protocols, keystroke logs (Translog). Allows for interim solutions that problem2 doesn't capture.
Exclusive domains: Translatability and solvability belong only to problem1. Interim solutions belong only to problem3. Problem1 is the most common in the literature (because the first, idealized discourse dominated historically) — which is why many scholars doubt the "solvability" of translation problems. Their "solutions" are really solutions to problem2 or problem3.

3. Excursus A: Pseudotranslations

Pseudotranslation (fictitious translation): A text COMPOSED as if it were a translation but with NO genuine source text. Activates the Source-Text Postulate — the assumption that every translation must have a source — which here proves impossible to locate.
From the host culture's perspective, pseudotranslations are "on a par with genuine translations." They're highly revealing for understanding a culture's view of translation:
  • Safe way to introduce novelties: Deviations in "translated" texts meet greater tolerance. E.g., The Book of Mormon (1830) — presented as a translation from "Reformed Egyptian" — birthed a new Church. Deliberately overused Biblical quotations (25,000 words) and "and it came to pass" (20% of verses vs. 1.45% in the KJV Bible).
  • Individual reinvention: Horace Walpole's The Castle of Otranto — "translated by William Marshal, Gent from the Italian." Karen Blixen's The Angelic Avengers — "by Pierre Andrézel, translated into Danish by Clara Svendsen" (wartime disguise).
  • Importing prestige: Russian "Gothic novels" attributed to Ann Radcliffe to bring English prestige into Russian literature. Hebrew westerns and romances in 1960s Israel "from the English."
  • Culture planning from above: The case of Dzhambul Dzabayev (Shostakovich's memoirs) — an elderly Kazakh singer whose supposed poems were written by a "brigade of Russian poetasters" who didn't know Kazakh. The "translations" existed; the originals never did. Continues until Dzhambul's death.

The Papa Hamlet Case (1889) — Exam Gold

Arno Holz and Johannes Schlaf published Papa Hamlet as a translation from Norwegian by "Bjarne Peter Holmsen." It was reviewed everywhere as a genuine translation — reviewers praised the translator's work, discussed the author's biography, even analyzed his portrait. Months later, it was revealed to be original German writing. The two authors had used the disguise to introduce Scandinavian naturalist innovations into German literature, which was resistant to new trends. The pseudotranslation succeeded in its goal — it's regarded as a forerunner of German Naturalism.

Exam Q&A

Q: Distinguish problem1, problem2, and problem3.
A: Problem1 = source-oriented, prospective, about translatability, never solved. Problem2 = target-oriented, retrospective, reconstructed from coupled pairs in actual translations. Problem3 = process-oriented, processual, allows interim solutions revealed by think-aloud protocols or keystroke logs.
Q: What is a pseudotranslation and why does it matter?
A: A text composed as a translation but with no real source. It activates the Source-Text Postulate. Pseudotranslations matter because they reveal how a culture views translation, provide a safe way to introduce innovations, and can be used for deliberate culture planning.
Q: What does the Papa Hamlet case show?
A: Holz & Schlaf used a Norwegian pseudotranslation to introduce Scandinavian naturalist innovations into Germany. It was accepted as genuine by critics and helped launch German Naturalism — proving pseudotranslations can be effective vehicles for literary change.
