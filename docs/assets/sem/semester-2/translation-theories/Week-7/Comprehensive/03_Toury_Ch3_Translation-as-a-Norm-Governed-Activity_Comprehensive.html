Translation as a Norm-Governed Activity

Study Guide · Gideon Toury, Descriptive Translation Studies – and beyond, Ch. 3
Norms between rules and idiosyncrasies, adequacy vs. acceptability, and competing norms

1. The Central Claim

Translation is a culturally-determined, norm-governed activity. Because it's inherently variable across and within cultures, its regularities can only be explained by reconstructing the norms that govern it. Norms mediate between abstract competence and actual performance — they're the central concept for a descriptive-explanatory account of translation.
The key concepts: norms (vs. conventions), the graded continuum (rules ↔ norms ↔ idiosyncrasies), adequacy vs. acceptability as the two overarching values, competing norms (mainstream/remnant/avant-garde), and "family resemblance."

2. From Conventions to Norms

Conventions: The outcome of a group's "sociability" (Davis) — shared agreements about acceptable actions that create order and predictability. But conventions are VAGUE — not binding enough to guide actual behaviour.
Norms: The translation of general VALUES (right/wrong, adequate/inadequate) into performance "instructions" for concrete situations. They specify what is PRESCRIBED, FORBIDDEN, TOLERATED, and PERMITTED. Unlike conventions, norms involve SANCTIONS (positive or negative).

3. The Graded Continuum

Constraints on behaviour form a scale: objective RULES ↔ (central) NORMS ↔ subjective IDIOSYNCRASIES. Norms are intersubjective and occupy nearly the whole continuum. Rules = more objective norms; idiosyncrasies = more subjective norms. The borderlines are diffuse and shift over time — whims can become norms, norms can become rules, and all can move up and down the scale.

4. Norms as Explanatory Hypotheses

Norms are NOT directly observable. What you observe are regularities of behaviour in recurrent situations. The norms themselves must be reconstructed from these regularities as explanatory hypotheses. The researcher's route mirrors the translator's: looking back from the end-product to what caused it. The correlation between norms and strategies is not 1:1 — one strategy can be fed by several norms, or one norm can underlie several strategies.
Two sources for extracting norms: (1) the products — translated texts and their regularities; (2) normative statements — prefaces, reviews, criticism — but these are "slanted" and must be handled with circumspection.

5. The Two Poles: Adequacy vs. Acceptability

Following Wittgenstein's "family resemblance" concept, there's no single feature all translations share. Translation involves two interwoven principles:
Adequacy: Subscription to the norms of the SOURCE text/language/culture — producing a representation of the existing source text.
Acceptability: Subscription to the norms of the TARGET culture/language — producing a text designed to fill a certain position in the host culture.
The normal state is a degree of INCOMPATIBILITY between the two. Getting closer to one means distancing from the other. Every translation is a BLEND — an ad hoc compromise reflecting the influence of the norms. No translation is ever 100% adequate or 100% acceptable. (Note: Toury's "adequacy" — source-norm adherence — is nearly the opposite of Reiss's use of the same term.)

6. Competing Norms and Change

At any moment in a culture, there are three competing norm types:
  • Norms dominating the centre (mainstream, trendy)
  • Remnants of previous mainstream norms (marginal, "dated," "old-fashioned")
  • Rudiments of a possible new set ("progressive," "avant-garde")
Who drives change? Novice translators tend to play safe with "respectable" (often dated) norms. Large-scale changes are usually made by experienced, prestigious translators who can afford to deviate — and a new norm exists only once others follow. Age isn't the determining factor; status and experience matter more.

7. Translation vs. Related Activities

Norms differ across three closely related activities: Translation (norms actually mobilized), Translation assessment/criticism (constructing arguments about existing translations), and Translator training (often imports "respectable" concepts that clash with real-world norms — causing novices to "unlearn" on entering the profession). The training situation is this "trickiest pair of all."

Exam Q&A

Q: What is a norm and how does it differ from a convention?
A: A norm translates general values into performance "instructions" and involves sanctions. A convention is vaguer and non-binding.
Q: Why are norms called "explanatory hypotheses"?
A: Norms aren't directly observable. Researchers observe regularities of behaviour and reconstruct the underlying norms as explanatory hypotheses.
Q: Explain the adequacy vs. acceptability distinction.
A: Adequacy = adherence to SOURCE norms. Acceptability = adherence to TARGET culture norms. Every translation is a blend/compromise of the two, never wholly one or the other.
Q: Describe the three types of competing norms at any time.
A: Central/mainstream (trendy), remnants of previous mainstream (dated), and rudiments of a new set (avant-garde). Novices tend to follow safe/dated norms; prestigious translators drive change.
