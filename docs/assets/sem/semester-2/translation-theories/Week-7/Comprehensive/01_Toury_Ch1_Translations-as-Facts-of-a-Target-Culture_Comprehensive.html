Translations as Facts of a 'Target' Culture

Study Guide · Gideon Toury, Descriptive Translation Studies – and beyond, Ch. 1
Target-orientedness, the "assumed translation," and the three postulates

1. The Central Claim

Translations are facts of the culture that hosts them — not the source culture. Their position, function, and form are determined first and foremost by considerations originating in the target culture. This is the axiom of Descriptive Translation Studies (DTS). It leads to the key working concept: the "assumed translation" — anything presented or regarded as a translation, on any grounds.
Know: target-orientedness, the difference between Toury's descriptive goal and Skopos theory's applied goal, the three postulates of "assumed translation," and the discovery vs. justification procedures.

2. Toury vs. Skopostheorie: Both Target-Oriented, Different Goals

Toury's programme and Vermeer's Skopostheorie emerged around the same time (Toury 1977; Vermeer 1978) with a shared target orientation. But their goals differ: Skopos theory is APPLIED — it aims to improve translation practice. Toury's programme is DESCRIPTIVE-EXPLANATORY — it aims to give exhaustive accounts of whatever is regarded as translational, aiming at generalizations about translational behaviour. Nord's reintroduction of "loyalty" as an a priori moral principle risks reopening the gap.

3. Translations as Cultural Facts

  • When a text is offered as a translation, it's "readily accepted bona fide as one" — which is why pseudotranslations can pass as genuine.
  • Cultures translate to fill gaps — so translation is "as good as initiated by the target culture." The initial state is one of deficiency in the recipient culture.
  • Translations tend to deviate from target-culture patterns (because of the need to retain some ST features), so they often constitute special systems, even "genres," of their own.
  • Toury REJECTS the notion of translation hovering in an autonomous "interculture" (Pym's term). A translation is always a fact of a target system.

4. The German Train Example — Why You Can't Assume Language = Culture

One of four versions of a warning on German trains reads like English but fits NO English-speaking culture's norms. Conclusion: it's situated within German culture — in a specific sector where English is assigned a role. Never assume language equals culture.

5. "Assumed Translation" and Its Three Postulates

Toury avoids restrictive, essentialist definitions of translation (they cause circular reasoning). Instead, he works with "assumed translation": all utterances in a target culture that are presented or regarded as translations. This expands the range of objects and stays operable even when no source text can be found.
Source-Text Postulate: The assumption that another text existed in another culture/language, with chronological/logical priority, which served as a point of departure. Crucially, it's the ASSUMPTION that matters — not whether the source can actually be located.
Transfer Postulate: The assumption that the process involved a transfer of certain features from the assumed source text to the target text.
Relationship Postulate: The assumption that there are tangible relationships tying the translation to its assumed original, based on shared/transferred features.

6. Discovery vs. Justification Procedures

Study starts with the OBSERVABLES — the translated texts. Then target segments are mapped onto assumed source segments, establishing "coupled pairs" of replacing + replaced segments. From these, equivalence (as the norm for that pair) is established. JUSTIFICATION procedures mirror DISCOVERY procedures; the progression is HELICAL, not linear.
Toury redefines equivalence: NOT prescriptive. He takes equivalence as a GIVEN (the TT is "equivalent" to its ST) and studies the web of relations between them.

Exam Q&A

Q: What does Toury mean by "translations are facts of the target culture"?
A: A translation's position, function, and systemic status are determined by considerations originating in the host (target) culture — not the source. This is the target-oriented axiom of DTS.
Q: How does Toury's programme differ from Skopostheorie, though both are target-oriented?
A: Skopos theory is applied (aims to improve practice). Toury's is descriptive-explanatory (aims to give exhaustive accounts and generalize about translational behaviour).
Q: What are the three postulates of "assumed translation"?
A: Source-Text Postulate (a prior text existed), Transfer Postulate (features were transferred from it), Relationship Postulate (tangible relationships tie the TT to its assumed original).
Q: How does Toury redefine equivalence?
A: Not prescriptively. He takes it as given that a TT is "equivalent" to its ST and studies the web of relations. Equivalence is the translation relationship that constitutes the norm for a given pair.
