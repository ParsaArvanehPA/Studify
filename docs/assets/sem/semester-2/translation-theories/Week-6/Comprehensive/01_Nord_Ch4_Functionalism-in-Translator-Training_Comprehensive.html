Functionalism in Translator Training

Study Guide · Christiane Nord, Translating as a Purposeful Activity, Ch. 4
Text functions, documentary vs. instrumental typology, the translation brief, problems, errors, and translation units

1. What This Chapter Is About

This is the most PRACTICAL chapter in Nord's book. It gives you a complete toolkit for actually DOING functionalist translation: four text functions, three typologies of translation, the Heidelberg brochure as a worked example, a four-part problem classification, the top-down translation process, a four-part error hierarchy, and the redefinition of translation units as "vertical" functional units.
The documentary vs. instrumental distinction is the biggest item. Then: the four problem types and the top-down hierarchy. The Heidelberg example is useful for showing how it all works in practice.

2. Four Text Functions (Bühler + Jakobson)

Referential: Reference to objects/phenomena. Sub-functions: informative, metalinguistic, directive, didactic. Example: "The waters of Maine" — incomprehensible if you don't know they're ice-cold.
Expressive: The sender's attitude toward objects/phenomena. Sub-functions: emotive, evaluative, ironic. Value-system dependent — comparing eyes to a cow's = admiration in India, insult in Germany.
Appellative: Directed at the receiver's disposition to act. Imperatives, rhetorical questions, superlatives. "Like a dart that must hit the board" — fails if the receiver can't cooperate.
Phatic: Establishing/maintaining contact. Small talk, opening proverbs. Relies on CONVENTIONALITY — what's conventional in one culture may be odd in another.

3. Documentary vs. Instrumental Translation (Nord 1989)

Documentary: Produces a DOCUMENT of a source-culture interaction. The TT is "a text about a text" — metatextual function. Forms: interlinear (SL system), literal/grammar (SL form, news quotations), philological/learned (ST form+content with footnotes, ancient texts), exoticizing/foreignizing (keeps source setting — changes appellative into informative).
Instrumental: Produces an INSTRUMENT for a new interaction modelled on the ST. Readers shouldn't notice it's a translation. Forms: equifunctional (same function, e.g. instructions), heterofunctional (different function, e.g. Gulliver's Travels for children), homologous (same literary status, e.g. poetry by poet).
The Garcí­a Márquez example: Colombian readers get an appellative message from Macondo (they can compare it to their own world); European readers receive information about an exotic world — the function changes from appellative to informative automatically.

4. Norms and Conventions

  • Genre conventions: Standardized forms for text types — English recipes = imperative; German = infinitive. Culture-specific.
  • General style conventions: E.g. relative clauses far more frequent in English/Spanish than German. Analysed by form, frequency, distribution.
  • Non-verbal behaviour conventions: Gestures, emblems (finger-ring sign = OK in US, money in Japan, sexual insult in Venezuela). How paraverbal behaviour is verbalized in literature varies dramatically across translations of Alice in Wonderland.
  • Translation conventions: Constitutive (what counts as translation vs. adaptation) vs. regulative (how to handle proper names: "William Shakespeare" → "Guillermo Shakespeare" in Spanish tradition).

5. The Heidelberg Brochure — A Full Worked Example

University of Heidelberg's 600th anniversary brochure. Brief: referential + appellative functions; addressees = visitors; time = one year; medium = brochure with photos. From the brief: formal register, culture-specific knowledge adjustment, layout constraints, anniversary info priority. Comparison: German source has higher appellative weight (potential sponsors, students). Target: informative over appellative → heterofunctional instrumental translation. Concrete choices: "Ruperto Carola" → "University of Heidelberg" in English (receiver-oriented) but kept in French/Spanish. Slogan: "Aus Tradition in die Zukunft" → English "From Tradition into the Future" (literal) vs. French "Tradition et modernisme" (complete restructuring for target-culture slogan conventions).

6. Translation Problems: Four Categories

Pragmatic: From situational differences (sender, receiver, medium, time, motive, function). Present in EVERY task. Most important in training. Cultural: From differences in norms/conventions between cultures. Linguistic: Structural differences in vocabulary, syntax, suprasegmentals (false friends, one-to-many equivalences, German compounds). Text-specific: Bound to one ST — figures of speech, neologisms, puns. Require creative solutions.

7. Top-Down vs. Bottom-Up

Bottom-up (traditional, problematic): Starts from linguistic surface structures → conventions → pragmatics. Students stay too close to ST. Leads to interference, intuitive (unexplainable) decisions. The Bremen hotel example: a translator used "As you make your bed so you must lie on it" to welcome guests — absurd.
Top-down (functional, recommended): Starts from pragmatics (intended function) → translation type (documentary vs. instrumental) → conventions → linguistic structures if still needed. Produces REASONED, explainable decisions.

8. Translation Errors: Functional Definition

Anything that obstructs the purpose = a translation error. Inadequacy is not inherent in an expression — it's assigned from the evaluator's perspective based on the brief. Four categories matching the problem types.
Pragmatic errors — MOST SERIOUS: Receivers don't realize they're getting wrong info. Can't be detected by TT alone. Example: Spanish Guía de la Universidad = a non-existent book in Heidelberg (non-functional) vs. French functional l'annuaire de l'université.
Cultural errors: Inadequate reproduction/adaptation of conventions.
Linguistic errors: Due to deficient SL/TL competence.
Text-specific errors: Related to text-specific problems.

9. Vertical Translation Units

Traditional translation units are "horizontal" — words, phrases, sentences. Nord proposes vertical units: functional units that bring together ALL linguistic and non-linguistic elements at various levels (title, adjectives, metacommentary, gestures) that serve the same communicative function. This means untranslatability ceases to be a nightmare — an untranslatable rhetorical figure can be rendered by another device serving the SAME purpose.

10. Five Basic Principles for Training

  • Translating without clear instructions = swimming without water. Always give students a brief.
  • Some theoretical knowledge should come before first practical exercises.
  • The most important tool = the translator's own native language.
  • To understand another culture, you must first know your own.
  • A wrong tense is less risky than using the right tense at the wrong time (cultural convention errors matter more than linguistic ones).

Exam Q&A

Q: Explain documentary vs. instrumental translation.
A: Documentary = a "document" of the source interaction (interlinear/literal/philological/exoticizing) — metatextual function. Instrumental = an "instrument" for a new interaction (equifunctional/heterofunctional/homologous) — readers don't notice it's a translation.
Q: List the four text functions.
A: Referential, expressive, appellative (Bühler) + phatic (Jakobson).
Q: What should a translation brief contain?
A: Intended function(s), addressee(s), time+place of reception, medium, motive. It specifies WHAT not HOW.
Q: Distinguish bottom-up from top-down translation processes.
A: Bottom-up = words → conventions → pragmatics (problematic). Top-down = pragmatics → conventions → language (produces reasoned decisions).
Q: What is a "vertical" translation unit?
A: A functional unit that brings together all elements at various linguistic/non-linguistic levels that serve the same communicative function — not bound to any single rank.
