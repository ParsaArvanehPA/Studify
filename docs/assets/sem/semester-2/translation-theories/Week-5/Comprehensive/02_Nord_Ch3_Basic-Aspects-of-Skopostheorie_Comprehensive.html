Basic Aspects of Skopostheorie

Study Guide · Christiane Nord, Translating as a Purposeful Activity, Ch. 3
The three rules, the translation brief, culture, and Reiss's text typology

1. What This Chapter Is Actually About

The big picture: This is the core theoretical chapter of Nord's book. It unpacks Vermeer's Skopostheorie — the Skopos rule, coherence, fidelity, and their hierarchy. Then it goes into the translation brief (who decides the Skopos), the concept of culture and culturemes, and Reiss's distinction between adequacy and equivalence plus her three text types.
The three rules and their hierarchy (Skopos > coherence > fidelity) are the single most important thing in this chapter. Then the translation brief, adequacy vs. equivalence, and the three text types.

2. The Three Rules of Skopostheorie

Rule 1: The Skopos Rule

The top-ranking rule: A translational action is determined by its Skopos (purpose). "The end justifies the means." This dissolves the old dilemmas: the Skopos may require a free translation OR a faithful one, depending on what the translation is FOR. It does NOT mean a good translation must always adapt to target-culture expectations — sometimes relative literalism is exactly what's needed (e.g., a marriage certificate, legal texts for comparison).

Rule 2: The Coherence Rule (Intratextual Coherence)

Intratextual coherence: The target text must make sense in the communicative situation and culture in which it is received. It must be "coherent with" — that is, "part of" — the receiver's situation. If the receiver can't understand it or it doesn't fit their situation, the translation fails.

Rule 3: The Fidelity Rule (Intertextual Coherence)

Intertextual coherence: There should be a relationship between the target text and the source text. Its form depends on the translator's interpretation of the ST and on the translation Skopos. One possible kind: maximally faithful imitation.

The Hierarchy

Fidelity (intertextual coherence) is subordinate to coherence (intratextual coherence), and BOTH are subordinate to the Skopos rule. If the Skopos requires a change of function, the standard is no longer fidelity but adequacy to the Skopos. A text is an "offer of information"; a translation is an "offer of information about an offer of information" — so the process is irreversible.

3. The Translation Brief

Translation is normally done "by assignment." A client needs a text for a particular purpose and acts as the initiator. The translation brief (Übersetzungsauftrag) specifies what kind of translation is needed — ideally detailing addressees, time, place, occasion, medium, and intended function. The brief specifies what is needed, NOT how to translate — strategy and type decisions are the translator's expert responsibility.

The term "brief" (from Fraser 1995) captures the idea well: it compares the translator to a barrister who receives basic information and instructions but is then free (as the responsible expert) to carry them out as they see fit. Often the client doesn't give an explicit brief; experienced translators can infer the Skopos from the situation itself — a "conventional assignment."

4. Culture and Culture-Specificity

Culture (Vermeer, after Göhring): Whatever one has to know, master, or feel in order to behave according to a community's expectations (or to deviate from them). A complex system of norms and conventions.
Cultureme: A social phenomenon of culture X that is found to be specific to X when compared with a corresponding phenomenon in culture Y (e.g., "having coffee" in England vs. Spain vs. Germany — different forms, different functions).
Translating MEANS comparing cultures. And there's NO neutral standpoint for comparison — you always perceive the other culture through your own cultural touchstones.

5. Adequacy vs. Equivalence

Adequacy (Reiss's sense): A DYNAMIC, process-oriented quality — the target text is "adequate to" the requirements of the translation brief. It's the goal-oriented selection of signs appropriate for the communicative purpose. This is the superordinate concept.
Equivalence: A STATIC, result-oriented concept — a relationship of "equal communicative value" between two texts. In Skopostheorie, equivalence = adequacy to a Skopos that requires the TT to serve the same function as the ST ("functional equivalence").

Important: Reiss's "adequacy" is almost the OPPOSITE of Toury's use of the term (where adequacy = adherence to source norms). For Reiss, adequacy is about meeting the brief, not the source.

6. Reiss's Three Text Types

Based on Bühler's organon model of language functions:

  • Informative texts — main function is to inform about objects/phenomena. The translator should give a correct and complete representation of the content, guided by TL norms.
  • Expressive texts — the aesthetic component complements or even overrules the informative. The translator should produce an analogous stylistic effect, guided by the ST's choices (e.g., preserving metaphor, rhythm).
  • Operative texts — both content and form are subordinate to the intended extralinguistic effect. The translator should aim to bring about the same reaction, even if that means changing content or form.

In Skopostheorie, this typology applies to the special case of functional invariance between ST and TT. Text types and text genres are different: a text type (informative/expressive/operative) is defined by dominant function; a text genre (Textsorte) is defined by conventional features (e.g., a recipe, a business letter). One genre can span types: a love letter is expressive, a business letter informative, a request letter operative.

Exam Q&A

Q: State the Skopos rule and what it resolves.
A: A translational action is determined by its Skopos (purpose) — "the end justifies the means." It resolves the old free vs. faithful dilemma: the required approach depends entirely on the translation's purpose.
Q: Give the three rules of Skopostheorie and their hierarchy.
A: Skopos rule (purpose governs) > Coherence rule (intratextual coherence — the TT must make sense to its receivers) > Fidelity rule (intertextual coherence with the ST). Fidelity is subordinate to coherence, and both to the Skopos.
Q: What is a translation brief, and what does it specify?
A: The client's specification of the translation purpose — addressees, time, place, medium, intended function. It specifies what is needed but not how — strategy decisions are the translator's expert responsibility.
Q: Distinguish adequacy from equivalence in Skopostheorie.
A: Adequacy is dynamic and process-oriented — the TT being "adequate to" the brief. It's the superordinate concept. Equivalence is static and result-oriented — equal communicative value. In Skopostheorie, equivalence is reduced to adequacy for a Skopos that requires the same function ("functional equivalence").
Q: Name Reiss's three text types and how each is translated when functional invariance is the goal.
A: Informative (inform — render content fully by TL norms). Expressive (aesthetic — produce analogous stylistic effect). Operative (persuade — bring about the same reaction, changing content/form if needed).

What to Absolutely Know

  • Skopos rule: purpose governs — "the end justifies the means"
  • Three rules hierarchy: Skopos > coherence > fidelity
  • Translation brief: specifies WHAT (purpose), not HOW
  • ST = an "offer of information"; the process is irreversible
  • Adequacy (to the brief) is the superordinate concept
  • Equivalence in Skopostheorie = functional equivalence only
  • Culture = norms/conventions; culturemes; no neutral standpoint
  • Reiss's 3 text types: informative / expressive / operative
  • Types ≠ genres (types = function; genres = conventional features)
    • 🧠 Purpose rules everything. Learn the hierarchy Skopos > intratextual coherence > fidelity, the translation brief (what not how), adequacy (to the brief) over equivalence, and Reiss's three text types.
