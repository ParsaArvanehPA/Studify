Main Issues of Translation Studies

Study Guide · Jeremy Munday, Introducing Translation Studies (2016), Ch. 1
Defining translation, the birth of a discipline, and the Holmes/Toury map

1. What This Chapter Is Actually About

Here's the big picture: Translation has been happening for thousands of years — since ancient Babylon, the Rosetta Stone, you name it. But studying translation as an actual academic discipline only started in the second half of the 20th century. That's wild when you think about it. This chapter gives you the lay of the land: what translation IS, how the field was born, and how scholars have mapped it out.
The three things you absolutely must know from this chapter: (1) the three meanings of the word "translation," (2) Jakobson's three categories of translation, and (3) every branch of the Holmes/Toury map. These are the exam gold.

2. So What IS Translation, Anyway?

The English word "translation" actually means three different things. You need to know which one you're talking about:

Translation (3 senses): (1) the general subject or phenomenon ("I studied translation at university") — (2) the product, the actual text that got translated ("have you read the Arabic translation of the report?") — (3) the process of doing it, also called translating ("the translation service")

Then there's the basic model: you have a Source Text (ST) in a Source Language (SL), which gets turned into a Target Text (TT) in a Target Language (TL). Simple, right? Well, not really — because what if there's no single "source" text? Think about EU law where all language versions are equally valid, or a website that's constantly being updated. The neat ST→TT model gets messy fast.

Halverson (1999): Thinks we should treat translation as a prototype concept — there's a core meaning, and then around it are fuzzier forms like adaptation, version, transcreation, localization. They're all translations, just not the purest example of one.

Jakobson's Three Categories — EXAM ESSENTIAL

Roman Jakobson (1959) gave us three types of translation, and this is one of those things that shows up on every exam ever:

  • Intralingual translation ("rewording") — same language, different words. When you summarize a chapter, or explain a complex idea in simpler terms. Also when you see "in other words" in a text.
  • Interlingual translation ("translation proper") — between two different languages. This is what most people mean by "translation." Russian syr → English "cheese" (even though syr actually excludes cottage cheese, so it's not a perfect match).
  • Intersemiotic translation ("transmutation") — from verbal signs to non-verbal sign systems. Novel → movie. Book → ballet. Poem → painting. This is where it gets creative.

Underneath all three is semiotics — the study of signs and sign systems. Language is just one kind of sign system, and translation isn't limited to it.

Also worth knowing: Different cultures have totally different metaphors for translation. Bengali uses rupantar ("change of form"), Hindi uses anuvad ("saying after" or "following"), Arabic uses tarjama ("biography"), and Chinese uses fan yi ("turning over"). These aren't just different words — they imply different ideas about what translation even IS.

3. The Birth of Translation Studies

James S. Holmes is the guy who gave us the name. His 1972 paper "The Name and Nature of Translation Studies" (not widely available until 1988) is considered the founding statement of the discipline. Gentzler says it's "generally accepted as the founding statement for the field," and Snell-Hornby agrees. Holmes wanted to create communication channels between scholars working on translation from different backgrounds — because back then, translation research was scattered across linguistics, comparative literature, language teaching, etc.

Before Holmes: Translation's Sad History

So why did it take so long for translation to become a real discipline? Well, for centuries translation was treated as a second-class activity:

  • Grammar-translation method (18th c. → 1960s): Language learning through rote translation of boring, decontextualized sentences. This made people think translation was just a mechanical exercise, not a serious field.
  • Communicative approach (1960s-70s): Reacted against grammar-translation by basically abandoning translation in language teaching entirely.
  • Translation workshops (1960s USA): Started at Iowa and Princeton — these were like creative writing workshops but for translators. A step forward, but still seen as a minor activity.
  • Comparative literature: Needed translations to compare works across cultures, but didn't really study the TRANSLATION itself — just used it as a tool.
  • Contrastive linguistics: Used translated examples to compare languages, but again, translation wasn't the focus.

By the 1950s-60s, a more systematic linguistic approach started emerging — Fedorov (1953), Vinay & Darbelnet (1958), Mounin (1963), and most importantly Nida (1964) with his "Toward a Science of Translating." The Germans even had a word for it: Übersetzungswissenschaft (translation science).

4. The Holmes/Toury Map — THE THING YOU MUST DRAW FROM MEMORY

Holmes drew a map of the whole discipline. It was later presented by Gideon Toury (1995), so it's often called the Holmes/Toury map. It splits translation studies into two main branches: PURE and APPLIED.

Pure Branch → Theoretical

  • General theory — tries to explain ALL types of translation (e.g., Toury's "laws")
  • Partial theories — restricted by:
    • Medium (machine vs. human, written vs. spoken/consecutive vs. simultaneous)
    • Area (specific language pairs or cultures)
    • Rank (word, sentence, or text-level analysis)
    • Text-type (literary, technical, religious, etc.)
    • Time (translation in specific periods)
    • Problem (e.g., equivalence, universals)

Pure Branch → Descriptive (DTS)

  • Product-oriented — studying existing translations (single ST-TT pairs, or comparing multiple TTs of the same ST; can be diachronic or synchronic)
  • Function-oriented — studying the ROLE of translations in the target culture ("socio-translation studies," now called sociology/historiography of translation). Which texts were translated when and where? What influence did they have?
  • Process-oriented — studying what goes on in the translator's MIND. Think-aloud protocols, eye-tracking, keystroke logging.

Applied Branch

  • Translator training — teaching methods, testing, curriculum design
  • Translation aids — dictionaries, grammars, CAT tools
  • Translation criticism — evaluating translations, reviewing published works
  • Translation policy — advising on translation's role in society
The key insight: DTS feeds back into theory. The three branches interact — they're not silos. Also: Holmes spent way more time on the "pure" side because that was his research interest. The applied side was less developed.

5. Since Holmes: Where the Field Went

After Holmes, things exploded. Here's what happened:

  • Skopos theory (Reiss & Vermeer, 1970s-80s Germany) — purpose of the translation determines the method
  • Discourse analysis (Hallidayan approach; Baker, Hatim & Mason, 1990s) — language as communication in social context
  • Polysystem theory & Descriptive approach (Even-Zohar, Toury, Tel Aviv) — translation as part of a literary system competing with non-translation
  • "Manipulation School" (Hermans 1985) — translation as manipulation of the original
  • The "Cultural Turn" (Bassnett & Lefevere 1990) — translation studies meets cultural studies
  • Postcolonial & Gender approaches — Niranjana, Spivak, Simon
  • Venuti's visibility — calling for translators to be seen, not invisible
  • Technology — machine translation, corpus-based studies, localization, audiovisual

6. The van Doorslaer Map

Because the field got so big, van Doorslaer (2007) made new maps. Key distinctions:

  • "Translation" (the act) — subdivided by lingual mode (interlingual/intralingual), media (print/audiovisual/electronic), mode (covert/overt, direct/indirect, pseudo/self/retranslation), and field (political, journalistic, technical, literary, religious)
  • "Translation Studies" (the academic field) — approaches, theories, research methods, applied TS
  • Plus a "basic transfer map" that distinguishes a strategy (overall orientation of the whole text) from a procedure (a specific technique at a point in the text, like borrowing or calque)

7. Interdiscipline or Just Scattered?

Translation studies is interdisciplinary — it lives in the gaps between other fields. McCarty calls it "the Phoenician trader among the settled nations." It has primary links to linguistics, modern languages, comparative literature, cultural studies, philosophy. But Daniel Gile warns that the partnerships are "almost always unbalanced" — the other discipline has more status and money, which could weaken translation studies' autonomy. Is this a strength or a danger?

Exam Q&A · Check Yourself

Q: What are the three different meanings of "translation"?
A: (1) The general subject field ("I studied translation"), (2) the product (the translated text itself), and (3) the process of producing it (translating).
Q: Name and define Jakobson's three translation categories.
A: Intralingual (rewording within the same language, e.g. a summary), Interlingual ("translation proper" between two languages), and Intersemiotic (transmutation between verbal and non-verbal systems, e.g. novel to film).
Q: What did James S. Holmes do, and why is his 1972 paper important?
A: He gave the discipline its name ("translation studies") and drew the first map of the field, dividing it into pure (theoretical + descriptive) and applied branches. It's "generally accepted as the founding statement for the field" (Gentzler).
Q: Walk through the three kinds of Descriptive Translation Studies (DTS).
A: Product-oriented — analysing translations themselves (single pairs or comparative studies; diachronic or synchronic). Function-oriented — the role/context of translations in the target culture ("socio-translation studies"). Process-oriented — what happens in the translator's mind (think-aloud protocols, eye-tracking).
Q: What's the difference between a translation "strategy" and a "procedure" (van Doorslaer)?
A: A strategy is the overall orientation of the whole translation (e.g., choosing to translate literally). A procedure is a specific technique at a given point in the text (e.g., borrowing a word, calque, transposition).
