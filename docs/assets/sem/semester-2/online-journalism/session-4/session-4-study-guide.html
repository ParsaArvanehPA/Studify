Translation Strategies in Global News

Session 4: A Case for Foreignised News Translation | Based on Claire Scammell, Translation Strategies in Global News (2018), Ch. 4 | MA Translation Studies

Session Overview

About this session

This chapter builds the direct case for a foreignised approach. It shows how translation undermines journalism's ideals of accuracy and objectivity in quotation, introduces the concept of "double mediation," examines the Sarkozy case study in full historical detail, and unpacks the three central culture-specific concepts — banlieue, quartier and cité — that anchor the rest of the book's corpus analysis.

1. Translation Undermines Accuracy and Objectivity in Quotation

Translation studies holds it as "universally accepted" that "translations are never fully homologous to the original — always containing shifts, errors, and subjective interpretations" (Gentzler and Tymoczko 2002, p. xvi). A domesticating strategy — rendering foreign information in terms the reader immediately recognises — increases the risk that source-language meaning is lost, using the popular maxim: something will get "lost in translation."

Waugh (1995): a speaker's voice is always "filtered and thereby changed in various ways" — pointing to "the pitfalls that translation brings." Journalists may genuinely aspire to impartiality, but translators are constantly forced to make subjective decisions about what to 'lose' and what to 'gain' in conveying a message across languages and cultures (Holland 2013).

2. Double Mediation of Quotation and Culture

Hernando (1999) — "double mediation" (doble mediación)

"If newswriting is mediation between facts and receivers, this becomes a process of double mediation in the case of translated texts."

The first stage of mediation is common to all newswriting: the journalist decides what information to include, what to leave out, and how to package it. The second stage occurs only when translation is involved. Valdeón (2007) and Károly (2010) describe parallel "two-fold"/"two distinct levels" of mediation — the first intra-lingual, the second inter-lingual (and inter-cultural).

As cultural mediator, the journalist-translator must have the cultural knowledge to interpret a foreign news event, and must judge what source-culture knowledge their audience can be expected to possess, and in what terms information will be understood. Filmer's (2014) interview with Bill Emmott (former Editor-in-Chief, The Economist) confirms this: even without translation, "the journalist is nevertheless an interpreter," but inter-lingual translation "obviously adds another layer."

3. What Sarkozy Said, In Detail: Two Speech Events Collapsed Into One

The Kärcher quote and the racaille quote were four months apart

English-language translations that continue to circulate today combine two separate speech events that occurred four months apart into what looks like a single quotation:

EventDateWhat actually happened
The "Kärcher" quote 19 June 2005 Sarkozy visits the Cité des 4,000 in La Courneuve, Paris banlieue, to meet the parents of an 11-year-old killed in gang crossfire. Filmed telling residents: "Les voyous vont disparaître... on nettoiera la Cité des 4,000" ("the hoodlums will disappear... we will clean up the Cité des 4,000"). Separately, he is heard using the phrase "nettoyer au Kärcher" ("clean out with a Kärcher").
The "racaille" quote 25 October 2005 Sarkozy visits Argenteuil banlieue, and — filmed looking up at a female resident on her balcony — says: "Vous en avez assez, hein? ... Vous en avez assez de cette bande de racaille? Eh bien, on va vous en débarrasser!" ("You've had enough, haven't you? ... You've had enough of this bunch of racaille? Well, we're going to rid you of them!")
How the two events get merged: a 2011 Mail Online report includes both quotes as if from one occasion: "As interior minister six years ago, he was accused of inflaming three weeks of nationwide street violence by saying of urban rioters: 'They are scum who need to be cleaned from the streets with a power hose.'" Since the aim of translation studies here is to clarify what Sarkozy said and when, the chapter deliberately does not offer translations for the two French quotes above — to keep the two events distinct.

The stakes of "racaille": a French AFP report from 30 October 2005 states that racaille was used without quotation marks (i.e. as an established, uncontested description) — by contrast, an English report puts "rabble" in quotation marks and attributes it as Sarkozy's own word for "rebellious youths." The choice to quotation-mark a translated word attributes a specific, contested word choice to the speaker in a way the original French source may not have intended.

4. Three Culture-Specific Concepts: banlieue, quartier, cité

banlieue — hard to translate because it is used differently

English typically renders the residential outskirts of any city as plural "suburbs." French uses banlieue collectively and in the singular for one city's periphery (e.g. "the banlieue of Paris"), and only pluralises when referring to peripheries of multiple cities. Reports commonly mistranslate this by rendering singular banlieue as plural "suburbs" or "banlieues" — even the Guardian's style guide notes: "French for suburbia, not suburb: strictly singular... the French for suburb is faubourg."

Connotation gap: in French media, banlieue carries strong connotations of violence, insecurity and a predominantly ethnic population (Glasze et al. 2012). In English, "suburb" connotes leafy, green, middle-class semi-detached housing. A British reader encountering "riot in the suburbs of Paris" will imagine a wholly different socio-economic reality than a French reader encountering "riot in a banlieue."

quartier and cité

Quartier = an administrative city district/area ("district"/"area" in dictionaries); cité = specifically a housing estate/project ("estate" UK / "project" US). Both closely relate to banlieue and carry the same strong negative connotations when collocated with terms like sensible ("sensitive," from Zones Urbaines Sensibles, France's official at-risk-area classification) or difficile — both commonly rendered in English simply as "problem area(s)."

"suburban youths" — a domesticating translation loses the reference

A Reuters report translates jeunes des cités ("youths of the cités") as "suburban youths." Given the very different connotations of "suburban" in English, the translation falls far short of the cultural context Begag's original phrase carried; space constraints leave the journalist little room to expand, so the culture-specific meaning is simply lost.

5. A Foreignised Approach for Reuters

The book develops the foreignised approach specifically for Reuters — the biggest of the Big Three by size, clients and international presence (74 million monthly readers in 2004; publishing over eight million words daily in six languages). Reuters has also broken from the traditional agency role of supplying only subscribers, providing news directly to the "broader public" via Reuters.com and mobile devices.

The investigation proceeds in two stages: first, examining Reuters journalists' current translation strategies (using the five-report corpus, analysed in Session 6); then developing and presenting a set of proposed updates to the Reuters Handbook of Journalism (Session 7) — the foreignised approach.

✦ Why This Matters for a Translator

This chapter is where the book's argument sharpens from "domestication has costs" into "here is exactly what those costs look like, concretely."

  • The collapsed Kärcher/racaille timeline shows that translation errors aren't only about word choice — attribution errors (putting a quotation mark around a translated word implies the speaker used precisely that word, at precisely that moment) can misrepresent a historical record, not just a nuance.
  • The banlieue/suburb mismatch is a textbook case of "double mediation" doing real damage: it isn't just less precise, it actively substitutes a different socio-economic reality for the reader.
  • "Suburban youths" for jeunes des cités shows how space constraints in news (headlines, leads) make domestication almost the default outcome — foreignising strategies need to be deliberately chosen against this pressure, not assumed to happen naturally.

Why it matters for you: this chapter's three terms — banlieue, quartier, cité — become the exact test cases used in the Reuters corpus analysis in Session 6, so understanding their specific connotation gaps now is essential groundwork for that later, more technical chapter.

Review Questions

Q&A for Exam Preparation

Q1: What is "double mediation" (Hernando 1999), and how does it apply to translated news?
A: All newswriting involves a first stage of mediation between facts and receivers (the journalist decides what to include/exclude and how to package it). When translation is involved, a second stage of mediation occurs — the journalist-translator also mediates between the source language/culture and the target audience, adding an inter-lingual, inter-cultural layer on top of the ordinary journalistic one.
Q2: Explain how the "Kärcher" and "racaille" quotes come from two entirely separate events, and how English-language reporting often merges them.
A: The Kärcher comment was made on 19 June 2005 at the Cité des 4,000 in La Courneuve; the racaille comment was made separately on 25 October 2005 in Argenteuil — four months apart. English reports (e.g. a 2011 Mail Online article) often present them as if from one occasion, attributing both "scum" and "power hose" to a single, undifferentiated quotation — misrepresenting the historical record.
Q3: Why is it inaccurate to render French banlieue as English plural "suburbs"?
A: Grammatically, French uses banlieue in the singular for one city's periphery collectively (only pluralising across multiple cities), while translations frequently and incorrectly render it as plural "suburbs" or "banlieues." Connotatively, "suburb" evokes affluent, leafy outskirts in English, while banlieue connotes poverty, high immigration, and (in French media) violence and insecurity — a substantially different reality.
Q4: Distinguish banlieue, quartier and cité.
A: Banlieue = the residential periphery of a city as a whole. Quartier = an administrative city district or area (translated "district"/"area"). Cité = specifically a housing estate/project within a banlieue (translated "estate" in British English, "project" in American English). All three carry negative connotations when paired with adjectives like sensible or difficile.
Q5: Why does the translation "suburban youths" for jeunes des cités lose important meaning?
A: "Suburban" in English carries connotations (affluent, quiet, safe) very different from the socio-economic reality of a French cité (a housing estate associated with poverty and social tension). Given space constraints, the journalist has little room to expand on the phrase, so the reader is left with a domesticated but misleading impression.
Q6: Why does putting quotation marks around a translated word matter, according to the discussion of the "rabble" example?
A: Quotation marks around a translated word imply the speaker used precisely that word — but the original French source (AFP) reported racaille without quotation marks, as an uncontested description, while an English report quotation-marks "rabble" and attributes it directly to Sarkozy as his chosen word. This misrepresents the degree of controversy and precision genuinely attributable to the speaker.
Q7: Why is Reuters specifically chosen as the news agency for developing the foreignised approach?
A: Reuters is the largest of the Big Three by size, client base and international presence — publishing over eight million words daily in six languages and reaching roughly 74 million people monthly (2004 figures) — and it has moved beyond serving only subscribers to reaching the "broader public" directly via Reuters.com, making its translation practices unusually consequential.
Q8: What two aspects of Reuters' practice does the investigation examine, in what order?
A: First, Reuters journalists' current translation strategies, using the five-report corpus (examined in the next session using Pedersen's taxonomy); second, a set of proposed updates to the Reuters Handbook of Journalism that collectively constitute the foreignised approach (presented later in the book).

Key Points to Review

  • Translation always involves subjective loss/gain — "lost in translation" is not just a cliché but a documented risk of domesticating strategies
  • Double mediation (Hernando 1999): intra-lingual mediation (all news) + inter-lingual/cultural mediation (translated news)
  • The Kärcher quote (19 June 2005, La Courneuve) and racaille quote (25 October 2005, Argenteuil) are separate events often wrongly merged in English reporting
  • banlieue (singular collective, poor/high-immigration) ≠ "suburbs" (plural, affluent) — a grammatical and connotative mismatch
  • quartier (district/area) and cité (housing estate/project) — related, similarly connoted culture-specific terms
  • "Suburban youths" for jeunes des cités — a domesticating translation under space constraints that loses cultural specificity
  • Quotation marks around a translated word can misattribute exact wording to a speaker
  • Reuters chosen as case study for scale, reach, and direct-to-public online presence
