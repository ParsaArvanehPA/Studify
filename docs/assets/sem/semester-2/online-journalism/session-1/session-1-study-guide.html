Translation Strategies in Global News

Session 1: Introduction — The Sarkozy Case and the Domestication Norm | Based on Claire Scammell, Translation Strategies in Global News: What Sarkozy Said in the Suburbs (Palgrave Pivot, 2018), Ch. 1 | MA Translation Studies

Session Overview

About this book and this session

This session covers the introductory chapter of Claire Scammell's Translation Strategies in Global News. The book opens with a real news event — what Nicolas Sarkozy said about the Paris banlieues in 2005 — to show how the translation process shapes both the accuracy of quotation and readers' interpretations of foreign news events. It sets out its central concern with the norm for domesticating news translation strategies, and makes the case for introducing a degree of foreignisation instead, focusing throughout on the translation practices of the Reuters news agency.

1. The Case Study Event: Sarkozy in the Paris Banlieue (November 2005)

What happened

In November 2005, Nicolas Sarkozy — French interior minister and presidential candidate — became the focus of international media attention. Riots in France's banlieues (poor, high-immigration residential areas on the peripheries of major cities) were reported to have been sparked by controversial language Sarkozy used on a visit to a cité (a high-rise housing development) in a Paris banlieue.

Key French terms: banlieue (a poor, high-immigration urban periphery — used only in the singular for one city's periphery), cité (a high-rise housing estate within a banlieue), and quartier (an administrative city district/area) — these three terms are the culture-specific concepts at the heart of the whole book.

2. How the British Press Translated What Sarkozy Said

French journalists, bound by ideals of accuracy and objectivity, could report exactly what Sarkozy had said. The translation process made this impossible for journalists reporting in other languages.

"Racaille" and the "Kärcher"

A typical British-press translation quoted Sarkozy promising to wash out the "scum" [racaille] with a "power hose" [Kärcher] — Kärcher being a pressure-washer brand. English renderings of racaille vary widely across reports: "scum," "rabble," "riff-raff," "yobs" — evidence that no single clear equivalent exists in English.

banlieue → "suburbs": translations tend to situate the speech event in the "suburbs" of Paris. In British English, "suburbs" typically means affluent, desirable outskirts; translating banlieue as "suburbs" therefore communicates a distinctly different reality to a British audience.

These translation choices reflect a broader norm for "domesticating" translation strategies in the news: a domesticating strategy uses only terms the reader will immediately recognise and understand.

3. Domestication vs. Foreignisation (Venuti)

Two opposing global strategies

Strategy Definition
Domestication Uses only terms the reader will immediately recognise and understand; matches the newswriting principle that readers should understand a report quickly, without needing to look beyond the text.
Foreignisation Retains something of the foreignness of the source text — foreign language, concepts or syntax; generally seen as unviable for news translation because it conflicts with readers' need for quick, unmediated understanding.

Lawrence Venuti (2008)

These terms come from Venuti's The Translator's Invisibility, where the translator either "brings the author back home" (domesticating) or "sends the reader abroad" (foreignising) — a framing he adapts from Schleiermacher's 1813 essay. Venuti champions foreignisation as an ethical, anti-imperialist stance in literary translation, but news translation research has largely dismissed it as impractical.

4. The Book's Proposal: A "Foreignised" Approach

Why "foreignised" and not "foreignising"?

The term foreignised, used to describe the approach developed in this book, is a deliberate variation on Venuti's "foreignisation" — to reflect that it is only foreignising in certain defined respects, i.e. foreignising only to a degree, not as an absolute strategy.

Esperança Bielsa and the cosmopolitan argument

Bielsa (2010, 2014, 2016) argues that translation can be a site of cosmopolitan openness in global news — but only through strategies that expose rather than obscure cultural and linguistic difference. This is the ethical foundation the book builds on.

5. The Book's Aim and Research Question

The book investigates the scope for a foreignised approach to translation in the news as an ethical alternative to the current domestication norm. The ethical potential of this approach is found in two places:

  • the key role news translation plays as a tool of intercultural communication;
  • the implications of the translation process for the accuracy of quotation.
Culture-specific concept: a term or phrase in a news report that refers to a reality existing in the originating news locale (the source culture). Banlieue is the book's central example. The study focuses on two key sites of translation in the news: quotation and culture-specific concepts.

6. The Case Study Agency and Corpus: Reuters

The investigation focuses on the translation practices of the global news agencies, since their content reaches wide international audiences via the print and online publications of subscribers to their newswires. Reuters — "the British agency" — is used as the case study.

✦ Why This Matters for a Translator

This session sets up a tension that recurs throughout the book: does fidelity to the foreignness of a source text conflict with the clarity news demands?

  • The dominant newswriting norm is domestication: readers should understand a report immediately, without background knowledge.
  • But full domestication has a cost — as the Sarkozy case shows, translating banlieue as "suburbs" doesn't just simplify, it substitutes a different reality altogether.
  • The book's proposal is a middle path: not full foreignisation (which risks unintelligibility), but foreignisation "to a degree" — retaining select foreign elements at the points where they carry the most meaning.

Why it matters for you: a news translator faces exactly this kind of decision every day — keep a foreign term and explain it, or replace it with the nearest target-language equivalent. Each choice shapes a different picture of reality in the reader's mind; recognising this spectrum is the first step toward deliberate, ethical news translation.

Review Questions

Q&A for Exam Preparation

Q1: Why couldn't English-language reporting on Sarkozy's 2005 comments match the accuracy achieved by French reporting?
A: French journalists could report Sarkozy's exact words. Journalists reporting in other languages had to translate what he said, and translation necessarily involves interpretation and choice (as shown by the several competing English renderings of racaille) — making full quotational accuracy impossible once a language boundary is crossed.
Q2: Define "domestication" in news translation and illustrate it using the Sarkozy case.
A: Domestication is a translation strategy that uses only terms the reader will immediately recognise and understand. Example: translating banlieue (a poor, high-immigration French periphery) as "suburbs" (which in British English connotes affluent, desirable outskirts) — communicating a substantially different reality to the reader.
Q3: What is foreignisation according to Venuti, and why has it traditionally been rejected for news translation?
A: Foreignisation is a strategy that retains something of the foreignness of the source text — foreign language, concepts, or syntax. It has generally been considered unviable for news translation because it conflicts with the need to conform to readers' expectation of quick, unmediated understanding.
Q4: Why does the author use the term "foreignised" rather than Venuti's "foreignising"?
A: To signal that the approach is only foreignising in certain defined respects — foreignising only to a degree, rather than as an absolute strategy — so it can retain some source-culture foreignness while still meeting news's need for clarity and concision.
Q5: What are the two key sites of translation in the news that this book focuses on? Define "culture-specific concept."
A: Quotation and culture-specific concepts. A culture-specific concept is a term or phrase in a news report referring to a reality that exists in the originating news locale (the source culture) — banlieue is the book's central example.
Q6: Why is Reuters chosen as the case study news agency?
A: Reuters is one of the "Big Three" global news agencies, providing news content that reaches a wide international audience through the print and online publications of subscribers to its newswires — making its translation strategies highly consequential for how global readers understand foreign events.
Q7: Where is the ethical potential of the foreignised approach found, according to the introduction?
A: In two places: the key role news translation plays as a tool of intercultural communication (drawing on Bielsa's cosmopolitan argument), and the implications of the translation process for the accuracy of quotation.
Q8: How many Reuters reports make up the book's corpus, and what do they have in common?
A: Five English-language Reuters reports (Crime policies, Immigration, Thugocracy, Security, Dummy), published between 2005 and 2012, all connected in some way to what Sarkozy said or did regarding France's banlieues — used to examine Reuters' current translation strategies before trialling the foreignised alternative.
