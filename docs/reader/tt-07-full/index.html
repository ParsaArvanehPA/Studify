Study Guide · Christiane Nord, Translating as a Purposeful Activity, Ch. 1
How the German functionalist school broke away from equivalence — Reiss, Vermeer, and Holz-Mänttäri

1. What This Chapter Is Actually About

The big picture:

Functionalism didn't appear out of nowhere. It grew out of a growing dissatisfaction with

equivalence-based

approaches among scholars who worked in

translator training

. They noticed something obvious: professional translators often handle situations where equivalence is NOT what's wanted. A British school certificate translated for a German university shouldn't LOOK like a German school certificate. This chapter gives you the history of the "German School" — Reiss, Vermeer's Skopostheorie, Holz-Mänttäri's translational action, and the functionalist methodology that grew out of training contexts.

The key shift: from equivalence to function. Know the four landmarks: Reiss (text typology + functional criticism), Vermeer (Skopos), Holz-Mänttäri (translational action), and the training scholars. Remember: functionalist views existed before the 20th century — Cicero, Jerome, and Luther all said different situations need different approaches.

2. Before Functionalism: The Limits of Equivalence

The linguistic approaches of the 1950s-60s saw translation as a

code-switching operation

. Nida's three-stage model (analysis→transfer→restructuring) was influential, but its

linguistic

side got taken up more than its

functional

side. Why? Because linguistics was the dominant discipline of the era, and early machine translation experiments needed contrastive language descriptions.

Key equivalence-based definitions that dominated the field:

  • Catford (1965): "The replacement of textual material in one language (SL) by equivalent material in another language (TL)."
  • Nida & Taber (1969): "Translating consists in reproducing in the receptor language the closest natural equivalent of the source-language message."
  • Wilss (1977): Translation leads from ST to TT "which is as close an equivalent as possible."
  • Koller (1979): Declared any TT not equivalent to the ST a non-translation — but later allowed "ad-hoc cases of adaptation" for pragmatic equivalence.
The problem: equivalence theorists set up "different or even contradictory standards for different genres." Some praised literalism as optimal (Newmark), others allowed adaptive procedures in specific cases. The criteria were arbitrary. And trainers found that professional translation often has nothing to do with equivalence — e.g. translating a British school certificate for a German university doesn't aim at equivalence at all.

3. Katharina Reiss: Text Typology and the Functional Category

As early as 1971, Reiss introduced a

functional category

into translation criticism — while still staying within equivalence theory. The ideal translation achieves equivalence in "conceptual content, linguistic form AND communicative function" — what she called "integral communicative performance."

But Reiss knew real life is messier:

She recognized exceptions from the equivalence requirement, based on the

translation brief

(

Übersetzungsauftrag

): (1) when the TT has a

different function

from the original (a prose text adapted for the stage, or word-for-word versions for a poet who doesn't know the source language); (2) when the TT addresses a

different audience

(

Gulliver's Travels

for children; ideological editing for religious or commercial reasons).

She called these "transfers" (Übertragungen), not "translations proper." In those cases, the functional perspective takes precedence over normal standards of equivalence. Later, in Reiss & Vermeer (1984), her text typology becomes a "specific theory" within Vermeer's general theory — where functional equivalence is no longer the normal aim.

4. Hans J. Vermeer: Skopostheorie

Vermeer's break with linguistics:

"Linguistics alone won't help us. First, because translating is not merely and not even primarily a linguistic process. Secondly, because linguistics has not yet formulated the right questions to tackle our problems. So let's look somewhere else." (1987)

For Vermeer, translation is a type of

human action

, and all action is

intentional, purposeful behaviour

that takes place in a situation embedded in a culture. This is why he calls his theory

Skopostheorie

—

skopos

is Greek for "purpose."

Key features of this approach:

  • Skopos = the prime principle — the purpose of the overall translational action determines everything.
  • The source text is demoted: For Reiss the ST was "the measure of all things." For Vermeer it's an "offer of information" (Informationsangebot) that is partly or wholly turned into an offer of information for the target audience.
  • The addressee is central: To translate means "to produce a text in a target setting for a target purpose and target addressees in target circumstances." The receiver's culture-specific world-knowledge, expectations, and needs are the main factors determining the Skopos.
Compare the status of the source text: In equivalence theories it's the measure of all things. In Skopostheorie it's just an "offer of information." That's a massive shift.

5. Justa Holz-Mänttäri: Translational Action

Holz-Mänttäri goes even further. She

avoids the word "translation"

altogether. Instead she talks about

"translational action"

(

translatorisches Handeln

) — a complex action designed to achieve a particular purpose. Translators are

experts

who produce

"message transmitters"

(

Botschaftsträger

) to coordinate communicative cooperation across cultural and language barriers.

She analyzes in detail the roles of participants (initiator, translator, user, message receiver) and the situational conditions (time, place, medium). She's particularly concerned with the status of translators in a world characterized by the division of labour — her vocational training concepts emphasize translators as experts.

6. Functionalist Methodology in Translator Training

Right from the start, functionalism had a huge impact on translator training. The key figure are

Hönig & Kussmaul

(their 1982

Strategie der Übersetzung

showed how functional strategies solve translation problems). Their core maxim: the

"necessary degree of precision"

— reproduce just those semantic features relevant to the function of the translation (echoing Grice's conversational maxims).

What all functionalist training scholars share: They focus on language-independent pragmatic and cultural aspects of translation, emphasizing translation competence over language proficiency. Unlike the linguistic theorists, they look at what translators actually DO in professional practice.

Nord's own contribution:

Her "translation-oriented model of text analysis" (1988/1991) analyzes extratextual and intratextual aspects of communication, designed to identify function-relevant elements in both the ST and the prospective TT as defined by the translation brief. By comparing the Skopos with ST functions before translating, translators can locate problems in advance and devise a holistic strategy.

Exam Q&A · Check Yourself

?Self-test · check yourself5 questions
01What dissatisfaction gave rise to functionalism?
A: Dissatisfaction with equivalence-based approaches among translator-training scholars. They found that professional practice includes many cases where equivalence is simply not wanted — e.g., a British school certificate translated for a German university doesn't need to look like a German one.
02How did Reiss introduce a functional category, and what exceptions did she allow?
A: Within an objective approach to translation criticism, she allowed exceptions when the translation brief specifies a different function from the original or a different audience. She called these "transfers" (Übertragungen), not "translations proper."
03Define Skopos and explain the status of the source text in Skopostheorie.
A: Skopos is the purpose of the translation — the prime principle determining everything. The source text is NOT the measure of all things; it's merely an "offer of information" that is partly or wholly re-offered to the target audience.
04What is "translational action" (Holz-Mänttäri)?
A: A complex, purpose-driven action in which translators, as experts, produce "message transmitters" to coordinate communicative cooperation across cultural and language barriers. It covers all forms of intercultural transfer, even those without source or target texts.
05State Hönig & Kussmaul's key maxim for functional translation.
A: The "necessary degree of precision" — reproduce just those semantic features that are relevant in a given context with regard to the function of your translation (in line with Grice's conversational maxims of relevance and quantity).
