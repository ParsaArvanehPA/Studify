Session 8: Conclusion | Based on Claire Scammell, Translation Strategies in Global News (2018), Ch. 8 | MA Translation Studies

Session Overview

About this session

The book's final chapter summarises its findings on current translation practices in global news and the foreignised approach developed as an alternative to the domestication norm. It reflects on the book's contribution as an initial exploration of the scope for foreignising news translation strategies, and points to avenues for further research.

1. What the Case Study Revealed

Analysis of the case study news event — what Sarkozy said in the "suburbs" — highlighted the secondary level of mediation (Session 4's "double mediation") introduced by the translation process when foreign-language quotation and culture-specific concepts are reported in the news. By extension, it highlighted the implications of a domesticating approach that obscures the journalist's intervention and distances the reader from the foreign source language and context.

Through analysis of Reuters reporting, the investigation revealed what domesticating strategies actually look like in practice (Session 6's corpus results: 21 domesticating instances vs. 8 foreignising). The book explores the potential for introducing a degree of foreignisation as a means of maximising translation's potential in the news to facilitate cross-cultural engagement and understanding.

2. Challenging the News Translation Literature

In doing so, the book challenges a conclusion widely held in the news translation literature — that foreignisation is simply not relevant in a newswriting context (Session 3: Bassnett, Bielsa and Bassnett, and others largely dismiss it as unviable). Scammell's contribution is to show that this needn't be an all-or-nothing question.

3. What the Foreignised Approach Does — and Doesn't — Change

Reuters' domesticating approach rests primarily on two core Handbook instructions: to avoid foreign language, and to translate quotations "idiomatically." The foreignised approach (Session 7) does not remove either core instruction — it adds guidance that expands on the exceptions the Handbook already specifies.

An incremental, not radical, shift:

the individual updates do not dramatically alter journalists' day-to-day translation practice. Collectively, though, they represent an overall shift toward a more foreignising approach — built on and informed by the approach to translation the Handbook already calls for (implicitly and explicitly), and by considerations around the accuracy of reporting quotation.

4. The Book's Purpose: A Basis for Dialogue, Not a Final Answer

The foreignised approach points to ways journalists can counter the impact of the translation process on the accuracy and objectivity of reporting, and bring readers closer to the foreign realities they are reporting on. As an initial exploration of the scope for foreignising news translation, it offers a basis for dialogue with journalists on the ethical aims and practicality of shifting away from the domestication norm — not a finished, mandatory system.

5. Avenues for Future Research

  • Collaboration with news agencies: a productive next step for assessing the real-world impact of translation strategies that deviate from the domestication norm.
  • Other English-speaking news organisations: research on national outlets in the UK and other English-speaking countries could clarify what forms/degrees of foreignisation are viable in English-language reporting.
  • Reader-participant research: involving readers directly would add the audience perspective — currently missing — to the debate on translation in global news.
  • Beyond English: the Handbook's guidance is English-language-specific, so the foreignised approach as developed is most directly relevant to English newswriting. Updates 3 and 4 (accuracy) and Update 5 (signalling foreign context) are not language-specific at all, however — and the retention strategy of Updates 1 and 2, while informed by English-language analysis, would need parallel research in other languages to test its viability for journalists writing in and for different audiences.

A widening audience for agency content

The book's focus on the "Big Three" is motivated by the vast global audiences they reach — a dominance that appears to be increasing. In June 2015, Reuters announced it would begin making some content freely available to digital publishers, explicitly aiming to expose "more readers to its award-winning news content" (Reuters press release, 9 June 2015). Combined with the rise of "viral" news sites like BuzzFeed and Upworthy, this has likely vastly increased the number of readers accessing Reuters content online.

6. Closing Argument

By approaching quotation and culture-specific concepts as key sites of translation in the news, the book casts light on precise forms of translation that can be clearly distinguished from intra-lingual editing practices. While translation's involvement in newswriting may extend beyond these two sites, they offer a productive starting point for examining news translation in any context. In an increasingly rapid, widespread and digital news landscape, news translation research can engage in highly relevant debates in media and journalism studies — continuing to draw attention to the integral, often invisible, role of translation in how news crosses the globe.

✦ Why This Matters for a Translator

The conclusion is deliberately modest, and that modesty is itself the takeaway: Scammell isn't arguing that news translation should become foreignising by default, only that the option shouldn't be dismissed outright, as it has been throughout most of the news translation literature.

  • The book's own limitation (English-language, Reuters-specific, no reader-response data) models good scholarly practice: state clearly what your findings do and don't establish, and name the next steps precisely.
  • The Reuters 2015 shift toward free digital distribution — and the rise of virality — is a reminder that these questions aren't academic abstractions: as agency content reaches ever-larger, less-filtered audiences, the stakes of a single translation choice (a domesticated banlieue, a collapsed racaille) scale with that audience.
  • Across the whole book, the throughline has been: domestication is a choice, not a neutral default — and every choice has a cost the reader rarely sees. That is the single idea worth carrying out of this course into any translation work you do.

Why it matters for you: as a future translator or journalist, this book gives you a concrete, evidence-based vocabulary (domestication/foreignisation, ECRs, the Venutian scale, double mediation) for a decision you will make constantly and mostly invisibly — whether to bring the reader to the foreign text, or bring the foreign text to the reader.

Review Questions

Q&A for Exam Preparation

?Self-test · check yourself8 questions
01What conclusion, widely held in the news translation literature, does this book challenge?
A: The conclusion that foreignisation is simply not relevant or viable in a newswriting context — a position held by scholars such as Bassnett and Bielsa and Bassnett (Session 3), who argue domestication is essential for clear communication in the news.
02Which two core Reuters Handbook instructions does the foreignised approach leave intact, and why?
A: To avoid foreign language, and to translate quotations "idiomatically." The foreignised approach doesn't remove these instructions because it isn't arguing for wholesale foreignisation — it expands on the exceptions the Handbook already allows, keeping the core domesticating instructions as the default.
03Is the foreignised approach, as implemented through the five updates, a radical or incremental change to Reuters' practice?
A: Incremental. Individually, the updates do not dramatically change journalists' day-to-day translation practice; collectively, however, they represent an overall shift toward a more foreignising approach, grounded in guidance the Handbook already implicitly or explicitly supports.
04How does the book itself characterise its contribution — as a finished solution or something else?
A: As an "initial exploration" of the scope for foreignising news translation strategies — offering a basis for dialogue with journalists about the ethical aims and practicality of shifting away from the domestication norm, rather than a finished or mandatory system.
05Name three avenues for future research the conclusion points to.
A: Any three of: (1) collaboration with news agencies to assess real-world impact; (2) research on other English-speaking national news organisations to test which forms/degrees of foreignisation are viable; (3) research involving readers as participants, to add the audience perspective; (4) research in other languages, to test whether retention strategies (Updates 1–2) are viable beyond English-language newswriting.
06Which of the five updates are explicitly noted as not being specific to English-language newswriting?
A: Updates 3 and 4 (which address translation's impact on quotation accuracy) and Update 5 (signalling the foreign context of quotations) — all are described as having direct application in other languages, unlike the retention strategy of Updates 1 and 2, which was developed and informed specifically by English-language analysis.
07What development did Reuters announce in June 2015, and why is it mentioned in the conclusion?
A: Reuters announced it would make some content freely available to digital publishers, explicitly aiming to expose "more readers to its award-winning news content." It's mentioned because it illustrates the continually growing global reach of agency content — reinforcing why the translation strategies of agencies like Reuters matter so much, and why research in this area remains highly relevant.
08According to the conclusion, why do quotation and culture-specific concepts offer a good "starting point" for examining translation in the news, even though translation's role may extend further?
A: Because approaching news translation through these two sites makes it possible to identify precise, clearly distinguishable forms of translation — separable from the intra-lingual editing practices that otherwise make news translation hard to isolate and study (Session 3's "complex object of investigation").

Key Points to Review

  • The Sarkozy case study revealed the "double mediation" of quotation and culture-specific concepts in translated news
  • The book challenges the literature's dismissal of foreignisation as irrelevant to newswriting
  • The foreignised approach keeps Reuters' core domesticating instructions intact — it expands existing exceptions, not the rule
  • Impact is incremental per-update, but collectively shifts practice toward more foreignisation
  • The book positions itself as an initial exploration / basis for dialogue, not a finished solution
  • Future research: agency collaboration, other English-speaking outlets, reader-response studies, non-English languages
  • Updates 3, 4 and 5 are language-general; Updates 1–2's retention strategy needs testing beyond English
  • Reuters' 2015 move to free digital distribution underscores the agencies' still-growing global reach and relevance
