Session 2: The Global News Agencies | Based on Claire Scammell, Translation Strategies in Global News (2018), Ch. 2 | MA Translation Studies

Session Overview

About this session

This chapter looks at the role of the "Big Three" global agencies — Reuters, Agence France-Presse (AFP) and Associated Press (AP) — as providers and translators of foreign news information. It discusses three factors driving the widespread reproduction of agency content, examines examples of that reproduction in the British press, and shows that quotation dominates written news — meaning agency journalists are, very often, the ones translating what the world reads as "what someone said."

1. The "Big Three" and the Globalised News System

Having journalists based in every corner of the globe is an unrealistic ideal for national news organisations. The Big Three global news agencies — Reuters, AP and AFP — instead maintain vast networks of bureaus and reporters worldwide, making this small handful of agencies the world's primary source for breaking news. If "news is a window to the world" (Tuchman 1978), then the global agencies are, to a large extent, responsible for what we see and understand of that world.

Scale:

AFP alone operates 200 bureaus across 150 countries with 2,260 collaborators and 80 nationalities. AP content is seen by half the world's population on any given day. In 2004, Reuters' reporting reached an estimated 74 million people every month.

2. Why Agency Content Dominates: Three Factors

Factor 1 — "Ready-to-publish" content and the inverted pyramid

Agencies release reporting specifically to be reproduced. Reuters describes its content as "ready-to-publish"; AP promises subscribers "ready-to-use story summaries." This is enabled by the inverted pyramid structure — a norm of English-language newswriting established in the late 19th-century US, where the most essential details appear first and background trails at the end (Bell 1991; Cotter 2010). Combined with a lack of connectors between paragraphs, this means a report can easily be shortened by cutting from the bottom or removing whole paragraphs.

Factor 2 — Decline in original foreign reporting

Research (Moore and Loyn 2010) shows a 40% fall in the number of international stories published by four major British newspapers between 1979 and 2009. Traditional print media cannot compete with the agencies' vast global reporter networks; the Media Standards Trust describes the agencies (and the BBC) as the "bedrock of international reporting," while most other outlets keep only a "rump" of foreign correspondents. This has produced a practice called "parachute journalism" — reporters urgently sent abroad only once a story breaks, too late to compete with agencies already "on the ground."

Factor 3 — Online news and aggregators

The Reuters Institute's 2012 Digital Survey found 82% of UK respondents had accessed news online in the past week (vs. 54% for print). The BBC News website led with 58% of respondents, followed by Sky News and Yahoo! News (15% each). Among British newspapers, only Mail Online (13%) and The Guardian (10%) reached the top spots. News aggregators (Yahoo! News, Google News, MSN) republish agency content on portal websites; research shows their "near total dependence" on AP and Reuters content, and agency content also spreads via social media.

3. Agency News in the British Press: Examples from the Corpus

Searching Google News for text strings from the five case-study Reuters reports (see Session 1) produced numerous examples of reproduction via aggregators worldwide, as well as reproductions by "content-producing" media. Two reports were reproduced in edited form by leading British newspapers: SECURITY in the online Daily Mail (Mail Online), and THUGOCRACY in both the online and print Daily Telegraph.

The THUGOCRACY report: three near-identical headlines

Daily Mail: made minimal edits (e.g. "a Paris suburb" → "Paris"; "on Thursday" → "yesterday"), cut some paragraphs from the middle/end, but did not reorder the report.

Daily Telegraph: reordered paragraphs more substantially and used additional quotation sources (from a police-officers' speech) not present in the Reuters original — yet the first five paragraphs remain a carefully edited reproduction of Reuters' paragraphs 1–4 and 13.

A telling edit:

the Telegraph journalist adds that Sarkozy's comments "risked inflaming tensions" and changes "riots" to "violence" — small edits with outsized interpretive effects on the reader's impression of the event.

racaille in the Telegraph's edited THUGOCRACY reproduction

The Reuters report offers two translations of racaille ("scum" or "rabble"), signalling to the reader that the French term has no precise equivalent. The Telegraph version retains only "scum" — the reader has no reason to question its equivalence to racaille. This shows how re-editing by downstream outlets can further reduce the translation's foreignising signal.

4. The SECURITY Report in the Mail Online

Published 8 February 2008, this report was reproduced online by the Mail Online the same day with very limited editing — six of its paragraphs were not edited at all. Where edits occurred, they mostly reworded without changing content: Sarkozy's title was removed, "today" was added, and "poor suburbs" became "riot-stricken slums." Notably, the unusual translation choice "steamblaster" for Kärcher (rather than the more common "power hose" or "industrial cleaner") was retained unedited — a striking, more violent image of what Sarkozy said, passed on unquestioned to the Mail's readers.

5. News Is What Someone Said (As the Agencies Translated It)

The Big Three provide "journalism of information" (Boyd-Barrett and Rantanen 1998); to keep subscribers' trust, they must appear objective and unbiased, and quotation is a primary means of achieving that appearance — reporting fact while distancing the journalist from what was said (Bell 1991; Tuchman 1978).

Content of news = predominantly what someone said.

In a Reuters story about consular protection for a citizen in Turkey (257 words, 8 paragraphs), 4 paragraphs are entirely reported speech and 2 more combine speech with context — only 2 of 8 paragraphs contain no reported speech at all.

More examples from the top Reuters stories analysed:

a report on a US/UK/France/Germany warning to the UN about an Iranian rocket test devotes 6 of 13 paragraphs to quoting a UN resolution and Iran's foreign minister. A report on France's left-wing party

La France Insoumise

defending Venezuela draws discourse from the EU, the US, the French government, three party members and the party leader's election manifesto in 7 of 10 paragraphs.

Implication: whenever the underlying speech event occurred in a foreign language, this predominance of quotation means a high proportion of everyday news content is, in practice, translation — even though (as later chapters show) agency journalists rarely think of themselves as translators.

Schäffner (2010) and Holland (2006) — how one translation becomes "the truth"

Since agency journalists are most often the ones who first translate quotations that circulate through the global news system, their choices become fixed. Schäffner found that of several competing translations of a controversial 2005 speech by President Ahmadinejad, one particular version became the one overwhelmingly used afterwards — regardless of its accuracy. Holland similarly observed the striking "unanimity" of British press reporting of a 1999 statement by Indonesia's president. The agencies' translation choices don't just report an event — they can effectively become it, for readers worldwide.

✦ Why This Matters for a Translator

This chapter establishes a structural fact that underlies everything the book argues: a handful of agencies effectively write the first translation of most foreign speech that reaches global audiences, and that translation is then copied, largely unedited, across dozens of downstream outlets.

  • The inverted pyramid and lack of connectors make agency copy easy to cut and paste — but also easy to reproduce without re-examining the translation choices embedded in it.
  • The Telegraph's THUGOCRACY edit, which collapsed Reuters' two-option translation of racaille down to one ("scum"), shows how re-editing downstream can silently erase a foreignising signal the original agency report preserved.
  • Because a single agency translation can become "the" translation for millions of readers (as with Ahmadinejad's speech), the ethical stakes of an agency journalist's local translation decisions are far higher than for an ordinary one-off news translation.

Why it matters for you: understanding how far and how fast an agency's translation choices propagate helps explain why later chapters focus so intensely on Reuters specifically — a change to one agency's practice has an outsized, multiplied effect on what the world reads as "what was said."

Review Questions

Q&A for Exam Preparation

?Self-test · check yourself8 questions
01Name the "Big Three" global news agencies and explain why national news organisations cannot compete with them for foreign reporting.
A: Reuters, Agence France-Presse (AFP) and Associated Press (AP). They maintain vast global networks of bureaus and correspondents that individual national organisations cannot afford to sustain, making the agencies the world's primary, fastest source for breaking foreign news — while other outlets increasingly rely on "parachute journalism," sending reporters abroad only after a story has already broken.
02What is the "inverted pyramid" structure, and how does it contribute to the widespread reproduction of agency content?
A: A newswriting convention (established in the late 19th-century US) that places the most essential information in the first paragraphs, with background and detail trailing at the end. Combined with a lack of connecting language between paragraphs, this makes agency reports easy to shorten by simply cutting from the bottom or removing paragraphs — encouraging downstream outlets to reproduce agency copy with minimal effort.
03Compare how the Daily Mail and Daily Telegraph each reproduced the THUGOCRACY report.
A: The Daily Mail made minimal wording edits (e.g. "a Paris suburb" → "Paris") and cut some paragraphs but kept the Reuters paragraph order intact. The Daily Telegraph made more substantial changes — reordering paragraphs, adding new quotations from other sources, and altering wording (e.g. "risked inflaming tensions," "violence" instead of "riots") — yet its first five paragraphs remain a recognisable, edited reproduction of Reuters' report.
04How did the Telegraph's version of the THUGOCRACY report reduce the foreignising effect of Reuters' original translation of "racaille"?
A: Reuters offered two possible translations ("scum" or "rabble"), signalling to readers that racaille has no exact English equivalent. The Telegraph's edited version retained only "scum," removing that signal and giving readers no reason to question the word's equivalence to racaille.
05What percentage of UK respondents in the Reuters Institute's 2012 survey had accessed news online in the past week, and which source led?
A: 82% (compared with 54% for print). The BBC News website led as an online news source with 58% of respondents, followed by Sky News and Yahoo! News at 15% each.
06Why does the chapter claim that news content is "predominantly what someone said"?
A: Analysis of Reuters' top stories shows most paragraphs consist of direct or reported speech from officials, institutions or documents — in one example, only 2 of 8 paragraphs contained no reported speech at all. Since quotation dominates written news, and much of that speech occurs in foreign languages, a high proportion of everyday news content is effectively translation.
07What did Schäffner's (2010) research on translations of Ahmadinejad's 2005 speech reveal about agency translation?
A: Despite the accuracy of the translation being debated, one particular English rendering became by far the most-used version once it entered the media system — showing how an agency's early translation choice can become fixed and accepted as "what was said," regardless of its fidelity to the original.
08What unusual translation for "Kärcher" was retained unedited in the Mail Online's reproduction of the SECURITY report, and why is this significant?
A: "Steamblaster" — a more striking and violent-sounding choice than the more common "power hose" or "industrial cleaner." Its unedited survival into the Mail Online version shows how a single agency journalist's local translation decision can be passed on to another outlet's readers completely unquestioned.

Key Points to Review

  • The "Big Three": Reuters, AFP, AP — the world's primary sources for breaking foreign news
  • Three drivers of agency-content dominance: ready-to-publish/inverted-pyramid copy, decline in original foreign reporting, and the rise of online aggregators
  • Case studies: THUGOCRACY (Daily Mail vs. Daily Telegraph reproductions) and SECURITY (Mail Online, minimal editing)
  • Downstream re-editing can quietly erase an agency's foreignising signals (e.g. dropping one of two offered translations of racaille)
  • News is predominantly "what someone said" — quotation dominates written news
  • Agency journalists are, in effect, the first translators of much of the world's foreign-language news
  • Schäffner/Holland: a single agency translation can become fixed as "the" accepted version, regardless of accuracy
