Study Guide · Susan Bassnett, Translation Studies, Ch. 2
From the Romans to the Internet — lines of approach across two millennia

1. The Central Argument

The study of translation is "a vital part of literary and cultural history." Rather than rigid

periodization

(which contradicts culture's dynamism — Lotman), Bassnett traces recurring

lines of approach

, above all the word-for-word vs. sense-for-sense line. The chapter spans from the Romans to the Internet age, covering Skopos theory, the cultural turn, and technological transformation.

A long historical survey. Focus on: Steiner's four periods, Romans as enrichment, Bible translation and the vernacular, Dryden and the systematizers, the Victorian archaizing debate (Arnold vs. Newman, Longfellow vs. Fitzgerald), the cultural turn, and the Internet paradox.

2. Steiner's Four Periods

(1) Cicero/Horace to Tytler (1791) — "immediate empirical focus." (2) To Larbaud (1946) — theory and hermeneutic enquiry. (3) From the 1940s — machine translation and structural linguistics. (4) From the 1960s — return to hermeneutic/metaphysical enquiry. Critique: first period spans ~1700 years, the last two cover 30 years. Idiosyncratic but avoids rigid compartmentalization.

3. Romans to the Renaissance

  • Romans: Jacobsen: "translation is a Roman invention" (hyperbole). Cicero and Horace: word-for-word vs. interpretation that produces an aesthetically pleasing TL. Translation enriched the target literature.
  • Bible translation (16th c.): Three aims: clarify errors from manuscripts, produce accessible vernacular style, clarify dogma. Luther: "grammar shall not rule over the meaning." Erasmus wanted the "plowman to sing Scripture." King Alfred (9th c.) translated "sometimes word by word, sometimes sense by sense" — function determined method.
  • Folena: Vertical translation (from prestige SL like Latin into vernacular) vs. horizontal (between languages of similar value). In medieval canon, imitatio was prized over originality.
  • Dolet (1540): Five principles — executed for heresy. Chapman: avoid word-for-word, reach the "spirit," base work on scholarship.
  • Renaissance: Affirmation of the present through contemporary idiom (North's Plutarch, Holland the "translator general" using "Lords" for Roman terms). Wyatt adapted Petrarch for function. Steiner: translation was the matière première of the imagination.

4. Dryden, Pope, Goethe, Tytler

Dryden's triad:

metaphrase / paraphrase / imitation; prefers paraphrase. Translators as portrait-painters.

Goethe's three epochs:

(1) acquainting us with the foreign on our own terms (Luther's Bible); (2) appropriation through substitution (French tradition); (3) the highest — perfect identity through a new "manner" (Voss's Homer).

Tytler (1791):

first systematic English study; three laws; moral duty to the reader.

5. Romantics and Victorians: The Archaizing Debate

Romanticism stressed Imagination (Coleridge). Two tendencies: translation as creative genius vs. mechanical function. The idea that meaning lies "below and between" language led to stress on untranslatability and deliberate archaism.

William Morris: Translated Norse sagas, Homer and Virgil into deliberately archaic, difficult English. Paradox: immense respect for the original paradoxically turned translation into a minority interest.
Arnold vs. Newman: Arnold said translate for "the competent scholar" who knows the original. He coined "to newmanize" as a term of abuse. Longfellow: "The business of the translator is to report what the author says, not to explain what he means." Extreme literalism. Fitzgerald: "Better a live sparrow than a stuffed eagle" — the translator as creative agent. Author of Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam.
Bassnett's five late-19th c. types: (1) Scholar's activity; (2) Encouraging return to the ST; (3) Deliberate foreignness (Schleiermacher tradition); (4) Translator's pragmatic choice (Fitzgerald); (5) Upgrading a perceived low-status ST.

6. Skopos Theory and the Cultural Turn

Two broad lines (late 20th c.):

(1)

Translation and power

— ideological, postcolonial (Even-Zohar, Lefevere/Bassnett's cultural turn 1990, Tymoczko, Spivak, Bourdieu, Derrida). Even-Zohar: cultures translate according to need (established cultures translate less). Translation is never innocent. (2)

Functionalism/Skopos theory

— Reiss, Vermeer, Nord. Gentzler: "breaks the two thousand year-old chain of theory revolving around the faithful vs. free axis." But functionalism leans toward domestication, creating tension with Venuti's foreignization.

7. The Internet Age

Pym's paradox:

More information ≠ more reading. Even highly educated web users read only ~25% of available content. If content isn't read carefully, less care may be taken with translation — a far cry from poetry debates hinging on a single word.

Audiovisual/technological translation

should be considered an autonomous field. "The old debates about faithfulness, equivalence, foreignization versus domestication, and the visibility of the translator are no longer relevant in this new context."

Exam Q&A

?Self-test · check yourself5 questions
01Why does Bassnett resist periodization?
A: Culture is dynamic (Lotman). Fixing stages contradicts that dynamism. She traces recurring lines of approach instead.
02Explain the Victorian archaizing paradox.
A: Archaizing showed "immense respect" for the original but restricted translation to an intellectual minority, paradoxically contributing to its devaluation.
03Contrast Arnold, Longfellow and Fitzgerald.
A: Arnold: serve the competent scholar. Longfellow: extreme literalism ("report, don't explain"). Fitzgerald: "live sparrow than stuffed eagle" — creative freedom.
04What were the two late 20th-century lines?
A: (1) Translation and power (postcolonial, cultural turn). (2) Functionalism/Skopos theory (purpose-driven, breaking the faithful/free axis).
05What is Pym's Internet paradox?
A: Info proliferation ≠ more reading. If even highly educated users read ~25%, lower translation quality may become acceptable — far from the care taken with poetry.
