Study Guide · Christiane Nord, Translating as a Purposeful Activity, Ch. 6
Skopostheorie applied to interpreting, and Pöchhacker on simultaneous interpreting

1. What This Chapter Is About

Skopostheorie claims to be a

general

theory of translation — so it should apply to interpreting too. This chapter looks at the role of interpreting in Skopostheorie, the "from interpreting to translation" training method, and Pöchhacker's model of

simultaneous interpreting

.

Know the term Translation (Kade) covering both modes, the differences vs. similarities between translating and interpreting, the Y-model and "from interpreting to translation" reform, and Pöchhacker's ideas: conference as hypertext, diaculture.

2. Interpreting Within Skopostheorie

Translation (Kade 1968): A generic German term (with German pronunciation) covering both written translation (Übersetzen) and oral interpreting (Dolmetschen) — seen as two varieties of the same intercultural communicative interaction.
Interpreting (Kade): The translation of a source-language text presented only once, usually orally, into a target-language text which is very difficult to check and can hardly be corrected because of lack of time.
The main difference: correctability.

A written translation can be revised after it's written down. An interpretation must be regarded as complete at the moment of production. The source is presented only once and received in successive parts; both texts are transitory; and the source and target communications share the same situational conditions.

Key similarities: Both seek a communicative purpose (Skopos rule); both are a target-culture "offer of information" about a source-culture offer; both must meet intratextual coherence; both should be coherent with their source texts (fidelity rule).

3. Training Reform: "From Interpreting to Translation"

German translator training follows the

Y-model

: shared initial training, then specialization into either translation or interpreting. Vermeer & Ammann (1990) proposed a reform: start with

interpreting

first — not professional simultaneous/consecutive interpreting, but the basic ability to "make someone understand someone else's message." This trains students to grasp the communicative purpose without being tempted to reproduce source structures word-for-word.

The advantage: students learn the importance of extratextual clues (time, place, speaker, listeners) and the communicative purpose. Without a written "source text" to lean on, they can't fall into word-for-word reproduction. This helps them escape what Wilss called the source text's "hypnotic compulsion."

4. Pöchhacker: Simultaneous Interpreting

Simultaneous interpreting (Pöchhacker): "The act of target text production in synchrony with the production and/or presentation of a source text."
The conference as hypertext:

Individual speeches at a conference aren't independent units each with their own Skopos. They're components of a greater semiotic whole — the conference itself, a

hypertext

with its own purpose. Pöchhacker locates the Skopos of simultaneous interpreting at the level of the

conference assignment

, not the individual target text.

The interpreter must take in non-verbal information (slides, gestures, body language) as part of the "source text," but is limited mainly to verbal and paraverbal means of production. Time-lag can create an asynchronous combination of auditory and visual signals for the receiver.

Diaculture (Vermeer): When English is used as a lingua franca at international conferences, the relevant "culture" isn't a national one — it's a group culture defined by shared professional background, technical expertise, and history of interaction. This "diacultural" ground enables successful communication despite different language backgrounds.

Pöchhacker admits his model doesn't challenge other functionalist approaches, but it shows Skopostheorie CAN be applied to simultaneous interpreting — a valuable basis for training and evaluation, with much research still to be done.

Exam Q&A

?Self-test · check yourself5 questions
01Why does Skopostheorie claim to apply to interpreting?
A: Because it's a general theory of translation. Both translating and interpreting are purposeful communicative actions governed by the Skopos rule.
02What is the main difference between translating and interpreting?
A: Correctability. Written translation can be revised after production; interpreting is complete at the moment of production — the source is presented only once, both texts are transitory.
03What does "from interpreting to translation" mean in training?
A: Students start with the basic ability to "make someone understand" (interpreting in the original sense), then move to written translation. This trains them to focus on communicative purpose and avoid word-for-word reproduction.
04Explain Pöchhacker's idea of the conference as a "hypertext."
A: Individual speeches aren't independent units with their own Skopos — they're components of the greater semiotic whole of the conference. The Skopos of simultaneous interpreting is at the level of the conference assignment.
05What is "diaculture" and why is it useful?
A: A group culture defined by shared professional background and expertise, transcending national/language borders. When English is a conference lingua franca, the participants' common diacultural ground — not national culture — enables successful communication.
