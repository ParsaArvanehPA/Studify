Session 6: The Domestication Norm in Reuters Journalism | Based on Claire Scammell, Translation Strategies in Global News (2018), Ch. 6 | MA Translation Studies

Session Overview

About this session

This chapter applies Pedersen's taxonomy (Session 5) to the five-report Reuters corpus, to locate exactly how foreignising or domesticating Reuters' current translations of culture-specific concepts really are. It then examines the Reuters Handbook of Journalism's own guidance on translation, and how closely Reuters' actual reporting follows it.

1. Strategies Used in the Reuters Corpus: The Numbers

Pedersen's taxonomy is applied to English translations of French culture-specific concepts across the five-report corpus. Since only five of Pedersen's six main strategies apply to translation proper (Omission is intra-lingual, not a translation choice — Session 5), the results are:

SL-oriented (foreignising) — Total: 8TL-oriented (domesticating) — Total: 21

Headline finding: the TL-oriented (domesticating) strategies (21 instances) considerably outnumber the SL-oriented (foreignising) strategies (8 instances). Substitution — the most domesticating strategy — is by far the most common (14 instances); Retention — the most foreignising — is the least common (just 1 instance).

2. SL-Oriented (Foreignising) Strategies in Practice

Retention — the single instance

Only once in the whole corpus is retention used: in the THUGOCRACY report, quartier and cité are kept untranslated, combined with an "Addition" — "The 'quartiers' or 'cites', as the estates are known..." The added phrase ensures the ECRs remain intelligible, which somewhat reduces the foreignising effect, but the choice to retain foreign language at all is unique in the corpus — no other instance of retained foreign language appears anywhere else.

the ECR is kept untranslated but made more specific with latent/connotative information a French reader wouldn't need. Examples: "the

3. TL-Oriented (Domesticating) Strategies in Practice

Generalization — 7 instances

Replaces a specific ECR with something more general — detaching it from the source-culture reality entirely. Four instances involve pejorative expressions used in French media to represent the banlieues negatively: quartiers sensibles → "problem neighbourhoods"; quartiers difficiles/en difficulté → "difficult areas"; jeunes des cités → "suburban youths" (see Session 4). The remaining three instances all involve CRS (Compagnies Républicaines de Sécurité, France's notorious riot police reserve, created 1945), rendered simply as "riot police" or "specially trained police," with the French institutional name dropped entirely.

Substitution — 14 instances (the most common strategy overall)

Removes the source ECR entirely and replaces it with either a different, target-culture ECR ("Cultural" substitution — all 14 instances in the corpus are this type) or a paraphrase. Every rendering of banlieue uses "suburb"; every rendering of cité and quartier uses "housing estate" or "neighbourhood." In 6 of the 14 instances, Substitution is combined with Addition (e.g. "France's poor suburbs, residential areas on the outskirts of big cities" for banlieue in the SECURITY report's lead) — arguably somewhat less domesticating, since it gives the reader extra source-culture context beyond just the English substitute term.

Why calling English "suburb" an ECR is complex:

the Reuters journalists aren't writing for a single national target culture — "housing estate," for instance, means something different depending which English-speaking country the reader is in. But the terms still carry

some

culture-bound reality wherever read, which is enough to warrant treating them as ECRs for this analysis.

4. Translation Guidance in the Reuters Handbook

The 578-page Reuters Handbook of Journalism does not include a dedicated section on translation — consistent with the finding (Session 3) that agencies don't formally recognise translation as separate from editing. The only explicit guidance on translating quotation appears in two near-identical paragraphs:

Standards & Values > Accuracy > Quotes: "When translating quotes from one language into another, we should do so in an idiomatic way rather than with pedantic literalness. Care must be taken to ensure that the tone of the translation is equivalent to the tone of the original."

General Style Guide > quotations: "When translating quotes from another language into English, do so in an idiomatic way rather than with pedantic literalness. However, give a literal translation if a statement is tendentious and likely to be the subject of close analysis."

An idiomatic default: in both paragraphs, journalists are instructed to translate "idiomatically" as the general rule — prioritising natural, comprehensible meaning over literal form — which qualifies as a domesticating approach. Literal translation is specified only as an exception, for tendentious statements likely to face close scrutiny.

Other relevant guidance

  • "Avoid excessive use of direct quotes in English when a speaker has spoken in another language" (General Style Guide, 12th paragraph) — an institutional awareness of translation's impact on quotation accuracy, without naming translation directly.
  • "Use such phrases or quotes only in exceptional cases, for instance where no generally recognised English equivalent exists" (foreign language phrases entry) — explicitly discouraging retention of foreign language, which is consistent with retention being the rarest strategy found in the corpus (just 1 instance).
  • If a French politician gives an interview to an American newspaper, "it is almost certain that the translation back into French will be wrong... the fewer quotes and the more reported speech, the better" — an institutional acknowledgment that translation can distort quotation.

5. "Golden Quotes" and Broken Quotes

The Handbook advises journalists to open with a strong direct quote — the "golden quote" that "will best illustrate the main point of the story." In four of the five corpus reports, this golden quote appears as a "broken" quote (only the sensitive/colourful word itself in quotation marks, e.g. Bloggs said she was "angry enough to kill"). Advice on broken quotes: "Avoid 'broken' quotes unless the words are unusual, contentious or highly colorful."

The THUGOCRACY report is the exception

that most closely matches the book's later "foreignised" proposal: its broken quote keeps the French word in brackets alongside

two

possible translations —

"racaille"

("scum" or "rabble") — the only instance in the corpus where original French wording is retained within a quotation at all.

How rare is retention really? A Factiva check

Searching all Reuters English-language reporting (via the Factiva database) found banlieue retained in only 27 reports total, and quartier retained in just 3 — despite Reuters publishing around 45 France-related reports per day. This confirms that the THUGOCRACY report's retention of quartier/cité is highly atypical of Reuters' broader current practice, not a hidden norm.

✦ Why This Matters for a Translator

This chapter converts everything theoretical from Sessions 3–5 into hard evidence: 21 domesticating choices to 8 foreignising ones, and only a single instance of true retention across a whole corpus.

  • The Handbook's own wording — "idiomatic... rather than pedantic literalness" as the default, literal translation only for "tendentious" statements — already tells you which way Reuters leans before you even look at the corpus data. The corpus simply confirms the Handbook is followed closely in practice.
  • The THUGOCRACY report's double-translation of racaille is important because it shows Reuters journalists are capable of a more foreignising choice when a word is genuinely untranslatable — it isn't a technical impossibility, just an exception to current practice.
  • The Factiva check matters methodologically: it proves the single retained instance in the corpus isn't cherry-picked evidence of a broader hidden tendency — it really is exceptional across Reuters' entire output.

Why it matters for you: this chapter is the direct evidentiary basis for the five concrete Handbook updates proposed in the next session — each update targets a specific pattern identified here (e.g. the "foreign language phrases" entry's exceptions-only wording, or the "idiomatic... unless tendentious" quotes guidance).

Review Questions

Q&A for Exam Preparation

?Self-test · check yourself8 questions
01Summarise the overall balance of foreignising vs. domesticating strategies found in the Reuters corpus.
A: SL-oriented (foreignising) strategies total 8 instances (Retention: 1, Specification: 6, Direct translation: 1); TL-oriented (domesticating) strategies total 21 instances (Generalization: 7, Substitution: 14). Domesticating strategies considerably outnumber foreignising ones, and Substitution is by far the single most common strategy.
02Describe the one instance of Retention found in the corpus.
A: In the THUGOCRACY report, quartier and cité are kept untranslated, combined with an Addition explaining them: "The 'quartiers' or 'cites', as the estates are known..." It is the only instance of retained foreign language anywhere in the corpus.
03How are quartiers sensibles and CRS both examples of Generalization?
A: Quartiers sensibles (a specific, officially defined French urban-policy category) is rendered as the more general "problem neighbourhoods," losing its specific institutional meaning. CRS (a named French riot-police institution created in 1945) is rendered simply as "riot police" or "specially trained police," dropping the specific French institutional name in favour of a general description.
04Why is "Cultural substitution" the most domesticating type of Substitution, and how does it apply to banlieue?
A: Cultural substitution replaces the source-culture ECR with a different, target-culture ECR — obscuring the foreign reality entirely by presenting a "local" equivalent instead. Every instance of banlieue in the corpus is translated as "suburb," a term that designates a genuinely different socio-economic reality in English (see Session 4).
05What does the Reuters Handbook instruct journalists to do when translating quotes, and what is the stated exception?
A: Translate "in an idiomatic way rather than with pedantic literalness" as the general rule (a domesticating instruction), but "give a literal translation if a statement is tendentious and likely to be the subject of close analysis" as the exception.
06What is a "golden quote," and what is unusual about how it appears in four of the five corpus reports?
A: The Handbook's term for the strongest direct quote in a report, ideally used early to "best illustrate the main point of the story." In four of the five corpus reports, the golden quote takes the form of a "broken" quote (only the most sensitive word or phrase in quotation marks) — advised only for words that are "unusual, contentious or highly colorful."
07How does the THUGOCRACY report's handling of "racaille" differ from the rest of the corpus, and why is it significant?
A: It retains the French word racaille in the quotation and offers two possible translations ("scum" or "rabble"), rather than silently choosing one. It is significant because it is the only instance in the corpus where original French wording is preserved inside a quotation — showing Reuters journalists can and occasionally do use a more foreignising strategy.
08What did the Factiva search across all Reuters reporting confirm about the retention of banlieue and quartier?
A: Banlieue was retained in only 27 Reuters reports total, and quartier in just 3 — despite roughly 45 France-related Reuters reports being published per day. This confirms the corpus's single retention instance is genuinely atypical of Reuters' broader current practice, not evidence of a hidden, more common tendency.

Key Points to Review

  • Corpus totals: SL-oriented (foreignising) = 8 (Retention 1, Specification 6, Direct translation 1); TL-oriented (domesticating) = 21 (Generalization 7, Substitution 14)
  • Substitution (most domesticating) is the most common strategy; Retention (most foreignising) is the rarest
  • THUGOCRACY's retention of quartier/cité, and its double-translation of racaille, are unique in the corpus
  • Reuters Handbook: "idiomatic... rather than pedantic literalness" is the default (domesticating); literal translation only for "tendentious" statements
  • Foreign language phrases discouraged except in "exceptional cases" — consistent with retention being rare in practice
  • "Golden quotes" often appear as "broken" quotes in the corpus, used for unusual/contentious/colourful words
  • Factiva check: banlieue retained in only 27 Reuters reports total, quartier in just 3 — confirms retention is genuinely atypical
