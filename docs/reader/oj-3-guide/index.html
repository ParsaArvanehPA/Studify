Session 3: Translation in Global News | Based on Claire Scammell, Translation Strategies in Global News (2018), Ch. 3 | MA Translation Studies

Session Overview

About this session

This chapter reviews what the news-translation literature says about the nature of translation in written news, and asks whether the practice is different enough from "traditional" translation to need a new name. It then examines the translation-studies debate on domestication and foreignisation (Venuti), and closes by looking at why domestication is the norm specifically in news.

1. Does News Translation Need a New Name? "Transediting"

Bielsa and Bassnett (2009) describe the translation carried out within news agencies as "rewriting", concluding that the level of intervention required from the translator changes their traditional role. The closest the field has come to a new label is "transediting", coined by Stetting (1989) to reflect the level of editing fundamental to news translation — e.g. removing "passages which might be irrelevant in the foreign context."

Transediting

— some researchers (Hernández Guerrero 2010; Lu and Chen 2011) adopt the term; Bielsa and Bassnett reject it as implying "the existence of another form of translating news." Schäffner (2012) concludes the term aids understanding of news translation as a distinct genre, but risks making it harder to distinguish, for researchers' outside audiences, which parts of a report actually

are

translation.

2. A Complex Object of Investigation

Translation's involvement in newswriting is typically limited and localised — confined to parts of a report where a journalist must render foreign-language material. Van Doorslaer (2010) describes translation as an "integral part" of a complex, integrated task combining information gathering, translating, selecting, reinterpreting, contextualising and editing.

Crucially, agencies do not translate the same report into their newswires' other languages; different reports are produced for different markets, adjusted to what is considered relevant and to readers' assumed background knowledge (Bielsa 2007; Wilke and Rosenberger 1994).

Bielsa and Bassnett (2009) — journalists don't see themselves as translators

Interviews with agency staff found that "journalists tend to be initially surprised when they are asked about their role as news translators, because they do not see translation as a separate process from the edition of texts." Davier's (2014) interviews at AFP's Swiss bureau found journalists use the word "editing" in place of "translation" to describe the same interlingual work — distancing themselves from the practice.

There is generally no single text that can be considered "the" source, and the result is not a "target" text but essentially a new text. Davier (2014) describes news agency reports as "patchworks of many different sources, many of which were originally in a different language."

3. Recontextualisation vs. Domestication: Two Names, One Idea

Researchers examining different news organisations and language pairs have all identified similar kinds of intra- and inter-lingual change that occur when news is "recontextualised" for a new audience in a new language (Bani 2006; Bielsa and Bassnett 2009; Franjié 2009; Hursti 2001; Kang 2007; Schäffner 2008, 2012).

No real distinction:

"recontextualisation" (news translation literature) and "domestication" (journalism studies literature — Clausen 2004, 2009; Gurevitch et al. 1991) describe essentially the same intra-lingual adjustment process, differing only in that domestication debates rarely acknowledge the role of translation explicitly. This book reserves "domestication" for the specific translation-studies sense (a "global" strategy — see Session 5 for global vs. local strategies), to avoid confusing the two.

4. Foreignisation and Domestication: A Translation Studies Debate

Schleiermacher's original formulation (1813)

"Either the translator leaves the author in peace as much as possible and moves the reader toward him; or he leaves the reader in peace as much as possible and moves the writer toward him." (Schleiermacher 1813/2006, p. 49)

Venuti takes Schleiermacher's idea further, adding an ideological dimension and naming the two opposing methods: foreignisation — leaving the author in peace — and domestication — leaving the reader in peace. Venuti firmly seconds Schleiermacher's preference for foreignisation, calling for translators to adopt it as a form of "resistance" against "ethnocentrism, racism, cultural narcissism and imperialism" (Venuti 2008, p. 16).

Criticism of Venuti

  • Pym (1996): Venuti's "translator's invisibility" is not a uniquely Anglo-American phenomenon as claimed; the same tendency toward fluency exists in other markets too.
  • Baker (2010): translators do not select purely one method or the other; they make individual choices, some foreignising and some domesticating.
  • Munday (2010): criticisms reflect a tendency to treat foreignisation/domestication as strict binary opposites — a framing Venuti himself denies ("I do not establish a neat binary opposition," Venuti 2008, p. 19).

The book notes these criticisms target mainly how Venuti's arguments are packaged (provocatively), not whether the underlying ideas are new — similar arguments about exposing rather than obscuring foreignness appear earlier in Berman's (1984) L'épreuve de l'étranger.

Parallel concepts:

House's (1977) "overt/covert" translation (a covert translation applies a "cultural filter" — i.e. domestication); Nida's (1964) "formal" vs. "dynamic" equivalence, where dynamic equivalence "aims at complete naturalness of expression" — Venuti himself cites this as evidence of Nida's emphasis on fluency.

5. News Translation and the Domestication Norm

Venuti repeatedly returns to two arguments relevant to this book's case for a degree of foreignisation in the news:

  • since translation always involves interpretation, the translator has an ethical obligation to signal to readers that they are reading a translation and an interpretation — obscuring this "inscribes the foreign text with a partial interpretation" while masquerading as a "true semantic equivalent" (Venuti 2008, p. 16);
  • a translation can "deviate from domestic norms to signal the foreignness of the foreign text and create a readership that is more open to linguistic and cultural differences" (Venuti 1998, p. 87).

Yet Venuti's arguments are largely dismissed in the news translation literature on the grounds that domestication is necessary for clear communication.

Bassnett (2005) — domestication as "essential"

Examining a Reuters translation of a televised Saddam Hussein speech in the British press, Bassnett argues domestication is "essential" and foreignisation "detrimental to understanding." The translator "has made no attempt to rework the speech for an Anglophone audience. The result is a text full of awkward English phrases that are sometimes meaningless" (p. 127).

In a further example, Bassnett discusses a Reuters translation of an Al Qaeda statement: the headline translation "The cars of death will not stop" has the effect of "distanc[ing] the producers of the text from Western reality" by highlighting their resistance to modernity through their choice of language — an effect Bassnett suggests was likely accidental (the journalist was probably just following the Handbook's instruction to "give a literal translation if a statement is tendentious").

The book's position: while an absolute foreignising strategy would not be viable for a genre aiming at clear communication, some degree of foreignisation might be possible without jeopardising that goal. This possibility is what the "foreignised" approach (developed in Chapter 7) explores.

✦ Why This Matters for a Translator

This chapter clarifies a distinction that's easy to blur: "domestication" in journalism studies and "domestication" in translation studies are not automatically the same thing, even though they describe similar-looking adjustments.

  • Journalism's "recontextualisation" happens to every news story, translated or not — a domestic story gets reframed for its audience just as a foreign one does.
  • Translation studies' "domestication" is specifically about a strategy applied at the point where source-language material crosses into the target language.
  • The Bassnett/Al Qaeda example is the clearest illustration in the chapter: an accidental, over-literal translation ("cars of death") can foreignise a text in a way that actively damages the source's credibility with readers — proving domestication isn't just a stylistic preference, it protects meaning and fairness too.

Why it matters for you: recognising which "domestication" a scholar means, and seeing that even accidental literalism can distort a speaker's image, is the theoretical foundation for the practical Reuters-corpus analysis coming in Session 6.

Review Questions

Q&A for Exam Preparation

?Self-test · check yourself8 questions
01What is "transediting," and why do Bielsa and Bassnett reject the term?
A: Coined by Stetting (1989), transediting describes the level of editing fundamental to news translation — e.g. removing content irrelevant to the new audience. Bielsa and Bassnett reject the term because it implies news translation is "another form of translating," a "somewhat artificial concept" that isn't needed to describe what is, in their view, still translation.
02Why do agency journalists tend to be surprised when asked about their role as "translators"?
A: Because they do not experience translation as a separate practice from ordinary editing — the interlingual element of their work is fully integrated into everyday newswriting. Davier's interviews found AFP journalists use "editing" rather than "translation" to describe the same task, actively distancing themselves from the label "translator."
03Why does this book keep "domestication" reserved for its translation-studies sense, rather than merging it with journalism's "recontextualisation"?
A: Because "recontextualisation" in journalism studies describes intra-lingual reframing that happens to any news story, translated or not, while "domestication" in translation studies (Venuti) refers specifically to a strategic choice made when rendering source-language material into the target language. Merging the two labels would blur an already complex picture.
04Define domestication and foreignisation using Schleiermacher's original 1813 formulation.
A: Foreignisation: "the translator leaves the author in peace... and moves the reader toward him" — the reader travels to meet the foreign text. Domestication: "he leaves the reader in peace... and moves the writer toward him" — the text is adapted to come to the reader.
05Summarise Baker's (2010) criticism of Venuti's foreignisation/domestication framework.
A: Baker argues that translators do not select one overall method or the other; instead, they make individual, local choices — some of which might be considered foreignising and others domesticating — challenging the idea of a clean, binary strategic split.
06What did Bassnett (2005) find in her analysis of a Reuters translation of a Saddam Hussein speech?
A: She found the translator made no attempt to rework the speech for an Anglophone audience, producing "a text full of awkward English phrases that are sometimes meaningless" — evidence, in her argument, that domestication is essential and foreignisation detrimental to understanding in a news context.
07In the Al Qaeda statement example, why does Bassnett argue the translated headline "The cars of death will not stop" distances the speakers from Western reality — and was this likely intentional?
A: The literal, foreignising translation highlights the producers' resistance to modernity through the unusual language they chose. Bassnett suggests it was likely not intentional — the journalist was probably following the Reuters Handbook's instruction to give a literal translation for "tendentious" statements, rather than deliberately trying to create a distancing effect.
08What is the book's own position on foreignisation in news translation, as set out at the end of this chapter?
A: That while an absolute foreignising strategy would be unviable for a genre requiring clear communication, some degree of foreignisation might be introduced without jeopardising that clarity — the possibility the book explores as its "foreignised" approach in Chapter 7.

Key Points to Review

  • "Transediting" (Stetting 1989) — a proposed but contested new label for news translation
  • Agency journalists typically don't see translation as separate from editing (Bielsa and Bassnett; Davier)
  • News translation output is a "new" text, not a clean target of a single source ("patchwork of many sources")
  • "Recontextualisation" (journalism studies) ≈ "domestication" (translation studies), but the book keeps the terms distinct
  • Schleiermacher (1813) → Venuti (2008): foreignisation (author to reader) vs. domestication (text to reader)
  • Critics of Venuti: Pym (not uniquely Anglo-American), Baker (not a binary choice), Munday (binary framing itself is the issue)
  • Bassnett: domestication as "essential" in news; the Al Qaeda "cars of death" example shows unintended foreignising harm
  • Book's position: absolute foreignisation unviable, but a degree of foreignisation may be possible — sets up Chapter 7
