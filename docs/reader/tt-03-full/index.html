Study Guide · Anthony Pym, Exploring Translation Theories (2014), Ch. 1
The chain from theorizing to theories to paradigms — and why theory matters

1. What This Chapter Is Actually About

Here's the big idea:

Pym's book is organized around

paradigms

, not individual theorists. But first, he needs to explain what a "theory" even IS when we talk about translation. His answer might surprise you:

all translators are always theorizing

, whether they know it or not. Every time you face a translation problem and pick one solution over another, you're bringing ideas about translation into play — that's theorizing. This chapter is about how that internal process becomes public theory, how theories group into paradigms, and why studying theory is actually useful.

Know the chain: theorizing → theories → paradigms. Understand the "Tory" example. And know Pym's six paradigms. Also important: why Pym says "start text" instead of "source text" — it's not just word choice, it's a theoretical argument.

2. All Translators Theorize — Whether They Like It or Not

Pym starts with a simple observation: translators are always solving problems. Let's say you have to translate the British political term "Tory." What do you do?

  • Keep the English term and add a footnote explaining it?
  • Just say "Conservative Party"?
  • Name the equivalent party in the target culture?
  • Leave it out entirely if the context doesn't need it?
The point:

All of those options could be legitimate — it depends on the text, the purpose, and the client. But

choosing between them

means you're bringing ideas into play about what translation should do. Should it explain the source culture? Make things understandable? Re-situate everything in the target culture? Every time you make that choice — even in split seconds — you're

theorizing

.

The word "theory" itself comes from Greek theā (view) + -horan (to see). Same root as "theatre." A theory "sets the scene" where the generation and selection of solutions takes place. So when Pym talks about "theories," he means the conceptual scenes we create to think about translation.

3. When Does Theorizing Become Public Theory?

There's a difference between

private theorizing

(what every translator does internally) and

public theory

. Private theorizing becomes public theory when there's

disagreement

. If two translators have different solutions to the same problem, one might argue "translation should explain the source culture" while the other says "it should make things understandable to the target culture." Those are theoretical positions. Without disagreement, people just accept common terms and move on.

Pym makes an interesting point about his own language: he deliberately says "start text" instead of "source text." Why? Because "source" implies some kind of pristine origin — like a river bubbling up from the earth. But any text may itself be made up of translations, reworked fragments, previous texts. And why should our terms reduce translation to just TWO sides (start and target)? Each target text is just a link toward further actions. Texts usually contain traces of more than one language and culture. So "start text" says something theoretical: it questions the assumption of a pure, singular source.

Compare with anuvad:

In Sanskrit and Hindi, the term for written translation basically means "saying later" or "repeating." The difference is in

time

, not space. That's a completely different theoretical scene than the Western "carry across" metaphor.

4. From Theories to Paradigms

A

paradigm

(after Kuhn, 1962) is a set of principles that underlies a group of theories — shared concepts, a shared point of departure, internal coherence. For example, theories that use the terms "source," "target," and "equivalence" form a paradigm. People within that paradigm can discuss translation productively with each other.

Problems arise when paradigms clash. Someone working in the equivalence paradigm and someone working descriptively (comparing translations to non-translations) will talk past each other. The equivalence theorist can calmly say "translations SHOULD be equivalent to their sources" — and the descriptive theorist's finding that translation language is different from non-translation language seems irrelevant. Each side continues without entertaining the other's perspective.

When paradigms clash, three things can happen: continued tension (debate without resolution), revolution (one wins out), or mutual ignorance (everyone goes their separate way). Pym's aim is to overcome the third option — to help people understand different paradigms even if they don't agree with them.

Pym's Six Paradigms

Pym organizes his whole book around paradigms, not individual theorists. The order is roughly chronological from the 1960s to today:

  • Equivalence (split into "natural" and "directional" — because equivalence isn't one simple thing)
  • Purposes (Skopos theory — the purpose of the translation determines the method)
  • Descriptions (descriptive translation studies — what translators actually do)
  • Uncertainty (deconstruction, etc. — present throughout, not a later development)
  • Localization (translating for specific markets, driven by technology)
  • Cultural translation (broader cultural and political dimensions)
Important: newer theories do NOT replace older ones. If they did, you'd only need to read the last chapter. But equivalence lives on within localization (in term bases, translation memories, CAT tools). Skopos theory can embed equivalence as appropriate for "special cases." The exception is uncertainty — which regards both equivalence and purpose as indefensible essentialisms. That's a real paradigm clash.

5. So Why Bother Studying Theory?

Here's the honest answer: there's

no empirical evidence

that a translator who knows theory works better. In fact, untrained translators might work faster — fewer doubts, less time reflecting on the obvious. So why study theory?

  • For novel problems: When there's no established solution and significant creativity is needed, theory can pose productive questions and suggest novel answers.
  • As an agent of change: Theories can challenge endemic thought. The simple idea of translation as "saying later" (anuvad) instead of "carrying across" changes how you think about what you're doing.
  • For the profession's image: Public theories show that translation is complex enough to be studied seriously at university — which helps the public image of translators and interpreters.
  • For arguments: When a client complains that the term "Tory" disappeared from your translation, you can say you achieved "compensatory correspondence" by comparing the British party with a target-culture party elsewhere. The client might not be entirely convinced, but they realize you have reasons.
  • For generating and selecting solutions: A plurality of theories widens the range of potential solutions you think of (generative side) and gives you reasons to choose among them (selective side). Pym thinks the selective side has become unfashionable and he wants to redress the balance.

6. How Should You Study Theory?

Pym is clear: never separate theory from practice. The best use of theory is in

active discussions about different ways of solving real translation problems

. His book is designed for this — each chapter ends with "Frequently Had Arguments" and "Projects and Activities" that have no correct solutions. The activities should come at the BEGINNING of a class, not as an appendage.

"The real learning of theory, even for the self-learner, should be in dialogue and debate."

A challenge Pym acknowledges: educational institutions tend to separate theory from practice, demanding a separate "translation theory" course. If that's necessary, fine — but the theories should still be drawn out from practical tasks, structured as discovery processes. Students should come to theory only when they find they actually need it.

Exam Q&A · Check Yourself

?Self-test · check yourself6 questions
01What does Pym mean by saying "translators are theorizing all the time"?
A: Every time a translator identifies a problem, generates possible solutions, and selects one, they bring ideas about what translation is into play. That internal generation-and-selection is theorizing — done in split seconds, whether you're trained or not.
02What's the difference between theorizing, theory, and paradigm?
A: Theorizing = the private, internal activity. It becomes public theory when there's disagreement about how to translate. A paradigm = a set of underlying principles shared by a group of theories, with a shared point of departure (from Kuhn).
03Why does Pym say "start text" instead of "source text"?
A: Because "source" implies a pristine, natural origin — like a river bubbling up. But any text may itself be built from translations and reworked fragments. Also, "start/target" still implies just two sides, but translation often involves more. Names-for-things are never neutral — they carry theoretical assumptions.
04What are Pym's six paradigms?
A: Equivalence (natural + directional), Purposes (Skopos), Descriptions (DTS), Uncertainty (deconstruction — present throughout history), Localization, and Cultural translation.
05What happens when paradigms clash? What outcomes are possible?
A: People use "translation" to mean different things and debate becomes pointless. Three possible outcomes: continued tension (endless debate), revolution (one wins), or mutual ignorance (they ignore each other). Pym aims to overcome mutual ignorance.
06Is there evidence that studying theory makes you a better translator?
A: No — there's "no empirical evidence" for that. But theory helps with problems that have no established solutions, gives you tools to defend and understand positions in arguments, raises the public image of the profession, and (with a plurality of theories) widens the range of solutions you consider and gives you reasons to choose among them.
