Study Guide · Susan Bassnett, Translation Studies, Ch. 3
Structures, the reader as translator, and the translation of poetry, prose and drama

1. The Central Argument

A literary text is "a complex set of systems existing in a dialectical relationship with other sets." Translators fail when they focus on one element at the expense of the

whole

. Every reading is an interpretation, so translation and interpretation CANNOT be separated. There is no single "right way" to translate a poem, and this applies equally to prose and dramatic texts.

Lefevere's seven strategies for translating poetry is the single most tested item. Also: the Catullus paradox (closer in form = further in function), Lotman's four reader positions, and the prose/drama sections (Belloc's six rules, the Racine examples).

2. Structures and the Reader

Cluysenaar:

The translator should work with "an eye on each individual structure." A literary text is a set of related systems — the dominant structure of each work needs description.

Lotman's four reader positions:

(1) reader focuses on content as matter; (2) reader grasps the complexity of the structure (ideal starting point for translation); (3) reader extrapolates one level for a specific purpose; (4) reader discovers elements not basic to the text and uses it for their own purposes.

The reader as translator:

Barthes makes the reader a producer, not consumer. Kristeva's intertextuality: every text is linked to all others ("all texts are translations of translations of translations" — Paz). The idea of a single "correct" reading dissolves, but this freedom must be handled responsibly. It's "foolish to argue that the translator should translate but not interpret," since

every reading is an interpretation

.

3. Lefevere's Seven Strategies for Translating Poetry

From studying translations of Catullus' Poem 64. Each strategy is deficient because it overemphasizes one element:

  1. Phonemic translation — reproduce SL sound. Works moderately for onomatopoeia, but usually clumsy and senseless.
  2. Literal translation — word-for-word. Distorts sense and syntax.
  3. Metrical translation — reproduce SL metre. Concentrates on one aspect at the expense of the whole.
  4. Poetry into prose — distorts sense, communicative value and syntax (but less than literal/metrical).
  5. Rhymed translation — "double bondage" of metre AND rhyme. Result: a "caricature" of Catullus.
  6. Blank verse translation — structural restrictions, but greater accuracy.
  7. Interpretation — versions (substance retained, form changed) and imitations (poem of translator's own, sharing only title/point of departure).
The deficiency in all seven: overemphasizing one element and failing to treat the poem as an organic structure. Popovic: the translator "has the right to differ organically, to be independent" — for the sake of the original.

4. Case Studies: Catullus 13 and The Seafarer

Catullus Poem 13 — the paradox:

Three English versions (Marris, Copley, Ben Jonson) share the invariant core (invitation, joky tone, plea of poverty). The closer a version tries to recreate the original's linguistic/formal structures (Marris' forced rhyme), the FURTHER it gets in function. Huge deviations (Ben Jonson's version) can come NEARER to the original's mood and tone — "giving it a new life in the context of Renaissance England."

Pound vs. Kennedy on The Seafarer:

Pound removes Christian references (

mid englum

= "among the angels" → "'mid the English"), focuses on the suffering individual, uses mock-Anglo-Saxon syntax and alliteration. Kennedy preserves the half-line format, uses 20th-century language, relates the poem to the contemporary reader. Both use the ST as a starting point to build a poem in its own right. The table comparing their criteria shows each operates on different levels.

Wyatt vs. Surrey translating Petrarch:

Wyatt's version of Petrarch's sonnet makes the Lover active and brave ("goode is the liff, ending faithfully") — reflecting Renaissance English individualism. Surrey's version retains military language but makes the Lover "captyve." The different socio-cultural contexts of Tudor England produced different poems from the same source. Both show translation as cultural negotiation.

5. Translating Prose

Far less studied than poetry translation, partly because of the "erroneous notion that a novel is somehow a simpler structure." Students often start translating without reading the whole novel — treating content as separable from form.

The Mann example:

The English translation of

The Magic Mountain

compresses sentences, removes stylistic registers, translates

einfacher

(ordinary) as "unassuming" — losing the novel's multi-level structure.

The Silone example:Fontamara

's five carefully structured opening sentences become four in English — the rhetorical pattern is lost.

Belloc's six rules for prose translation: (1) "Block out" — translate in sections, not word-by-word. (2) Render idiom by idiom — "By the Dog!" → "By God!" (3) Render intention by intention — weighting matters. (4) Beware false friends. (5) "Transmute boldly" — translation is "the resurrection of an alien thing in a native body." (6) Never embellish.

6. Translating Dramatic Texts

The most neglected area. A theatre text is incomplete — it's only realized in performance. The translator must decide: translate as a literary text, or as one element in the complex system of theatre?

Ubersfeld:

It's impossible to separate text from performance.

Corrigan:

The translator must "hear the voice that speaks" and consider the "gesture" of the language.

The Racine example:

Three translations of Phèdre's first line — "I have resolved, Theramenes, to go" (Cairncross — formal); "No, no, my friend, we're off" (Lowell — colloquial, performable); "No. No. I can't. I can't. How can I stay?" (Harrison — entirely restructured). Each reflects a different concept of theatre translation.

Ambrose Phillips (18th c.):

Adapted Racine's

Andromache

for English audiences — shortened, added scenes, gave it a happy ending. His criteria: playability, relationship to English stage conventions, clarity of character relationships.

Tony Harrison (20th c.):

His

Phaedra Britannica

replaced Greek myths with colonial India — "India put dark passions in my breast" instead of "Le ciel mit dans mon sein une flamme funeste."

Exam Q&A

?Self-test · check yourself5 questions
01Why must a literary text be treated as a whole?
A: It's a complex set of interrelated systems. Translators who focus on one element (content, metre, rhyme) produce an unbalanced "perceptual bad form."
02List Lefevere's seven strategies.
A: Phonemic, literal, metrical, poetry into prose, rhymed, blank verse, and interpretation (versions + imitations). Each fails by overemphasizing one element.
03What paradox emerges from the three versions of Catullus 13?
A: Closer in form (Marris) = further in function. Huge deviations (Ben Jonson) = nearer to the original's mood and tone. Shows form and function don't correlate linearly.
04State Belloc's six rules for prose translation.
A: (1) Block out; (2) Render idiom by idiom; (3) Render intention by intention; (4) Beware false friends; (5) Transmute boldly; (6) Never embellish.
05What's special about translating dramatic texts?
A: A theatre text is incomplete — realized only in performance. The translator must consider playability, gestural patterns, and the conventions of the target stage (Phillips' Racine, Harrison's Phaedra Britannica).
