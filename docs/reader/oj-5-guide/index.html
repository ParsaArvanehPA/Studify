Session 5: Investigating Translation Strategy in the News | Based on Claire Scammell, Translation Strategies in Global News (2018), Ch. 5 | MA Translation Studies

Session Overview

About this session

This chapter defines "strategy" as the book uses it, surveys the methods available for investigating translation strategy, and introduces the analytical tool used in the next session: Pedersen's (2005) taxonomy for rendering culture in subtitling — borrowed and adapted to examine how Reuters journalists handle culture-specific concepts.

1. Defining "Strategy": Global vs. Local

Chesterman's (1997) distinction

"Strategy" can refer either to the overall approach taken to translating a whole text (a "global" strategy), or to individual decisions made at word, phrase or sentence level to resolve "local problems" (a "local" strategy). Example: a client might commission a "creative" translation of a marketing text (global strategy = creative) while also specifying non-technical language for a key term (a local strategy applied wherever that term appears).

Davier's (2013) parallel:

macro-level domestication (e.g. "rewriting" a whole report) vs. micro-level domestication (e.g. "omission" of a single detail) — mirroring Chesterman's global/local split in a specifically news-translation context.

Lörscher (1991) — strategy as problem-solving

"A translation strategy is a potentially conscious procedure for the solution of a problem which an individual is faced with when translating a text segment from one language into another."

Like Chesterman, Lörscher's definition is exclusively about the local level, and emphasises that translators only apply a strategy when the text presents a genuine problem — echoing Pedersen's (2005) idea of "crisis points" where a subtitler must make a translation choice.

This book's usage: Reuters' current "global" approach to translation is simply referred to as its current "approach" (rather than "strategy"), since the limited guidance in the Handbook doesn't amount to an explicit, deliberate global strategy Reuters would necessarily recognise as such. "Strategy" is reserved for the local strategies used to translate foreign-language quotations and culture-specific concepts — the level Pedersen's taxonomy examines.

2. A Brief Survey of Methods

Method 1 — Text-based comparison

The most common method across translation studies: comparing a translated (target) text against its original (source) text, sometimes alongside documentation like the translation brief or a commissioner's style guide. Combined with staff interviews, this method has produced the field's key findings on agency translation practice (Bielsa and Bassnett 2009; Davier 2014) — including the finding (Session 3) that agency journalists don't recognise translation as separate from editing.

Method 2 — Think-aloud protocols (TAP)

Asking a translator to talk through their decision-making live while translating, then analysing the recording (Bernardini 2003; Lörscher 1992). No published research has used TAP specifically with news professionals — likely because TAP slows down the translation process considerably (Jääskeläinen 2010), and time-pressured news professionals are unlikely to have the time or willingness to complete one.

Method 3 — Textual analysis (the most common by far)

Used most widely to examine local translation strategies, often in relation to ideological issues like news framing. Also used to study "recontextualisation" (Session 3) — but existing categorisations of this process describe the general transformations that accompany translation, rather than the specific range of local strategies used.

3. Existing Categorisations of Intra-Lingual Change

Researcher(s)Categorisation
Bielsa and Bassnett (2009)Five types of "textual intervention" comparing an AFP Latin-America report against its Spanish-wire version — "the usual modifications of a translation that relies on a domesticating strategy which privileges fluency and transparency and conformance to the expectations of the target reader" (p. 101).
Hursti (2001)Distinguishes Editing (transforming language/structure via deletion, addition, substitution, reorganization) from Translation (rendering newsworthy parts of the original into the target language).
Franjié (2009)Analysing Arabic-to-French translations in Courrier International: omission, summarisation, and explicitation — intra-lingual editing practices occurring alongside translation.
Bani (2006)Analysing Italian translations in Internazionale: four most common strategies for rendering culture-specific information — cutting/summary, inclusion of explanations, generalization, and substitution.

The gap these approaches leave: all describe intra-lingual changes that happen around translation, not the local strategies used at the point of translation itself. Pedersen's (2005) taxonomy, developed for subtitling, is adopted precisely because it targets this gap.

4. Pedersen's Strategies for Rendering Culture

Extralinguistic Culture-bound References (ECRs)

"An Extralinguistic Culture-bound Reference (ECR) is defined as reference that is attempted by means of any culture-bound linguistic expression, which refers to an extralinguistic entity or process, and which is assumed to have a discourse referent that is identifiable to a relevant audience as this referent is within the encyclopedic knowledge of this audience." (Pedersen 2005, p. 2)

Example from subtitling: the American "Prison board" (a source-language ECR) replaced with the Swedish kriminalvårdsstyrelsen, naming a distinct Swedish institution (a target-language ECR). This is directly analogous to banlieue and its translation "suburb" — two terms designating two distinct realities.

Why Pedersen's taxonomy fits news translation:

(1) it deals specifically with strategies for rendering cultural specificity; (2) it places strategies along a "Venutian" scale of foreignising/domesticating; (3) subtitlers and news agency journalists share very similar constraints — space (subtitling: screen space; news: word/character limits) and time (subtitling: seconds on screen; news: tight deadlines, multiple stories per day).

The six strategies, from most foreignising (SL-oriented) to most domesticating (TL-oriented)

StrategyOrientationDescription
RetentionSL-oriented (most foreignising)Keeps the ECR in its untranslated form, with no added help for the reader.
SpecificationSL-orientedKeeps the ECR untranslated but makes it more specific, via explicitation (e.g. adding a first name) or addition (adding latent/connotative information, e.g. "the estates, as they are known").
Direct translationSL-oriented (can be "Calque" or "Shifted")"Nothing is added or subtracted. No effort made to transfer connotations or guide the TT audience." A Calque translation is literal/exotic-sounding; a Shifted translation is more unobtrusive.
GeneralizationTL-orientedReplaces an ECR referring to something specific with something more general (e.g. quartiers sensibles → "problem neighbourhoods").
SubstitutionTL-oriented (most domesticating)Removes the source ECR and replaces it with either a different (target-culture) ECR ("Cultural" substitution) or a paraphrase.
OmissionNeither (sits outside the scale)The ECR is simply removed without replacement — a standard, intra-lingual feature of "recontextualising" a story, so it is not counted as a translation strategy in this book's analysis.

A seventh strategy, "Official Equivalent," applies only when an ECR already has a recognised official translation (Pedersen's example: Donald Duck → the Swedish "Kalle Anka") — the subtitler/journalist has no real decision to make in that case.

5. Shared Constraints: Subtitling and News Agency Journalism

Both the subtitler and the news agency journalist face the dual constraints of space and time:

  • Space: the subtitler is limited by screen space; the news journalist by the norm of brevity (particularly in the lead paragraph) and the need for a report that can be read and understood quickly.
  • Time: the subtitler has limited time to spend per subtitle under tight deadlines and low pay; the news journalist must produce multiple reports a day and get stories onto the wire before competitors break the story.

Both practices are further shaped by national norms and audience expectations — subtitling norms and commissioning guidelines for the subtitler; newswriting conventions (e.g. the norm of using "said" as the reporting verb — Cotter 2010, p. 26) and agency-specific style guides for the journalist. These shared pressures both push toward translations that can be read and understood quickly — a domesticating tendency common to both fields.

✦ Why This Matters for a Translator

This is the chapter that gives you the actual analytical toolkit used in the rest of the book — worth learning precisely, since Session 6 applies it directly to real Reuters text.

  • Notice the taxonomy is a genuine scale, not three domesticating vs. three foreignising boxes: Retention is the most extreme foreignising choice, Substitution the most extreme domesticating one, with Specification, Direct Translation and Generalization occupying graduated middle positions.
  • Omission sitting outside the scale is a subtle but important point: removing information entirely isn't more or less "foreignising" — it just isn't a translation strategy for handling an ECR at all.
  • The subtitling/news parallel is not superficial — Pedersen's taxonomy transfers so well precisely because both fields share the same two structural constraints (space, time), which is exactly why domestication tends to dominate in both.

Why it matters for you: when you read the Reuters corpus results in Session 6, try to place each translation choice on this six-point scale yourself before checking the book's classification — that's the skill this course is ultimately training.

Review Questions

Q&A for Exam Preparation

?Self-test · check yourself8 questions
01Distinguish Chesterman's "global" and "local" translation strategies, with an example.
A: A global strategy is the overall approach taken to a whole text (e.g. a "creative" translation of a marketing text); a local strategy is an individual decision made at word/phrase/sentence level to resolve a specific problem (e.g. using non-technical language for one key term wherever it appears). A global strategy can specify constraints on the local strategies used within it.
02Why does this book avoid describing Reuters' overall approach to translation as a "global strategy"?
A: Because Chesterman notes that a "strategy" implies at least a potentially conscious choice, and the limited translation guidance in the Reuters Handbook doesn't constitute an explicit, deliberate global strategy that Reuters would necessarily recognise as such. The book therefore calls it Reuters' current "approach," reserving "strategy" for local-level choices.
03Why is think-aloud protocol (TAP) unlikely to be a useful method for studying news translation?
A: TAP requires a translator to narrate their decision-making live, which significantly slows down the translation process. Given the severe time pressure news professionals face (multiple stories a day, urgent deadlines), it is unlikely a professional would have the time or willingness to complete a think-aloud protocol.
04Define "Extralinguistic Culture-bound Reference" (ECR) and give the example used to illustrate it.
A: An ECR is a reference made via a culture-bound linguistic expression, referring to an extralinguistic entity or process assumed to be within the audience's encyclopedic (background) knowledge. Example: the American "Prison board" (source-language ECR) replaced with Swedish kriminalvårdsstyrelsen (target-language ECR) — two terms for two genuinely distinct institutions.
05List Pedersen's six main strategies for rendering ECRs, from most foreignising to most domesticating.
A: Retention (most SL/foreignising-oriented) → Specification → Direct Translation → Generalization → Substitution (most TL/domesticating-oriented), with Omission sitting outside the scale as neither.
06Why is "Omission" excluded from Pedersen's foreignising/domesticating scale?
A: Because omission simply removes the ECR without replacing it in any way — it is treated as a standard, intra-lingual feature of "recontextualising" a story for a new audience, rather than a strategy for actually rendering (translating) the culture-specific content.
07Why does Pedersen's taxonomy — developed for subtitling — transfer well to news translation research?
A: Because the subtitler and the news agency journalist face closely comparable constraints: both must work within tight space limits (screen space vs. word/character limits and newswriting brevity norms) and tight time limits (seconds on screen and low pay vs. urgent deadlines and multiple daily stories) — constraints that in both fields push practice toward domestication.
08What is the "Official Equivalent" strategy, and why is it treated as a special case?
A: It applies when an ECR already has a recognised, official translation in the target language/culture (Pedersen's example: Donald Duck → the Swedish "Kalle Anka"). It is a special case because the subtitler/journalist isn't really making a foreignising-vs-domesticating decision at all — the equivalent is simply given.

Key Points to Review

  • Chesterman: global strategy (whole-text approach) vs. local strategy (word/phrase-level problem-solving)
  • Lörscher: a translation strategy is a "potentially conscious procedure" for solving a specific translation problem
  • Methods surveyed: text-based comparison (most common), interviews, think-aloud protocol (rarely used in news), textual analysis (used most for local strategies)
  • Existing categorisations (Bielsa & Bassnett; Hursti; Franjié; Bani) describe intra-lingual change around translation, not local translation strategies themselves — the gap Pedersen fills
  • ECR (Extralinguistic Culture-bound Reference) — Pedersen's (2005) unit of analysis
  • Six strategies on the Venutian scale: Retention → Specification → Direct Translation → Generalization → Substitution; Omission sits outside the scale
  • Subtitling and news agency journalism share space and time constraints, both pushing toward domestication
