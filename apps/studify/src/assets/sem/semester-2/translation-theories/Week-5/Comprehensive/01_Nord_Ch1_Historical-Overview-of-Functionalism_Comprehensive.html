Functionalism: A Historical Overview

Study Guide · Christiane Nord, Translating as a Purposeful Activity, Ch. 1
From equivalence to function — the rise of the German functionalist school

1. Core Argument

Central thesis: Functionalism did not appear overnight. It grew out of dissatisfaction with equivalence-based linguistic approaches, especially among scholars in translator training who found that professional translating includes many cases where equivalence is not called for at all. The chapter sketches the 'German School': Reiss, Vermeer's Skopostheorie, Holz-Mänttäri's translational action, and functionalist methodology.
Know the shift from equivalence to function, and the four landmark contributions: Reiss (text typology + criticism), Vermeer (Skopos), Holz-Mänttäri (translational action) and the training scholars (Hönig & Kussmaul, Nord).

2. Early Functionalist Views

Function is old: Functional approaches were not invented in the 20th century — translators throughout history observed that different situations call for different renderings (Cicero, Jerome, Luther all reproduced form in some passages and sense in others). Nida's dynamic equivalence (1964) already emphasised communicative effect, and his 1976 framework stressed purpose: the key question is 'Best for whom?' — the relative adequacy of a translation depends on how well it fulfils its intended purpose.
Why the linguistic reading of Nida dominated
Linguistics was the dominant discipline of the 1950s–60s; early machine translation and structuralism nourished the idea that translation could be a strictly scientific, code-switching operation. So Nida's linguistic three-stage model was taken up more than his dynamic functionalism (Gentzler: Nida founded the 'science of translation').

3. The Limits of Equivalence

Equivalence approaches: Source-text oriented; the ST's features (content, style, function) must be preserved in the TT (Catford, Nida & Taber, Wilss, Koller)

Koller's normative statement: Any target text that is not equivalent 'as far as possible' is declared a non-translation
Why training scholars broke away: Scholars in training institutions looked at the profession they were training for and found many cases where equivalence is not wanted. Example: translating a British school certificate for a German university — the target text is not expected to look like or function as a German school certificate. Kelly: 'A translator moulds his image of translation by the function he assigns to language.' A new theory was called for.

4. Katharina Reiss: Text Typology and Translation Criticism

A functional category within equivalence: As early as 1971 Reiss introduced a functional category into her 'objective approach to translation criticism' — still within equivalence theory. The ideal translation achieves equivalence in conceptual content, linguistic form and communicative function ('integral communicative performance').
Exceptions from equivalence
Reiss recognised real-life exceptions, due to the translation brief (Übersetzungsauftrag): (1) when the TT has a different function from the original (e.g. a prose text adapted for the stage, or word-for-word versions for a poet); (2) when the TT addresses a different audience (e.g. Gulliver's Travels for children). She called these 'transfers' (Übertragungen), where the functional perspective takes precedence over equivalence.

5. Hans Vermeer: Skopostheorie and Beyond

Breaking with linguistics: Vermeer: 'Linguistics alone won't help us' — translating is not merely (or even primarily) a linguistic process. Translation is a type of human action, and action is intentional, purposeful behaviour in a situation embedded in a culture.
Skopos: Greek for 'purpose'/'aim'; the technical term for the purpose of a translation. The prime principle determining any translation process is the purpose of the overall translational action

Status of the source text: Much lower than in equivalence theories. For Reiss the ST is 'the measure of all things'; for Vermeer it is an 'offer of information' partly or wholly turned into an offer of information for the target audience
The addressee
One of the most important factors determining the Skopos is the addressee — the intended receiver with their culture-specific world-knowledge, expectations and needs. To translate is 'to produce a text in a target setting for a target purpose and target addressees in target circumstances'.

6. Holz-Mänttäri and Functionalist Training

Justa Holz-Mänttäri: theory of translational action
She goes further, even avoiding the word 'translation'. Translation is a complex action designed to achieve a particular purpose — the generic term is 'translational action' (translatorisches Handeln). Translators are experts who produce 'message transmitters' (Botschaftsträger) to coordinate communicative cooperation across culture barriers. She emphasises the roles of participants (initiator, translator, user, receiver) and situational conditions.
Functionalist methodology in training: Hönig & Kussmaul (Strategie der Übersetzung, 1982) show how functional strategies solve translation problems; their key maxim is the 'necessary degree of precision' — reproduce just those semantic features relevant to the function of your translation (echoing Grice). Nord adds a translation-oriented model of text analysis (extratextual + intratextual factors), comparing the Skopos with the source-text functions to locate problems before translating.
Common feature of the functionalist training scholars: unlike the linguistic theorists, they focus on language-independent pragmatic/cultural aspects, emphasising translation competence over language proficiency.

Exam Q&A · Check Yourself

Q: What dissatisfaction gave rise to functionalism?
A: Dissatisfaction with equivalence-based approaches among translator-training scholars, who found that professional practice includes many cases where equivalence is not wanted — e.g. a British school certificate translated for a German university is not expected to look or function like a German one.
Q: How did Reiss introduce a functional category, and what are her 'exceptions'?
A: Within an objective approach to translation criticism, she allowed exceptions from equivalence based on the translation brief: when the target text has a different function than the original, or addresses a different audience (e.g. Gulliver's Travels for children). She called these 'transfers'.
Q: Define Skopos and explain the status of the source text in Skopostheorie.
A: Skopos is the purpose of the translation — the prime principle determining the translation process. The source text's status is low: for Vermeer it is merely an 'offer of information' to be partly or wholly re-offered to the target audience.
Q: What is 'translational action' (Holz-Mänttäri)?
A: A complex, purpose-driven action in which translators, as experts, produce 'message transmitters' to coordinate communicative cooperation across language and culture barriers — emphasising participant roles and situational conditions.
Q: State Hönig & Kussmaul's key maxim.
A: The 'necessary degree of precision': reproduce just those semantic features that are relevant in the given context with regard to the function of your translation (in line with Grice's maxims).
