History of Translation Theory

Study Guide · Susan Bassnett, Translation Studies, Ch. 2
Lines of approach from the Romans to the Victorians

1. Core Argument

Central thesis: The study of translation is 'a vital part of literary and cultural history'. Rather than rigid periodization (which contradicts culture's dynamism, per Lotman), Bassnett traces recurring lines of approach — above all the word-for-word vs. sense-for-sense line — emerging again and again 'with different degrees of emphasis in accordance with differing concepts of language and communication.'
The chapter is a chronological survey. Know the recurring word/sense line, Steiner's four periods, the Roman view, Bible translation and the vernacular, Dryden/Dolet/Tytler, and the Romantic/Victorian shifts.

2. The Problem of Period Study

Steiner's four periods (After Babel)
(1) From Cicero and Horace to Tytler (1791) — 'immediate empirical focus' (theory grows directly from practice). (2) To Larbaud (1946) — theory and hermeneutic enquiry. (3) From the 1940s — machine translation, structural linguistics, communication theory. (4) From the early 1960s (coexisting with the third) — a reversion to hermeneutic, almost metaphysical enquiry. Bassnett notes the divisions are idiosyncratic — the first spans ~1700 years, the last two a mere thirty.
Against rigid periodization: Human culture is a dynamic system (Lotman); trying to fix stages within strict temporal boundaries contradicts that dynamism (e.g. the endless debate over whether Petrarch/Chaucer/Dante were medieval or Renaissance). Bassnett follows a loose chronology without clear-cut divisions.

3. The Romans

Translation and enrichment: Jacobsen claims (hyperbolically) that 'translation is a Roman invention'. Cicero and Horace discussed translation within the poet's two functions — acquiring/disseminating wisdom and the art of shaping a poem — and distinguished word-for-word from the interpretation that produces an aesthetically pleasing TL text. Roman translation enriched the target literature rather than merely copying.

4. Bible Translation and the Vernacular

The sixteenth-century aims: The Reformation was 'primarily a dispute between translators' (Cary). Sixteenth-century Bible translators' aims fall into three categories:
  • To clarify errors arising from inadequate manuscripts or incompetence
  • To produce an accessible, aesthetically satisfying vernacular style
  • To clarify points of dogma and reduce the metatext imposed on the laity
Luther, Erasmus, and the educative role
Luther used übersetzen and verdeutschen ('to Germanize') almost interchangeably: 'the grammar shall not rule over the meaning.' Erasmus wanted the plowman and weaver to sing Scripture. Earlier, King Alfred translated Latin texts to help England recover learning, rendering 'sometimes word by word, sometimes sense by sense' — the finished function determining the method.
Folena: vertical vs. horizontal translation: Vertical — into the vernacular from a prestige SL (e.g. Latin); horizontal — between languages of similar value (e.g. Provencal into Italian). In the medieval canon, imitatio was highly prized and originality was not

5. Early Theorists and the Renaissance

Dolet and Chapman
Etienne Dolet (executed for heresy) gave five principles (1540): understand the sense; know both languages perfectly; avoid word-for-word; use common speech; order words for the right tone. George Chapman, the great translator of Homer, added: avoid word-for-word; reach the 'spirit' of the original; base the work on sound scholarship. The translator seeks a 'transmigration' of the original as a skilled equal.
Renaissance affirmation of the present: A key characteristic is the affirmation of the present through contemporary idiom (North's Plutarch; Holland the 'translator general' rendering Roman terms as 'Lords', 'Parliament'). Wyatt and Surrey adapted Petrarch not to individual words but to the poem's function for its readers. Steiner: translation was the matiere premiere of the imagination — a primary, shaping activity, not a secondary one.

6. From the 17th Century to the Victorians

Denham, Cowley, Dryden, Pope: Denham: it is the translator's business to translate 'not Language into Language, but Poesie into Poesie', warning against literal translation of poetry. Cowley (the 'libertine' translators) 'took, left out and added what I please'. Dryden: the triad metaphrase / paraphrase / imitation, preferring paraphrase, using the translator-as-portrait-painter metaphor. Pope followed Dryden, keeping alive the 'fire' of the poem.
The eighteenth century and Goethe
The translator has a moral duty to the contemporary reader (Dr Johnson: 'the purpose of a writer is to be read'). Goethe proposed three epochs of translation: (1) acquainting us with the foreign on our own terms (Luther's Bible); (2) appropriation through substitution/reproduction (the French tradition); (3) the highest — aiming for perfect identity of ST and TT through a new 'manner' (Voss's Homer). Tytler (1791) gave the first systematic English study with his three principles.
Romanticism and after: Romanticism stressed imagination (Coleridge's Fancy vs Imagination; A. W. Schlegel: all speaking/writing is translation). Two conflicting tendencies: translation as a category of thought / creative genius, vs. the mechanical function of 'making known'. The idea that meaning lies below and between language led to a stress on untranslatability and, in the Victorians (Newman, Carlyle, Morris, Rossetti), to deliberate archaism and Schleiermacher's proposed separate 'sub-language' for translation — conveying the foreign through strangeness.

Exam Q&A · Check Yourself

Q: Why does Bassnett resist rigid 'periodization' of translation history?
A: Because human culture is a dynamic system (Lotman); fixing stages within strict temporal boundaries contradicts that dynamism (e.g. the futile debate over whether Petrarch or Dante were medieval or Renaissance). She traces recurring lines of approach instead.
Q: Summarise Steiner's four periods of translation literature.
A: (1) Cicero/Horace to Tytler (1791) — immediate empirical focus; (2) to Larbaud (1946) — theory and hermeneutic enquiry; (3) from the 1940s — machine translation and structural linguistics; (4) from the early 1960s — a return to hermeneutic, almost metaphysical enquiry.
Q: What were the three aims of sixteenth-century Bible translators?
A: To clarify errors from inadequate manuscripts; to produce an accessible, aesthetically satisfying vernacular style; and to clarify points of dogma, reducing the metatext imposed on the laity.
Q: Explain Folena's vertical vs. horizontal translation.
A: Vertical translation is into the vernacular from a source language of special prestige (e.g. Latin); horizontal translation is between languages of similar value (e.g. Provencal into Italian). In the medieval canon imitatio was prized over originality.
Q: Describe Goethe's three epochs of translation.
A: (1) Acquainting us with foreign countries on our own terms (Luther's Bible); (2) appropriation through substitution and reproduction (the French tradition); (3) the highest — aiming for perfect identity between ST and TT through a new fused 'manner' (Voss's Homer).
