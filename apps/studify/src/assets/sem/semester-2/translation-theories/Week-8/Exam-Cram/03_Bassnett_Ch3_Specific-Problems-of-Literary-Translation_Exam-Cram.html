⚡ Specific Problems of Literary Translation · EXAM CRAM

Susan Bassnett, Translation Studies, Ch. 3 | Essential Points Only

🎯 Core Argument

Central thesis: A literary text is 'a complex set of systems existing in a dialectical relationship with other sets'. Translators fail when they focus on one element at the expense of the whole. Through close analysis of examples, Bassnett shows that every reading is an interpretation, so translation and interpretation cannot be separated — and there is no single 'right way' to translate a poem.

📚 Key Terms & Concepts

Lotman's four reader positions: (1) reader focuses on content as matter; (2) reader grasps the complexity of the structure — the ideal starting point for translation; (3) reader deliberately extrapolates one level for a specific purpose; (4) reader discovers elements not basic to the genesis of the text and uses it for their own purposes

🗝️ Rapid Q&A

Q: Why does Bassnett insist a literary text must be treated as a whole?
A: Because it is a complex set of interrelated systems; translators who focus on one element (e.g. content, or metre, or rhyme) at the expense of others produce an unbalanced, 'perceptual bad form'. The ideal is to grasp and reproduce the dominant structure of the whole work.
Q: List Lefevere's seven strategies for translating poetry.
A: Phonemic, literal, metrical, poetry into prose, rhymed, blank verse, and interpretation (which covers 'versions' and 'imitations'). Each is deficient because it overemphasizes one element at the expense of the poem as an organic whole.
Q: What paradox emerges from the three versions of Catullus' Poem 13?
A: The closer a version tries to recreate the original's linguistic and formal structures (Marris), the further it moves in function; huge deviations of form (Ben Jonson) can come nearer to the original's mood and tone, giving it new life.
Q: Explain the claim that translation and interpretation cannot be separated.
A: Because every reading is an interpretation. The translator first reads/interprets in the SL and then decodes into the TL — doing more, not less, than an ordinary reader. So it is a fallacy to say the translator should translate 'what is there' without interpreting.
Q: How does intertextuality reframe the translator's task (Eagleton)?
A: It holds that every text is itself in a sense a translation — a set of transformations of preceding and surrounding texts. This frees the translator from rigid conventions to treat the source responsibly as the starting point for the metatext, or translation-reading.
🔥 Literary translation must serve the whole organic structure. Every reading is interpretation, so translation and interpretation are inseparable — and Lefevere's seven poetry strategies each fail by overemphasizing one element, proving there is no single right way to translate a poem.
