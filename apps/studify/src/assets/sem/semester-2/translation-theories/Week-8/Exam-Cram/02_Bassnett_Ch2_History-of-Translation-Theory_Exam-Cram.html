⚡ History of Translation Theory · EXAM CRAM

Susan Bassnett, Translation Studies, Ch. 2 | Essential Points Only

🎯 Core Argument

Central thesis: The study of translation is 'a vital part of literary and cultural history'. Rather than rigid periodization (which contradicts culture's dynamism, per Lotman), Bassnett traces recurring lines of approach — above all the word-for-word vs. sense-for-sense line — emerging again and again 'with different degrees of emphasis in accordance with differing concepts of language and communication.'

📚 Key Terms & Concepts

Folena: vertical vs. horizontal translation: Vertical — into the vernacular from a prestige SL (e.g. Latin); horizontal — between languages of similar value (e.g. Provencal into Italian). In the medieval canon, imitatio was highly prized and originality was not

🗝️ Rapid Q&A

Q: Why does Bassnett resist rigid 'periodization' of translation history?
A: Because human culture is a dynamic system (Lotman); fixing stages within strict temporal boundaries contradicts that dynamism (e.g. the futile debate over whether Petrarch or Dante were medieval or Renaissance). She traces recurring lines of approach instead.
Q: Summarise Steiner's four periods of translation literature.
A: (1) Cicero/Horace to Tytler (1791) — immediate empirical focus; (2) to Larbaud (1946) — theory and hermeneutic enquiry; (3) from the 1940s — machine translation and structural linguistics; (4) from the early 1960s — a return to hermeneutic, almost metaphysical enquiry.
Q: What were the three aims of sixteenth-century Bible translators?
A: To clarify errors from inadequate manuscripts; to produce an accessible, aesthetically satisfying vernacular style; and to clarify points of dogma, reducing the metatext imposed on the laity.
Q: Explain Folena's vertical vs. horizontal translation.
A: Vertical translation is into the vernacular from a source language of special prestige (e.g. Latin); horizontal translation is between languages of similar value (e.g. Provencal into Italian). In the medieval canon imitatio was prized over originality.
Q: Describe Goethe's three epochs of translation.
A: (1) Acquainting us with foreign countries on our own terms (Luther's Bible); (2) appropriation through substitution and reproduction (the French tradition); (3) the highest — aiming for perfect identity between ST and TT through a new fused 'manner' (Voss's Homer).
🔥 A history organised by recurring lines, above all word vs sense. Romans enriched via translation; the Reformation fought over the Bible and vernacular; Dryden's triad and Goethe's three epochs systematize; Romantics/Victorians turn to imagination, untranslatability and archaism.
