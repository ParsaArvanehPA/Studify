Central Issues in Translation Studies

Study Guide · Susan Bassnett, Translation Studies, Ch. 1
Language and culture, equivalence, loss and gain, untranslatability and visibility

1. Core Argument

Central thesis: Translation has a core of linguistic activity but 'belongs most properly to semiotics'. It cannot be separated from culture: 'language is the heart within the body of culture', and the translator who treats the text in isolation from its culture works 'at his or her peril'. The chapter surveys the field's central issues: equivalence, loss and gain, untranslatability, and visibility.
Know the language–culture link (Sapir–Whorf, Lotman), the types of equivalence (Popovic, Neubert), loss and gain, Catford's & Popovic's untranslatability, and Venuti's visibility/domestication–foreignization.

2. Language and Culture

Sapir, Whorf, Lotman
Sapir: 'language is a guide to social reality'; no two languages are similar enough to represent the same social reality. Whorf endorses this. Lotman: language is a primary modelling system, literature a secondary one — 'no language can exist unless it is steeped in the context of culture; and no culture can exist which does not have at its center the structure of natural language.'
Decoding and recoding: The translator operates criteria that transcend the purely linguistic, in a process of decoding and recoding (illustrated by Nida's model). Even 'simple' words are culture-bound: translating yes (French oui/si), hello (telephone vs face-to-face; Italian ciao), or butter vs Italian burro (different colour, use and status) shows each language represents a separate reality.

3. Problems of Equivalence

Idioms, puns and metaphor: Idioms are culture-bound: menare il can per l'aia ('lead the dog round the threshing floor') becomes 'to beat about the bush' — substituted on the basis of function, not words or image. Dagut: a metaphor is a 'semantic novelty' with no existing TL equivalence — it must be created, not found.
Popovic's four types of equivalence: (1) Linguistic (word-for-word); (2) Paradigmatic (grammar/expressive axis); (3) Stylistic/translational (functional equivalence, expressive identity); (4) Textual/syntagmatic (equivalence of form and shape)

Neubert's semiotic categories: Equivalence is a semiotic category with syntactic, semantic and pragmatic components, hierarchically ordered: semantic takes priority over syntactic, and pragmatic conditions both (e.g. porca Madonna → 'fucking hell')
The invariant core: If a dozen translators tackle one poem, they produce a dozen versions — yet all share Popovic's 'invariant core': stable, basic, constant semantic elements. Variants (transformations) change the expressive form but not the core. Equivalence should not be a search for sameness, which cannot exist even between two TL versions of the same text (Holmes calls seeking sameness 'perverse').

4. Loss and Gain

Not only loss: Once we accept that sameness cannot exist between two languages, we can consider both loss and gain. Too much time has been spent on what is lost; the translator can also enrich or clarify the source text. What is 'lost' in the SL context may be replaced in the TL context.
Nida's examples
Loss arises with terms/concepts absent from the TL: the Guaica language of Venezuela uses a trichotomous (good / bad / violating taboo) rather than dichotomous classification; the many terms for snow (Finnish), camel behaviour (Arabic), or bread (French) present, on one level, an untranslatable problem.

5. Untranslatability

Catford: linguistic untranslatability: No lexical or syntactic substitute exists in the TL for an SL item (but this can often be resolved by restructuring)

Catford: cultural untranslatability: The absence in the TL culture of a relevant situational feature (e.g. the different concepts of bathroom, or Danish Tak for mad)
Bassnett criticises Catford: he claims home and democracy are translatable, but home has associative meanings not caught by chez moi, and democracy means different things in 'the German Democratic Republic' vs 'the American Democratic Party'. Since language is the primary modelling system of a culture, cultural untranslatability must be implied in any translation. Popovic avoids separating the linguistic and cultural, defining two types without that split. Mounin: too much attention to untranslatability distracts from real problems — translation is a dialectic process, never completely finished but never wholly impossible.

6. Visibility, and Science vs. 'Secondary Activity'

Venuti: The Translator's Invisibility (1995)
In Anglo-American culture translators are rendered invisible: a translation is deemed acceptable when it reads fluently, creating an 'illusion of transparency'. Venuti (using Schleiermacher) sets foreignization above domestication/acculturation, arguing these are fundamentally ethical attitudes to foreign texts. Lefevere: translation is rewriting — most readers meet literature 'as rewritten by its rewriters'.
Not secondary, not merely 'science': The purpose of translation theory is to understand the processes of translation, not to provide norms for a 'perfect translation' (just as literary criticism does not produce the ultimate poem). The myth of translation as a secondary activity is dispelled once we see the translator as both receiver and emitter: Author – Text – Receiver = Translator – Text – Receiver. Theory and practice are indissolubly linked.

Exam Q&A · Check Yourself

Q: Why does Bassnett say the translator must not treat the text in isolation from its culture?
A: Because 'language is the heart within the body of culture' — translation belongs properly to semiotics, and meaning depends on extra-linguistic, cultural criteria. Ignoring the culture is like a surgeon operating on the heart while neglecting the surrounding body.
Q: What is Popovic's 'invariant core'?
A: The stable, basic, constant semantic elements shared by all translations of a single text. Variants (transformations) change the expressive form but not this core — so equivalence is not sameness but a dynamic relationship around the invariant.
Q: Distinguish Catford's two types of untranslatability and Bassnett's critique.
A: Linguistic untranslatability (no TL lexical/syntactic substitute) and cultural untranslatability (a situational feature absent from the TL culture). Bassnett argues Catford underestimates culture — e.g. home and democracy carry culture-specific meanings — so cultural untranslatability is implied in any translation.
Q: Explain 'loss and gain' in translation.
A: Since sameness between languages is impossible, translation involves both loss (e.g. concepts absent from the TL, as in Nida's Guaica example) and gain — the translator can enrich or clarify the source, and what is lost in the SL context may be replaced in the TL context.
Q: What does Venuti mean by the translator's 'invisibility'?
A: That Anglo-American culture prizes fluent, transparent translations that erase traces of foreign origin, rendering the translator invisible. Venuti advocates foreignization over domestication as a more ethical stance toward foreign texts.
