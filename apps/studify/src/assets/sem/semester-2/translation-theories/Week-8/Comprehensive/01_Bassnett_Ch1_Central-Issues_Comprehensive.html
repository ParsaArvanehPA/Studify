Central Issues in Translation Studies

Study Guide · Susan Bassnett, Translation Studies, Ch. 1
Language and culture, equivalence, loss and gain, untranslatability, and visibility

1. The Central Claim

Translation has a core of linguistic activity but "belongs most properly to semiotics." It cannot be separated from culture: "language is the heart within the body of culture." The translator who treats a text in isolation from its culture works "at his or her peril." This chapter surveys the central issues: language and culture, equivalence, loss and gain, untranslatability, and the translator's visibility.

2. Language and Culture — Inseparable

Sapir: "Language is a guide to social reality." No two languages are similar enough to represent the same social reality. Lotman: Language is a primary modelling system; literature/art are secondary modelling systems. "No language can exist unless it is steeped in the context of culture; and no culture can exist which does not have at its center the structure of natural language."

Concrete examples: Translating yes into French requires knowing when to use oui vs. si. Translating hello into Italian requires knowing that pronto is for the telephone and ciao is both greeting and goodbye. Translating butter into Italian as burro ignores that Italian butter is unsalted and used for cooking — different product, different cultural status, different function.

3. Problems of Equivalence

Popovic's four types: (1) Linguistic (word-for-word); (2) Paradigmatic (grammatical); (3) Stylistic/translational (functional equivalence aiming at expressive identity); (4) Textual/syntagmatic (equivalence of form and shape).
Neubert's semiotic categories: Equivalence is a semiotic category with three hierarchically ordered components: syntactic, semantic, and pragmatic. Pragmatic conditions and modifies the other two.
The invariant core (Popovic): All translations of a single work share an "invariant core" — stable, basic, constant semantic elements. Variants (transformations) change the expressive form but not this core. Equivalence should NOT be a search for sameness (Holmes calls seeking sameness "perverse").

4. Loss and Gain

Once you accept that sameness between languages is impossible, you can talk about both loss AND gain. Too much time has been spent on what is lost; translators can also enrich or clarify the source text. Nida's Guaica example (Venezuela — a trichotomous good/bad/taboo system instead of dichotomous good/bad) shows how different conceptual systems create challenges but also opportunities.

5. Untranslatability

Catford: Two types — Linguistic (no lexical/syntactic substitute in TL, e.g., German Um wieviel Uhr...) and Cultural (a situation absent from TL culture, e.g., different concepts of bathroom, Danish Tak for mad).
Bassnett criticizes Catford: home and democracy seem translatable but carry culture-specific meanings that can't be fully captured. "Home" ≠ "chez moi"; "democratic" means different things in "the American Democratic Party" vs. "the German Democratic Republic." Because language is the primary modelling system of culture, cultural untranslatability is implied in ANY translation. Popovic avoids the linguistic/cultural split, while Mounin argues too much attention to untranslatability distracts from real problems.
"Translation may always start with the clearest situations... But as it involves the consideration of a language in its entirety... there is no doubt that communication through translation can never be completely finished, which also demonstrates that it is never wholly impossible either." (Mounin)

6. Visibility — Venuti and Lefevere

Venuti (1995): Anglo-American culture makes translators invisible — translations are judged acceptable when they read fluently, creating an "illusion of transparency." Using Schleiermacher's distinction, Venuti advocates foreignization (preserving the foreignness of the source) over domestication (adapting to target norms) as a more ethical stance.
Lefevere (1992): Translation is rewriting. Most readers meet literature not as written by its authors but as rewritten by translators, anthologists, critics, editors. Translation should be at the core of literary and cultural studies, not at the margins.

7. Theory vs. Practice

The purpose of translation theory is to understand the processes of translation — NOT to provide norms for a "perfect translation" (just as literary criticism doesn't produce the ultimate poem). Translation is not a "secondary activity": the translator is both receiver and emitter: Author → Text → Receiver = Translator → Text → Receiver. Theory and practice are "indissolubly linked."
"Every text is unique and, at the same time, it is the translation of another text. No text is entirely original because language itself, in its essence, is already a translation." — Octavio Paz

Exam Q&A

Q: Why does Bassnett say translation cannot be separated from culture?
A: "Language is the heart within the body of culture." Translation belongs to semiotics, not just linguistics. The translator who ignores culture is like a surgeon operating on the heart while neglecting the surrounding body.
Q: What is Popovic's "invariant core"?
A: The stable, basic semantic elements shared by all translations of a single text. Variants change the expressive form but not this core. Equivalence is not sameness — it's a dynamic relationship around the invariant.
Q: Distinguish Catford's two types of untranslatability and Bassnett's critique.
A: Linguistic (no TL substitute) and Cultural (situation absent from TL culture). Bassnett: Catford underestimates culture — home and democracy carry culture-specific meanings, so cultural untranslatability is implied in any translation.
Q: What does Venuti mean by the translator's "invisibility"?
A: Anglo-American culture prizes fluent, transparent translations that erase foreign traces. Venuti advocates foreignization over domestication as a more ethical stance toward foreign texts and cultures.
