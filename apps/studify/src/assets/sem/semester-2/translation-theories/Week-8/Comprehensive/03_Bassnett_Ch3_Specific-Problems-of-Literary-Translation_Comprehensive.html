Specific Problems of Literary Translation

Study Guide · Susan Bassnett, Translation Studies, Ch. 3
Structures, the reader as translator, and the translation of poetry

1. Core Argument

Central thesis: A literary text is 'a complex set of systems existing in a dialectical relationship with other sets'. Translators fail when they focus on one element at the expense of the whole. Through close analysis of examples, Bassnett shows that every reading is an interpretation, so translation and interpretation cannot be separated — and there is no single 'right way' to translate a poem.
Know Cluysenaar on structure, Lotman's four reader positions, the 'reader as translator' / intertextuality argument, and above all Lefevere's seven strategies for translating poetry.

2. Structures and the Reader

Cluysenaar and structuralism
Anne Cluysenaar: the translator should not use general precepts but work with 'an eye on each individual structure'. A literary text is a set of related systems within larger systems (Scholes). Translators who ignore the relation of parts to the whole produce a 'perceptual bad form' — what is needed is 'a description of the dominant structure of every individual work'.
Lotman's four reader positions: (1) reader focuses on content as matter; (2) reader grasps the complexity of the structure — the ideal starting point for translation; (3) reader deliberately extrapolates one level for a specific purpose; (4) reader discovers elements not basic to the genesis of the text and uses it for their own purposes
The reader as translator: Twentieth-century study re-evaluated the reader: Barthes makes the reader a producer not consumer; Kristeva's intertextuality holds that every text is linked to all others ('all texts are translations of translations of translations', Paz). So the idea of a single 'correct' reading dissolves — but this freedom 'must be handled responsibly'. It is 'foolish to argue that the translator should translate but not interpret', since every reading is an interpretation.

3. Poetry and Translation: Lefevere's Seven Strategies

Seven strategies (from translations of Catullus' Poem 64): Andre Lefevere catalogues seven strategies English translators used — each deficient because it overemphasizes one element at the expense of the whole:
  • Phonemic translation — reproduce the SL sound while paraphrasing the sense; works moderately for onomatopoeia but is usually clumsy and often senseless
  • Literal translation — word-for-word emphasis distorts sense and syntax
  • Metrical translation — reproduce the SL metre; concentrates on one aspect at the expense of the whole
  • Poetry into prose — distorts sense, communicative value and syntax (though less than literal/metrical)
  • Rhymed translation — the translator enters a 'double bondage' of metre and rhyme; result is a mere 'caricature'
  • Blank verse translation — structural restrictions, but greater accuracy and higher literalness
  • Interpretation — versions (substance retained, form changed) and imitations (a poem of the translator's own, sharing only title/point of departure)
Lefevere's finding confirms Cluysenaar: deficiencies come from overemphasizing one element and failing to treat the poem as an organic structure. Popovic: the translator 'has the right to differ organically, to be independent' — for the sake of the original, to reproduce it as a living work.

4. Case Studies: Catullus and The Seafarer

Catullus Poem 13
Three versions (Marris, Copley, Ben Jonson) share Popovic's invariant core (the invitation, the joky tone, the plea of poverty). The paradox: the closer a version tries to recreate the linguistic/formal structures (Marris' rhymed version), the further it gets in function; huge deviations of form (Jonson) can come nearer to the original's mood and tone — giving it 'a new life in the context of Renaissance England'.
Pound vs. Kennedy on The Seafarer
Pound removes all Christian references (mid englum 'among the angels' → 'mid the English'; eorl 'man' → the specific 'earl'), focusing on the suffering of a great individual and reproducing Anglo-Saxon sound/alliteration and archaism. Kennedy preserves the visual half-line format and regular stress, uses 20th-century language, and relates the Anglo-Saxon world to the contemporary reader. Both use the SL text as a starting point to build a poem in its own right.

5. Interpretation, Intertextuality and the Metatext

Translation and interpretation are inseparable: Since every reading is an interpretation, translation and interpretation cannot be separated (Holmes' diagram places verse translation where interpretation intersects with imitation and derivation). A translator keeps producing new versions not to reach a 'perfect' translation but because each version is context-bound — a reading accessible to its own time. 'There is only one Divina Commedia but... innumerable translations.'
Intertextuality (Eagleton, Rifaterre)
Eagleton: a gain of semiotic enquiry is that every text is in a sense a translation — 'a set of determinate transformations of other, preceding and surrounding texts'. This frees the translator from prevailing conventions to treat the text responsibly as the starting point for the metatext (the translation-reading). The Ungaretti case shows even spatial arrangement and syntax (normalcy vs 'making strange', ostranenie) are meaningful choices.

Exam Q&A · Check Yourself

Q: Why does Bassnett insist a literary text must be treated as a whole?
A: Because it is a complex set of interrelated systems; translators who focus on one element (e.g. content, or metre, or rhyme) at the expense of others produce an unbalanced, 'perceptual bad form'. The ideal is to grasp and reproduce the dominant structure of the whole work.
Q: List Lefevere's seven strategies for translating poetry.
A: Phonemic, literal, metrical, poetry into prose, rhymed, blank verse, and interpretation (which covers 'versions' and 'imitations'). Each is deficient because it overemphasizes one element at the expense of the poem as an organic whole.
Q: What paradox emerges from the three versions of Catullus' Poem 13?
A: The closer a version tries to recreate the original's linguistic and formal structures (Marris), the further it moves in function; huge deviations of form (Ben Jonson) can come nearer to the original's mood and tone, giving it new life.
Q: Explain the claim that translation and interpretation cannot be separated.
A: Because every reading is an interpretation. The translator first reads/interprets in the SL and then decodes into the TL — doing more, not less, than an ordinary reader. So it is a fallacy to say the translator should translate 'what is there' without interpreting.
Q: How does intertextuality reframe the translator's task (Eagleton)?
A: It holds that every text is itself in a sense a translation — a set of transformations of preceding and surrounding texts. This frees the translator from rigid conventions to treat the source responsibly as the starting point for the metatext, or translation-reading.
