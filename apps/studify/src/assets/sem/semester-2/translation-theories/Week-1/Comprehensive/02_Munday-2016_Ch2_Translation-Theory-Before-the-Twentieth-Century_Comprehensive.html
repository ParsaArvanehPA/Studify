Translation Theory Before the Twentieth Century

Study Guide · Jeremy Munday, Introducing Translation Studies (2016), Ch. 2
The word-for-word vs. sense-for-sense debate, from Cicero to Schleiermacher

1. Core Argument

Central thesis: For nearly two thousand years, Western translation theory was dominated by one recurring debate: 'word-for-word' (literal) vs. 'sense-for-sense' (free) translation. Newmark called this the 'pre-linguistics period'; Steiner called the triad literalism/paraphrase/imitation a 'sterile' debate whose terms were vague and subjective.
The single most examined thread is the literal–free (word/sense) debate and the figures who reformulated it: Cicero, Jerome, Luther, Dryden, Dolet, Tytler, Schleiermacher (plus the non-Western Chinese/Arabic traditions and Yan Fu).

2. The Foundational Poles: Cicero and St Jerome

Cicero (106–43 BCE)
In De optimo genere oratorum he says he translated 'not as an interpreter, but as an orator' — keeping the ideas and forms but not rendering word for word, preserving 'the general style and force of the language'. The 'interpreter' = the literal translator; the 'orator' = the one who moves the audience.
St Jerome (347–420 CE)
The most famous of Western translators; produced the Latin Vulgate. In his letter to Pammachius he makes the most famous statement on translation: 'I render not word-for-word, but sense-for-sense' — except in Holy Scripture, 'where even the syntax contains a mystery.' He uses the image of the ST being marched into the TL like a prisoner by its conqueror.
Why it matters: In these two poles lie the origins of both the literal vs. free and the form vs. content debates. Jerome already distinguishes text types: he translated a letter idiomatically but insisted the Bible needed a more literal method.

3. Non-Western Traditions: China and the Arab World

Chinese sutra translation
Three phases (Hung & Pollard): an early word-for-word phase adhering to source syntax; a swing towards yiyi (free translation, e.g. Kumārajīva); then Xuan Zang's balanced approach matching polish to text type. Dao'an listed five 'losses' (shiben) and three 'difficulties' (buyi) in translating into Chinese.
Arabic (Abbasid Baghdad, 750–1250)
Two methods (Rosenthal): the first (Ibn al-Batriq) was highly literal, word-by-word, borrowing Greek where no Arabic word existed; the second (Ibn Ishaq) was sense-for-sense, creating fluent target texts. Gutas rejects a simple chronological account, stressing social, political and ideological factors and different 'translation complexes'.
The point Munday makes: the literal/free poles recur across rich, ancient non-Western traditions too — but the Western terminology can distort them.

4. The Reformation: Luther and the Vernacular

Language as a power struggle: Latin, controlled by Rome, held a stranglehold on knowledge until the Humanist movement and the Protestant Reformation challenged it through vernacular Bible translation. Translation could be deemed heretical: Tyndale was executed (1536); Dolet was burned at the stake (1546).
Martin Luther (1483–1546)
His East Central German New Testament (1522) and Old Testament (1534) helped standardize German. Defending himself in the Sendbrief vom Dolmetschen (1530), he justified adding allein ('only') to Romans 3:28 as 'pure, clear German'. Famous maxim: look at the mouths of 'the mother at home, the children in the street, the ordinary man in the market' and translate that way.

5. Fidelity, Spirit and Truth

Amos vs. Rener; Kelly
Flora Amos saw early theory as unconnected prefaces with no orderly progression; terms like 'faithfulness' meant different things to different writers. Louis Kelly (The True Interpreter) traces the 'inextricably tangled' terms fidelity, spirit and truth: spiritus meant creative energy but also the Holy Spirit; veritas only became fully equated with 'content' by the 12th century.

6. Early Systematic Theories: Dryden, Dolet, Tytler, Yan Fu

Dryden's triad (1680): John Dryden reduced all translation to three categories, a hugely influential 'triadic' model:
  • Metaphrase — 'word by word and line by line' (= literal). Dismissed: 'dancing on ropes with fettered legs'
  • Paraphrase — 'translation with latitude', the author kept in view but words not strictly followed (= sense-for-sense). Dryden's preferred middle path
  • Imitation — forsaking both words and sense (= Cowley's very free method, close to adaptation)
Dolet's 5 principles (1540): understand the sense and material; know both SL and TL perfectly; avoid word-for-word; avoid Latinate/unusual forms; assemble words eloquently for the right tone

Tytler's 3 laws (1790): the translation should give a complete transcript of the ideas; the style and manner should match the original; the translation should have all the ease of the original composition
Both Dolet and Tytler rank their principles/laws — an early anticipation of the modern discussion of translation 'loss' and hierarchy of priorities. Tytler is the first comprehensive, systematic study in English; unlike Dryden's author-orientation, he defines a good translation by its effect on the TL reader.
Yan Fu (1854–1921)
The Chinese thinker's three principles — xin (fidelity), da (fluency/expressiveness), ya (elegance) — became central to 20th-century Chinese translation theory. He generally placed xin above da, but often privileged ya.

7. Schleiermacher and the Valorization of the Foreign

Schleiermacher (1813): In 'On the different methods of translating' he first distinguishes the Dolmetscher (commercial translator) from the Übersetzer (scholarly/artistic translator). His famous claim: there are only two paths — 'Either the translator leaves the writer in peace as much as possible and moves the reader toward him, or he leaves the reader in peace and moves the writer toward him.'
Preferred strategy: Move the reader toward the writer — an 'alienating' / 'foreignizing' method that bends TL usage to preserve the foreignness of the ST. This requires a special 'language of translation'.
Huge influence
Schleiermacher's alienating/naturalizing opposition is taken up by Venuti as foreignization/domestication (Ch. 9); his 'language of translation' informs Walter Benjamin; his hermeneutics informs Steiner's 'hermeneutic motion' (Ch. 10).

Exam Q&A · Check Yourself

Q: State the central debate of pre-20th-century translation theory and its two founding figures.
A: The word-for-word (literal) vs. sense-for-sense (free) debate. It goes back to Cicero (translate as an orator, not an interpreter) and St Jerome ('sense-for-sense, except in Holy Scripture where the syntax contains a mystery').
Q: Why was Jerome's exception for Scripture important?
A: It showed an early awareness of text type: he translated ordinary letters sense-for-sense but insisted sacred texts needed a more literal method, because altering the sense of a sacred text risked a charge of heresy.
Q: Explain Dryden's triadic model and which he preferred.
A: Metaphrase (word-for-word), paraphrase (sense-for-sense, author kept in view) and imitation (forsaking words and sense). Dryden preferred paraphrase as the balanced path, rejecting the two extremes.
Q: How do Dolet's principles and Tytler's laws advance translation theory?
A: They are early prescriptive, systematic statements, and both rank their principles in order of importance — anticipating the modern idea of a hierarchy of priorities and translation 'loss'. Tytler also shifts focus to the effect on the TL reader.
Q: Summarise Schleiermacher's two methods and his preference.
A: Either move the reader toward the writer (foreignizing/alienating) or move the writer toward the reader (naturalizing/domesticating). He prefers foreignizing, valuing the foreign and bending the TL — later taken up by Venuti as foreignization vs domestication.
