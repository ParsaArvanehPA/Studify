Translation Theory Before the Twentieth Century

Study Guide · Jeremy Munday, Introducing Translation Studies (2016), Ch. 2
Two thousand years of the literal vs. free debate — from Cicero to Schleiermacher

1. The Big Picture: One Debate, Two Thousand Years

Here's the thing: For nearly 2,000 years, Western translation theory was basically stuck on ONE debate: should you translate word-for-word (literally) or sense-for-sense (freely)? Newmark calls this the "pre-linguistics period" of translation. Steiner calls the debate "sterile" — the terms were vague, nobody agreed on what they meant, and people kept repeating the same arguments. But you still need to know who said what, because this debate shaped everything that came after.
The exam loves this chapter. Know the chain: Cicero → Jerome → Luther → Dryden → Dolet → Tytler → Schleiermacher. And don't forget the non-Western traditions (Chinese sutra translation, Arabic Abbasid translation).

2. Where It All Started: Cicero and St Jerome

Cicero (106-43 BCE): In *De optimo genere oratorum*, he says he translated Greek speeches "not as an interpreter, but as an orator" — keeping the ideas and forms but NOT rendering word for word. The "interpreter" was the literal translator; the "orator" moved the audience. This is the earliest surviving statement of the distinction.
St Jerome (347-420 CE): The most famous translator of the ancient world. He produced the Latin Vulgate (the standard Latin Bible). In his letter to Pammachius (395 CE), he makes the most famous statement in translation history: "I render not word-for-word, but sense-for-sense" — EXCEPT in Holy Scripture, "where even the syntax contains a mystery." So even Jerome recognized that different text types need different approaches. Key image: the original text being "marched into the TL like a prisoner by its conqueror."
In these two guys lie the origins of both the literal vs. free AND the form vs. content debates. Jerome already distinguished text types — he translated a private letter freely but insisted the Bible needed more literal treatment because its sacredness meant you couldn't risk changing the sense.

3. Non-Western Traditions (Yes, You Need to Know These)

Chinese Sutra Translation

Buddhist texts were translated from Sanskrit into Chinese over centuries, and the debate played out there too:

  • Phase 1 (148-265 CE) — extremely literal, word-for-word, because they believed "sacred words shouldn't be tampered with." Result: almost incomprehensible.
  • Phase 2 (265-589 CE) — swung to yiyi ("free translation"). Kumārajīva pioneered smoothing out syntax and polishing to high literary quality.
  • Phase 3 (589-1100 CE) — balanced approach by Xuan Zang: match the style to the text type. He set rules for using transliteration.
Dao'an (312-385 CE) listed five "losses" (shiben) in translation — changes in word order, adapting to Chinese elegance, cutting repetitive exclamations, reducing commentaries, restructuring for logical flow — and three "difficulties" (buyi) — directing to a new audience, the sanctity of ST words, and the ST's special status as collective work. This is probably the earliest systematic thinking about translation problems.

Arabic Translation in Abbasid Baghdad (750-1250 CE)

The Bayt al-Hikma (House of Wisdom) in Baghdad was a massive translation centre for Greek science and philosophy:

  • First method (Ibn al-Batriq) — highly literal, translating each Greek word with an Arabic equivalent or borrowing.
  • Second method (Ibn Ishaq) — sense-for-sense, creating fluent target texts.

But Gutas warns against a simple chronological explanation: different "translation complexes" (groups of translators and patrons) worked independently on different texts (medicine, philosophy, mathematics), each with different goals.

4. The Reformation: Luther and the Vernacular Bible

Translation became a matter of life and death. William Tyndale was executed (1536) for translating the Bible into English. Etienne Dolet was burned at the stake (1546) for adding "nothing at all" in a translation that was deemed heretical. Translation wasn't an academic exercise — it was a battlefield.
Martin Luther (1483-1546): His German New Testament (1522) and complete Bible (1534) weren't just translations — they helped standardize the German language itself. When criticized for adding "allein" (alone/only) to Romans 3:28, he defended himself in the *Sendbrief vom Dolmetschen* (1530): he was translating into "pure, clear German." His famous advice: "You must ask the mother at home, the children in the street, the ordinary man in the market and look at their mouths, how they speak, and translate that way."

5. Fidelity, Spirit and Truth — The Vague Vocabulary

Louis Kelly (1979): The terms "fidelity," "spirit," and "truth" were "inextricably tangled." "Fidelity" originally meant literal word-for-word translation (the fidus interpres). Only by the end of the 17th century did it come to mean faithfulness to the meaning. "Spirit" could mean creative energy or the Holy Spirit, depending on who was using it. "Truth" only became fully equated with "content" in the 12th century. So when you read old translation prefaces, they may not mean what you think they mean.

6. The First Systematic Theories: Dryden, Dolet, Tytler, Yan Fu

Dryden's Triad (1680) — HUGELY INFLUENTIAL

John Dryden reduced all translation to three categories, and this model was used for centuries:
  • Metaphrase — word-for-word, line-by-line. Dryden called this "dancing on ropes with fettered legs."
  • Paraphrase — "translation with latitude." The author is kept in view but the words aren't strictly followed. Dryden's preferred middle path.
  • Imitation — the translator abandons both words and sense. Essentially adaptation. Dryden thought this did "the greatest wrong to the memory of the dead."
Dolet's 5 Principles (1540): (1) Understand the sense and material of the original. (2) Know both SL and TL perfectly. (3) Avoid word-for-word rendering. (4) Avoid Latinate/unusual forms — use common speech. (5) Choose and order words for the right tone. — These are ranked in order of importance, which is itself a theoretical move.
Tytler's 3 Laws (1790): (1) Complete transcript of the ideas. (2) Style and manner should match the original. (3) The translation should have all the ease of the original composition. — Tytler ranks these too: sacrifice ease before manner, and manner before meaning. His definition of a good translation is oriented toward the TL reader (unlike Dryden's author-orientation).
Yan Fu (1854-1921): The Chinese thinker whose three principles — xin (fidelity/faithfulness), da (fluency/expressiveness), ya (elegance/gracefulness) — became central to 20th-century Chinese translation theory. He generally prioritized xin over da, but often violated his own hierarchy by privileging ya (elegance).

7. Schleiermacher: The Game Changer

Friedrich Schleiermacher (1813) moved beyond the old literal/free binary. He said there are only TWO paths for the "true" translator:
"Either the translator leaves the writer in peace as much as possible and moves the reader toward him, OR he leaves the reader in peace as much as possible and moves the writer toward him."

Schleiermacher's preferred path: move the reader toward the writer. This means an "alienating" or "foreignizing" method — bending the target language to preserve the foreignness of the original. It might require creating a special "language of translation." This is the seed of everything Venuti later called "foreignization" vs. "domestication" (see Ch. 9 of Munday).

Schleiermacher also distinguished two types of translator: the Dolmetscher (for commercial texts) and the Übersetzer (for scholarly/artistic texts). The second type, he thought, operates on a higher creative plane.

8. The Case Studies: How This Old Vocabulary STILL Persists

Munday closes the chapter with two case studies that prove the literal/free vocabulary is STILL ALIVE in modern translation assessment. This is really useful for exams — it shows you the historical debate isn't dead.

Case Study 1: CIoL & UNESCO Assessment Criteria

Even in supposedly "scientific" assessment criteria from the Chartered Institute of Linguists and UNESCO, the old vague vocabulary keeps creeping in:

  • "Accuracy" is the modern version of "faithfulness" and "truth"
  • "Literal" is used as a CRITICISM — but qualified with "too" or "totally" (suggesting that "literal" alone is no longer the extreme; it's now seen as a scale)
  • UNESCO's guidelines say translation is like "tightrope walking" — almost identical to Dryden's "dancing on ropes" simile from 300 years earlier
  • Different text types get different treatment: periodical articles should be "readable," politically sensitive speeches need "very close translation"

Case Study 2: The Proust Translations

The most interesting example: Proust's À la recherche du temps perdu has been translated multiple times. The different prefaces show the literal/free debate in action:

  • Kilmartin (1981 revision): Criticizes the original Scott Moncrieff translation for being too literal — "a whiff of Gallicism" in the English. He prefers a naturalizing English style. Uses words like "loyalty" and "faithful" — straight out of the 17th century.
  • Prendergast (2002 new translation): Takes the opposite view. He argues that "oddly unEnglish shapes" may be the best way to preserve Proust's "estranging force." This is Schleiermacher's foreignizing approach, 200 years later.
  • Davis (2002 translator): Says she aimed to reproduce Proust's word order, syntax, punctuation — even "the rhythms of a sentence."
This is a great example to drop in an essay: the literal/free debate didn't end in the 19th century. It's alive in every new translation of a classic text.

Exam Q&A · Check Yourself

Q: What was the central debate of pre-20th-century translation theory, and who set up the two poles?
A: The word-for-word (literal) vs. sense-for-sense (free) debate. Cicero set up the distinction (translate as an orator, not an interpreter) and St Jerome made it famous ("sense-for-sense, except in Holy Scripture where the syntax contains a mystery").
Q: Why was Jerome's exception for Scripture important?
A: It shows an early awareness of text type differentiation — ordinary letters could be translated freely, but sacred texts needed a more literal approach because altering their sense risked heresy (and Jerome knew this from personal experience with controversy).
Q: Explain Dryden's triad and which one he preferred.
A: Metaphrase (word-for-word, "dancing on ropes with fettered legs"), Paraphrase (sense-for-sense, author kept in view), Imitation (abandoning both words and sense). He preferred the middle path of paraphrase, avoiding the two extremes.
Q: What's significant about Dolet's and Tytler's approaches?
A: They were the first to produce systematic, ranked principles — a hierarchy of priorities. Dolet had five principles in order of importance; Tytler had three laws ranked so you know what to sacrifice first (ease → manner → meaning). This anticipates modern discussions of translation "loss."
Q: What were Schleiermacher's two methods and which did he favour?
A: Either move the reader toward the writer (foreignizing/alienating — his preference) or move the writer toward the reader (naturalizing/domesticating). His alienating method influenced Venuti's foreignization concept centuries later.
Q: How do the Proust translations show the old debate is still alive?
A: Kilmartin (1981) criticized Scott Moncrieff for being too literal and favoured a naturalizing approach. Prendergast (2002) argued the opposite — that "oddly unEnglish shapes" might preserve Proust's effect better. That's Schleiermacher's foreignizing vs. naturalizing debate playing out in the 21st century.

What to Absolutely Know for the Exam

  • The literal/free debate IS the backbone of pre-20th-century theory
  • Cicero = orator vs. interpreter; Jerome = sense-for-sense except Scripture
  • Chinese and Arabic traditions had the same debate, independently
  • Luther = vernacular Bible, language of the common people
  • Dryden's triad: metaphrase / paraphrase / imitation
  • Dolet and Tytler gave the first ranked, systematic principles
  • Schleiermacher: foreignizing (move reader to writer) was his preference
  • Yan Fu: xin, da, ya
  • The Proust translations prove the debate is LIVE, not historical
    • 🧠 Bottom line: Everything orbits the literal/free (word/sense) debate. Cicero & Jerome set the poles; Luther vitalized the vernacular; Dryden's triad and the systematizers (Dolet/Tytler) organized it; Schleiermacher championed the foreign. And the Proust case shows it's NOT dead.
