Main Issues of Translation Studies

Study Guide · Jeremy Munday, Introducing Translation Studies (2016), Ch. 1
Defining translation, the birth of a discipline, and the Holmes/Toury map

1. Core Argument

Central thesis: The practice of translation is ancient, but translation studies as an academic discipline is young — emerging only in the second half of the 20th century. The chapter defines what translation is, sketches the field's early history, and presents the maps (Holmes/Toury and van Doorslaer) that organise it.
Know the three meanings of 'translation' (field, product, process), Jakobson's three categories, and every branch of the Holmes/Toury map. These are the most examined items in the chapter.

2. What Is Translation? Key Definitions

Translation (3 senses): (1) the general subject field/phenomenon; (2) the product (the translated text); (3) the process of producing it (translating)

ST / SL: Source text / source language — what you translate from

TT / TL: Target text / target language — what you translate into

Interpreting: Oral translation; Otto Kade: the SL text is presented only once and cannot be reviewed, and the TL text is produced under time pressure with little chance for correction

Jakobson's Three Categories of Translation (1959)

  • Intralingual translation ('rewording') — interpretation of verbal signs by other signs of the same language (e.g. a summary, a children's version)
  • Interlingual translation ('translation proper') — interpretation of verbal signs by means of some other language; the traditional focus of the field
  • Intersemiotic translation ('transmutation') — interpretation of verbal signs by signs of non-verbal sign systems (e.g. novel → film, music, painting)
Semiotics underpins Jakobson
These definitions draw on semiotics, the general science of communication through signs. Translation is thus not limited to verbal language.
Broadening the concept: Internationalization complicates the neat ST→TT model: some texts have no single fixed source (multilingual EU law; constantly updated websites). Halverson: translation is best seen as a prototype classification — core features plus peripheral forms (adaptation, version, transcreation, localization). Tymoczko shows non-Western metaphors for translation (Bengali rupantar 'change of form', Hindi anuvad 'saying after', Arabic tarjama, Chinese fan yi 'turning over') that do not prize close lexical fidelity.

3. The Birth of the Discipline

James S. Holmes: The name 'translation studies' is owed to the US-based scholar James S. Holmes. His 1972 paper 'The name and nature of translation studies' (published 1988) is 'generally accepted as the founding statement for the field' (Gentzler). Holmes urged the field to forge its own communication channels across older disciplines.

Before the discipline: translation as a poor relation

  • Grammar-translation method — from the late 18th c. to the 1960s, language learning centred on rote translation of decontextualized sentences; this lowered translation's academic status
  • The communicative approach and direct method (1960s–70s) abandoned translation in language teaching
  • Translation workshops (1960s USA, Iowa/Princeton) and comparative literature promoted literary translation
  • Contrastive linguistics (from the 1930s USA) used translated data — e.g. Vinay & Darbelnet (1958), Catford (1965)
The 'scientific' turn
More systematic linguistic approaches emerged in the 1950s–60s: Fedorov, Vinay & Darbelnet (1958), Mounin (1963), and Nida's Toward a Science of Translating (1964). The German Übersetzungswissenschaft (translation science) was taken up by Wilss, Koller and the Leipzig School (Kade, Neubert).

4. The Holmes/Toury Map

Structure: Holmes divides translation studies into 'pure' and 'applied' branches. The pure branch splits into theoretical and descriptive.

Pure — Theoretical

  • General theories — seek to describe/account for every type of translation (e.g. Toury's 'laws')
  • Partial theories — restricted by: medium (machine vs human, written vs spoken), area (languages/cultures), rank (word/sentence/text), text-type (literary, technical), time, and problem (e.g. equivalence)

Pure — Descriptive (DTS)

  • Product-oriented — examines existing translations (single ST–TT pairs or comparative; diachronic or synchronic)
  • Function-oriented — the function of translations in the recipient socioculture ('a study of contexts rather than texts'); today's sociology/historiography of translation
  • Process-oriented — the psychology of translation; think-aloud protocols, eye-tracking

Applied

  • Translator training — teaching methods, testing, curriculum
  • Translation aids — dictionaries, grammars, CAT tools
  • Translation criticism — evaluation, revision, reviews
  • Translation policy — the scholar advising on translation's place in society
The results of DTS feed back into the theoretical branch. Holmes himself stressed the branches interact; the divisions are somewhat artificial but allow a useful division of labour (Toury).

5. Developments Since Holmes & the van Doorslaer Map

  • Skopos theory (Reiss & Vermeer, Germany, 1970s–80s) — text purpose and type
  • Discourse analysis / systemic functional grammar (Hallidayan; Baker, Hatim & Mason) from the early 1990s
  • Descriptive & polysystem approaches (Even-Zohar, Toury, Tel Aviv), the Manipulation School (Hermans 1985), the 'cultural turn' (Bassnett & Lefevere 1990)
  • Postcolonial, gender, and Cannibalist approaches; Venuti's call for translator visibility (USA)
  • Technology: machine/automatic translation, audiovisual & multimodal, localization, corpus-based studies
van Doorslaer (2007)
A newer map distinguishes 'translation' (lingual mode, media, mode, field) from 'translation studies' (approaches, theories, research methods, applied TS), plus a 'basic transfer map' distinguishing a strategy (overall orientation of a text) from a procedure (a specific technique at a given point).

6. Discipline, Interdiscipline or Multidiscipline?

Interdisciplinarity: Recent research is markedly interdisciplinary. Following McCarty, an interdiscipline is 'the Phoenician trader among the settled nations' — it exists in the interstices and challenges how we organise knowledge. Translation studies has primary links to linguistics, modern languages, comparative literature, cultural studies and philosophy.
Is interdisciplinarity a strength or a threat? Daniel Gile warns that partnerships are often unbalanced — status and funding lie with the partner discipline — which may weaken translation studies' autonomy.

Exam Q&A · Check Yourself

Q: Distinguish the three meanings of the word 'translation'.
A: (1) the general subject field/phenomenon ('I studied translation'); (2) the product, i.e. the translated text; (3) the process of producing it, i.e. translating.
Q: Name and define Jakobson's three categories of translation.
A: Intralingual (rewording within the same language), interlingual ('translation proper', between two languages), and intersemiotic ('transmutation', between verbal and non-verbal sign systems, e.g. novel → film).
Q: Why is James Holmes important, and what did his 1972 paper do?
A: He gave the discipline its name ('translation studies') and, in 'The name and nature of translation studies', proposed an overall framework/map of the field — widely regarded as its founding statement.
Q: Explain the three kinds of descriptive translation studies (DTS).
A: Product-oriented (analysis of existing translations), function-oriented (their role in the recipient socioculture — contexts rather than texts), and process-oriented (the psychology of translating; think-aloud protocols, eye-tracking).
Q: What is the difference between a translation 'strategy' and a 'procedure' (van Doorslaer)?
A: A strategy is the overall orientation of a translated text (e.g. literal translation); a procedure is a specific technique used at a given point in the text (e.g. borrowing, calque).
