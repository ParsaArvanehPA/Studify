⚡ Functionalism in Translator Training · EXAM CRAM

Christiane Nord, Translating as a Purposeful Activity, Ch. 4 | Essential Points Only

🎯 Core Argument

Central thesis: Functionalism is most useful in translator training. Rather than the vague 'Well, it depends...', teachers can use the intended communicative function of the target text as a guideline: 'Let your translation decisions be guided by the function you want to achieve.' The chapter presents a model of text functions, three functional typologies of translation, and the role of norms and conventions.

📚 Key Terms & Concepts

Referential: Reference to objects/phenomena of the world; sub-functions: informative, metalinguistic, directive, didactic. Depends on the receiver sharing the relevant world-knowledge (the 'waters of Maine' example: incomprehensible to those who don't know they are ice-cold)
Expressive: The sender's attitude toward objects/phenomena; sub-functions: emotive, evaluative, ironic. Based on a shared value system — e.g. comparing a wife's eyes to a cow's is admiration in India, an insult in Germany
Appellative: Directed at the receiver's disposition to act/respond (imperatives, rhetorical questions, superlatives); like a dart that must hit the board — it fails if the receiver cannot cooperate (the 'saltines'/'Pledge of Allegiance' example)
Phatic: Establishing, maintaining or ending contact (small talk, opening proverbs). Relies on the conventionality of its form; a form conventional in one culture may be unconventional in another
House (1977): covert vs. overt: Covert translation — the ST function is kept invariant, so the TT aspires to the status of an original in the target culture (ST not specifically tied to the source community). Overt translation — has a second-level function; the receiver is made aware it is a translation (ST tied to the source community)
Reiss (1977): Correlates text concept, translation type and aim; any type may be justified, but she favours the 'communicative-translation' type whose form does not betray its translational origin
Conventions: Implicit, tacit, non-binding regulations of behaviour based on common knowledge and mutual expectation

🗝️ Rapid Q&A

Q: Name Nord's four text functions and their orientation.
A: Referential (world/objects), expressive (the sender's attitude), appellative (the receiver's disposition to act), and phatic (establishing/maintaining contact). The first three are from Bühler; the phatic is added from Jakobson.
Q: Explain the difference between documentary and instrumental translation.
A: Documentary translation produces a 'document' of a source-culture interaction — a text about a text with a metatextual function (interlinear, literal, philological, exoticizing). Instrumental translation produces an 'instrument' for a new interaction modelled on the ST (equifunctional, heterofunctional, homologous).
Q: Give the forms of instrumental translation with an example of each.
A: Equifunctional (same function — instructions for use), heterofunctional (different function — Gulliver's Travels for children), and homologous (same literary status — poetry translated by a poet).
Q: Distinguish House's covert and overt translation.
A: Covert — the ST function is kept invariant, so the TT can pass as an original in the target culture. Overt — the TT has a second-level function and the receiver is made aware it is a translation.
Q: Why is the translation brief especially important in training?
A: Trainees lack the professional experience to infer a translation's purpose from an artificial classroom situation, so each task must come with an explicit brief defining the conditions under which the target text should carry out its function.
🔥 In training, let the target-text function guide decisions. Know the four functions (referential/expressive/appellative/phatic) and above all Nord's documentary vs instrumental typology with its sub-forms.
