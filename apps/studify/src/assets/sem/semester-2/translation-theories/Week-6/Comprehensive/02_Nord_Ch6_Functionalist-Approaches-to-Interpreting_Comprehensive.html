Functionalist Approaches to Interpreting

Study Guide · Christiane Nord, Translating as a Purposeful Activity, Ch. 6
Skopostheorie applied to interpreting, and Pöchhacker on simultaneous interpretation

1. Core Argument

Central thesis: Since Skopostheorie is a general theory of translation, it applies to interpreting too. The chapter examines the role of interpreting in Skopostheorie, the 'from interpreting to translation' training approach, and Pöchhacker's functionalist model of simultaneous interpreting.
Know the term Translation (Kade) covering both modes, the differences and similarities between translating and interpreting, and Pöchhacker's key ideas: the conference as hypertext and the notion of diaculture.

2. Interpreting Within Skopostheorie

Translation (Kade, 1968): A generic German term (with German pronunciation) covering both written translation (Übersetzen) and oral interpreting (Dolmetschen) — seen as 'twins', two varieties of the same intercultural communicative interaction

Interpreting (Kade): The translation of a source-language text, presented only once (usually orally), into a target-language text which is very difficult to check and can hardly be corrected because of lack of time
Main difference: correctability: A written translation is potentially correctable after it is written down; interpreting must be regarded as complete at the moment of production. This non-correctability follows from specific conditions of reception and production:
  • In translation the source can be received repeatedly; in interpreting it is presented only once, received in successive parts
  • In translation both texts remain available; in interpreting both are transitory
  • In translation source and target are processed in a separate situation; in interpreting source and target communications share the same situational conditions
Main similarities: Both seek a communicative purpose (Skopos rule); both are a target-culture 'offer of information' about a source-culture offer of information; both must meet intratextual coherence; and both should be coherent with their source texts (fidelity rule).

3. Training: From Interpreting to Translation

The Y-model and Vermeer & Ammann's reform: German training follows the Y-model: shared initial training, then specialization into translation or interpreting. Vermeer & Ammann (1990) propose 'from interpreting to translation' — here interpreting means the original sense of 'making someone understand someone else's message'. Every would-be translator needs the basic ability to grasp a brief and retextualize a source — which is 'interpreting'.
Privileging interpreting in training makes students aware of extratextual clues (time, place, speaker, listeners) and the importance of communicative purpose. Because the 'source text' is not available in writing, students are not tempted to reproduce structures word-for-word — escaping the ST's 'hypnotic compulsion' (Wilss).

4. Pöchhacker: A Functionalist Approach to Simultaneous Interpreting

Simultaneous interpreting (Pöchhacker): 'The act of target text production in synchrony with the production and/or presentation of a source text'
The conference as hypertext: Individual speeches cannot be analysed as independent units each with its own Skopos; they are components of a greater semiotic whole — the conference itself, a hypertext forming a holistic unit. Pöchhacker locates the Skopos of simultaneous interpreting at the level of the conference assignment, not the individual target text.
The multimedial 'source text': The interpreter must take in a large amount of non-verbal acoustic and visual information (slides, transparencies, handouts, gestures, body-language) — all part of the 'source text' — while being limited mainly to verbal and paraverbal means of production. Time-lag can create an asynchronous combination of auditory and visual signals for the receiver.
Diaculture (Vermeer): The key to the 'which culture?' problem when English is a conference lingua franca: a group culture defined by shared professional background, common technical expertise and a history of interaction — transcending national/language borders. The common diacultural ground lets participants communicate successfully
Pöchhacker admits his model poses no serious challenge to other functionalist approaches, but shows the functional approach can be applied to simultaneous interpreting — a valuable basis for evaluating interpreters' output and for training, and a field where much research remains to be done.

Exam Q&A · Check Yourself

Q: Why does Skopostheorie claim to apply to interpreting?
A: Because it is a general theory of translation. Vermeer argues it applies to both translating and interpreting, since both are purposeful communicative actions governed by the Skopos rule.
Q: What is the main difference between translating and interpreting?
A: Correctability: a written translation is potentially correctable after being written down, whereas an interpretation must be regarded as complete at the moment of production — the source is presented only once and both texts are transitory.
Q: What does 'from interpreting to translation' mean in training?
A: Vermeer & Ammann's approach uses 'interpreting' in its original sense of making someone understand another's message. Every trainee translator needs this basic ability to grasp a brief and retextualize a source; privileging it foregrounds extratextual clues and communicative purpose, and avoids word-for-word reproduction.
Q: Explain Pöchhacker's idea of the conference as a 'hypertext'.
A: Individual speeches are not independent units each with its own Skopos; they are components of the greater semiotic whole of the conference. Pöchhacker therefore locates the Skopos of simultaneous interpreting at the level of the conference assignment.
Q: What is 'diaculture' and why is it useful for interpreting?
A: A group culture defined by shared professional background, technical expertise and interaction history, transcending national/language borders. When English is a conference lingua franca, the participants' common diacultural ground — not national culture — enables successful communication.
