Introduction: Toward a Science of Translating

Study Guide · Eugene A. Nida, Toward a Science of Translating, Ch. 1
The problems of interlingual communication and the field of translation

1. Core Argument

Central thesis: Never in history have so many people devoted so much effort to translation, yet the practice of translating has far outdistanced the theory. Nida argues that translation, like linguistics, is a valid subject for descriptive scientific study, while acknowledging the artistic sensitivity indispensable to first-rate literary translation.
Know Nida's framing of translation as a 'science', the form-vs-meaning and letter-vs-spirit tensions, Jakobson's three-way division of the field, and the many disciplines Nida draws on.

2. The Enduring Problems of Interlingual Communication

Ancient problems, modern urgency: From the multilingual scribes of ancient Babylon to simultaneous interpreting at the UN, the basic problems of interlingual communication remain the same — but modern technology makes effective understanding between peoples more urgent.
Opposition to translation
Translation has always had detractors: Showerman ('translation is meddling with inspiration'), Eastman ('almost all translations are bad'). Theological objections abounded too — medieval Jewish texts likened the Greek translation of the Law to the day Israel made the golden calf; Sir Thomas More opposed Bible translation. Yet 'a live sparrow is better than a stuffed eagle' (Fitzgerald).

3. The Fundamental Tensions

Languages differ radically: So different that some claim one cannot say in one language what was said in another — yet what unites mankind is greater than what divides, so a basis for communication always exists

Form vs. meaning: If the translator approximates the stylistic qualities of the original, they sacrifice meaning; strict adherence to literal content loses the stylistic flavour

The letter vs. the spirit: Being faithful to the things talked about can destroy the spirit of the original; over-reproducing the 'feeling' and 'tone' risks playing loose with the substance

Language is always changing: The medium of communication is constantly being remade; Casares: 'Translation is a customs house through which passes... more smuggled goods of foreign idioms than through any other linguistic frontier'

4. Is Translation an Art or a Science?

Is translating an art or a science? Practice has far outdistanced theory. No one denies the artistic elements, but linguists increasingly recognise the processes of translation are amenable to rigorous description. When Nida speaks of 'the science of translating' he means the descriptive aspect — just as linguistics is a descriptive science.
Nida's balance: those who insist translation is 'an art and nothing more' fail to probe its principles; those who take the opposite view rarely study translating enough to appreciate its artistic sensitivity.

5. The Field of Translation (after Jakobson)

  • Intralingual — rewording within the same language
  • Interlingual ('translation proper') — interpreting the signs of one language by another; concerned with the equivalence of both symbols and their arrangements, so the whole utterance must be understood
  • Intersemiotic ('transmutation') — transferring a message from one symbolic system to another (e.g. verbal message → flag signals; speech → sign language)
Cultural and stylistic restrictions: Translation faces restrictions from cultural context (e.g. Jiggs's corned beef and cabbage becomes rice and fish in Southeast Asia), from poetry (where form carries meaning), and from media (dubbing must match timing, syllabic structure and facial movements).
Why Bible translation is central
Bible translating surpasses all other types in range of subject matter, linguistic variety (1,200+ languages), historical depth, cultural diversity, manuscript evidence and accumulated data on principles — so it supplies rich data for a general theory, though the book is not restricted to Scripture.

6. The Interdisciplinary Foundations

Drawing on many fields: Nida grounds the study of translation in contemporary work across disciplines: anthropological semantics (von Humboldt), structural linguistics (Saussure), anthropology (Sapir, Whorf, Malinowski), general semantics (Korzybski's 'the map is not the territory'), symbolic logic (Russell, Wittgenstein: 'all philosophy is the philosophy of language'), psychology (Gestalt, Skinner) and psychiatry (Jung, Fromm).
A generative view of language
Following Chomsky (1957), Nida rejects seeing language as a fixed corpus of sentences and instead views it as a dynamic, generative mechanism capable of producing infinite utterances. The translator must decode, transfer and transform the whole message — not merely list correspondences. I. A. Richards: translation may be 'the most complex type of event yet produced in the evolution of the cosmos'.

Exam Q&A · Check Yourself

Q: What does Nida mean by a 'science of translating'?
A: The descriptive study of the transfer of a message from one language to another. Just as linguistics is a descriptive science, translation processes can be rigorously described — while still recognising the artistic sensitivity essential to literary translation.
Q: Explain the 'form vs. meaning' and 'letter vs. spirit' tensions.
A: If the translator approximates the original's style/form, they sacrifice much meaning; strict literal content loses the stylistic flavour. Likewise, fidelity to the letter can destroy the spirit, while over-reproducing tone and feeling risks betraying the substance.
Q: State Jakobson's three-way division of the field as used by Nida.
A: Intralingual (rewording), interlingual ('translation proper' between two languages), and intersemiotic ('transmutation' between symbolic systems, e.g. verbal message to flag signals).
Q: Why does Nida treat Bible translation as a key source of data?
A: Because it surpasses all other types in range of subject matter, linguistic variety (1,200+ languages), historical depth, cultural diversity, volume of manuscripts and accumulated data on principles — giving abundant, representative material for a general study of translation.
Q: Why does Nida adopt a 'generative' view of language (after Chomsky)?
A: Because a language is not a fixed corpus but a dynamic mechanism generating infinite utterances. To explain how a unique source message is decoded and re-created as an equally unique target message, the translator needs more than lists of correspondences.
