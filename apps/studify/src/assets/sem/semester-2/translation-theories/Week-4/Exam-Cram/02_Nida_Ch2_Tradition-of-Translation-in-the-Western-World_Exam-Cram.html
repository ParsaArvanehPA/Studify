Eugene A. Nida, Toward a Science of Translating, Ch. 2 | Essential Points Only

🎯 Core Argument

Central thesis: Despite major shifts across epochs and countries, two basic conflicts have persisted throughout the history of Western translation: literal vs. free translating, and emphasis on form vs. concentration on content. Nida surveys the tradition from antiquity to the 20th century to show these tensions recurring.
Two sets of conflicting 'poles' persist: (1) literal vs. free translating, and (2) emphasis on form vs. concentration on content. These are closely related but not identical. Terms like 'literal vs. free' and 'words vs. sense' are often mere battle cries, rarely analysed in detail.

🗝️ Rapid Q&A

Q: What are the two basic conflicts Nida identifies across the history of translation?
A: Literal vs. free translating, and emphasis on form vs. concentration on content. They are closely related but not identical, and recur throughout Western translation history.
Q: Summarise Dolet's five principles and their significance.
A: Understand the author's content and intention; have perfect knowledge of both languages; avoid word-for-word rendering; use common speech; produce the right overall tone. They are significant as the first formulation of a theory of translation.
Q: How did Dryden respond to the extreme freedom of his age?
A: He rejected Cowley's very free 'imitation', set out the triad metaphrase / paraphrase / imitation, and advocated the 'golden mean' of paraphrase, since 'it is impossible to translate verbally and well at the same time.'
Q: Why is the literal–free distinction described as a 'polar' one?
A: Because it is not a simple positive–negative dichotomy but a scale with many grades between the extremes, as illustrated by the range of Bible versions from strictly formal to fully idiomatic renderings.
Q: Describe the 'inspiration vs. philology' opposition in Bible translation.
A: Augustine accepted the tradition of the divinely inspired, miraculously identical Septuagint translation; Jerome rejected the divine inspiration of translators and adopted a philological approach — shared by most modern scholarship.
🔥 A sweep from the Rosetta Stone to machine translation, all showing two enduring conflicts: literal vs free and form vs content. Dolet gives the first theory; Dryden the golden mean; and Bible translation raises special inspiration-vs-philology problems.
