Equivalence and Equivalent Effect

Study Guide · Jeremy Munday, Introducing Translation Studies (2016), Ch. 3
Jakobson, Nida, Newmark and Koller on meaning and equivalence

1. Core Argument

Central thesis: To escape the old literal vs. free debate, theorists of the 1950s–60s pursued a more systematic analysis centred on meaning and equivalence. The chapter runs from Jakobson through Nida's formal/dynamic equivalence and the principle of equivalent effect, Newmark's semantic/communicative translation, and Koller's five equivalence relations.
The core exam items are Nida's formal vs. dynamic equivalence + equivalent effect, Newmark's semantic vs. communicative translation, and Koller's five equivalence types — plus the criticisms of equivalent effect.

2. Jakobson: Linguistic Meaning and Equivalence

Signifier / signified (Saussure): The signifier is the spoken/written signal; the signified is the concept. Together they form the linguistic sign, which is arbitrary

Key claim: 'There is ordinarily no full equivalence between code-units' (e.g. Russian syr is not identical to English cheese, which excludes cottage cheese = tvarog)
Translatability: Interlingual translation substitutes whole messages, not separate code-units. Jakobson: 'Languages differ essentially in what they must convey and not in what they may convey' (differences of gender, aspect, semantic fields). 'All is conveyable in any existing language' — only poetry, with its unity of form and sense, is 'untranslatable' and requires 'creative transposition'.

3. Nida and the Science of Translating

The influence of Chomsky
Nida borrows Chomsky's generative-transformational grammar. The ST surface structure is analysed into kernels (basic structural elements) by back-transformation, then transferred and restructured into the TT surface structure — a three-stage model: analysis → transfer → restructuring. Four functional classes: events, objects, abstracts, relationals.
Linguistic meaning: The relationship between linguistic structures (e.g. his house = possession vs his kindness = quality)

Referential meaning: The denotative 'dictionary' meaning (son = male child)

Emotive/connotative meaning: The associations a word produces ('Don't worry, son' = endearment)
Techniques of meaning analysis: Hierarchical structuring (superordinate animal vs hyponyms), componential analysis (plotting kinship terms by sex, generation, lineality), and semantic structure analysis (the many senses of spirit), all stressing that meaning is 'conditioned' by context.
Formal vs. Dynamic Equivalence: Nida discards 'literal/free/faithful' for two basic orientations:
  • Formal equivalence (later 'formal correspondence') — focuses on the message itself, in form and content; ST-oriented; typical of 'gloss translations' with footnotes, giving access to source language and customs
  • Dynamic equivalence (later 'functional') — based on the principle of equivalent effect: 'the relationship between receptor and message should be substantially the same as that between the original receptors and the message'; aims at 'complete naturalness of expression', the 'closest natural equivalent'
Four basic requirements + priority rule: A translation must: (1) make sense; (2) convey the spirit and manner of the original; (3) have a natural and easy form; (4) produce a similar response. As a general rule, 'correspondence in meaning must have priority over correspondence in style'.
Criticisms: Lefevere — equivalence too word-focused; van den Broeck & Larose — equivalent effect/response is impossible (how can it be measured, and how can a text have the same effect in two different cultures and times?). Gentzler attacks Nida's theological, proselytizing standpoint; ironically some religious groups find dynamic equivalence near-sacrilegious. The famous case: 'greet one another with a holy kiss' → 'give one another a hearty handshake all round'.

4. Newmark: Semantic and Communicative Translation

Communicative translation: Attempts to produce on its readers an effect as close as possible to that on the readers of the original (resembles Nida's dynamic equivalence)

Semantic translation: Attempts to render, as closely as syntax allows, the exact contextual meaning of the original (resembles formal equivalence)
Newmark's departure from Nida: Newmark feels equivalent effect is 'illusory' and that the conflict of loyalties (source vs. target) is the overriding problem. Semantic translation stays within the SL culture, is more complex and detailed (tends to overtranslate), suits serious literature; communicative translation transfers foreign elements to the TL culture, is smoother (tends to undertranslate), suits the majority of texts. Where equivalent effect is secured, he holds literal word-for-word translation to be 'the only valid method'; where the two conflict, communicative translation wins (e.g. bissiger Hund → 'beware of the dog!', not 'dog that bites!').

5. Koller: Equivalence Relations

Correspondence: Belongs to contrastive linguistics; compares two language systems (Saussure's langue); e.g. identifying false friends

Equivalence: Relates to equivalent items in specific ST–TT pairs and contexts (Saussure's parole)
Koller's five types of equivalence: Constrained by 'double linkage' (the ST on one side, the receiver's communicative conditions on the other):
  • Denotative — the extralinguistic content ('content invariance')
  • Connotative — lexical choices, near-synonyms ('stylistic equivalence')
  • Text-normative — text-type norms (linked to Reiss)
  • Pragmatic ('communicative') — oriented to the receiver (= Nida's dynamic equivalence)
  • Formal — form and aesthetics, wordplay, individual style ('expressive equivalence'; not to be confused with Nida's 'formal equivalence')
The equivalence types are hierarchically ordered by the needs of the communicative situation: the translator tries denotative first, then moves up (connotative, text-normative...) as needed.

6. Later Developments in Equivalence

Pym: natural vs. directional equivalence
Natural equivalence — identifying naturally-occurring terms in SL and TL (as in glossaries/term bases). Directional equivalence — rendering ST meaning in the TT (as translation memories impose existing equivalents via exact/fuzzy matches).
Equivalence remains central but criticised for circularity (Kenny: 'equivalence defines translation, and translation defines equivalence'). Bassnett: translation involves far more than replacing lexical/grammatical items. In descriptive studies the contentious tertium comparationis (the invariant against which segments are measured) entails inevitable subjectivity. Toury shuns a prescriptive definition, instead identifying the web of relations between ST and TT.

Exam Q&A · Check Yourself

Q: Distinguish Nida's formal and dynamic equivalence.
A: Formal equivalence focuses on the message itself in form and content, is ST-oriented and often uses gloss translations with footnotes. Dynamic equivalence is based on the principle of equivalent effect, aims at complete naturalness and the closest natural equivalent, tailoring the message to the receptor.
Q: What is the 'principle of equivalent effect' and why is it criticised?
A: The relationship between receptor and message should be substantially the same as that between the original receptors and the message. Critics (van den Broeck, Larose) argue effect/response is impossible to measure and a text cannot produce the same effect across different cultures and times.
Q: Compare Newmark's semantic and communicative translation.
A: Semantic translation renders the exact contextual meaning as closely as syntax allows, stays within the SL culture and tends to overtranslate (serious literature). Communicative translation aims at an effect as close as possible to the original's, transfers foreign elements to the TL culture, and tends to undertranslate (most texts).
Q: List Koller's five equivalence relations.
A: Denotative (content), connotative (lexical/stylistic choices), text-normative (text-type norms), pragmatic (receiver-oriented = Nida's dynamic), and formal (form/aesthetics/wordplay). They are hierarchically ordered by communicative need.
Q: How does Jakobson treat translatability and poetry?
A: He holds that 'all is conveyable in any existing language' because interlingual translation substitutes whole messages, not code-units — languages differ in what they must convey. Only poetry, with its unity of form and sense, is 'untranslatable' and requires 'creative transposition'.
