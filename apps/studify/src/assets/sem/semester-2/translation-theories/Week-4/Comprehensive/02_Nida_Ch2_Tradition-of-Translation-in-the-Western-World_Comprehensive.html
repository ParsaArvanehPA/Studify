The Tradition of Translation in the Western World

Study Guide · Eugene A. Nida, Toward a Science of Translating, Ch. 2
A history from the Rosetta Stone to the 20th century, and the basic conflicts in translation theory

1. Core Argument

Central thesis: Despite major shifts across epochs and countries, two basic conflicts have persisted throughout the history of Western translation: literal vs. free translating, and emphasis on form vs. concentration on content. Nida surveys the tradition from antiquity to the 20th century to show these tensions recurring.
Focus on the chronological landmarks (Rosetta Stone → Jerome → Luther → Dolet → Dryden → Tytler → 20th c.) and the two enduring 'poles', plus the special theological problems of Bible translation.

2. Antiquity

  • The Rosetta Stone (2nd c. BCE) — the most famous ancient translation; a biscript plus Greek translation that unlocked Egyptian hieroglyphics
  • Ancient Babylon and Assyria used corps of scribes who translated edicts; early bilingual word-lists ('dictionaries') survive on cuneiform tablets
  • The Septuagint — the Greek Old Testament for the large Greek-speaking Jewish community of Alexandria
  • Greco-Roman translators (Livius Andronicus, Cicero, Horace) translated the Greek classics with skill, but produced no systematic study of principles

3. Jerome and the Middle Ages

St Jerome
The most systematic and disciplined of the ancient translators. He rendered 'sense for sense and not word for word', citing Cicero's authority and the way the Gospel writers freely adapted the Hebrew/Septuagint. Aquila (2nd c.) by contrast made a painfully literal Greek Old Testament.
Medieval centres: Medieval translation was mostly religious essays in stiff ecclesiastical Latin. Then Baghdad (9th–10th c.) became a centre for translating Greek classics into Arabic, and Toledo (12th c.) for translating them into Latin (often via Syriac and Arabic). Maimonides insisted word-for-word renderings make for a confused translation.

4. Renaissance to the Reformation

Martin Luther
The dominant 16th-century figure. He deserves credit for sensing the importance of full intelligibility, and worked out the implications of his principles: shifts of word order, modal auxiliaries, added connectives, suppression of untranslatable terms, using phrases for single words, shifting metaphors, and careful exegetical accuracy. Tyndale paralleled Luther in the first modern English New Testament.
Etienne Dolet's five principles (1540): Credit for the first formulation of a theory of translation goes to Dolet (later executed for heresy). His five headings:
  • The translator must perfectly understand the content and intention of the author
  • The translator should have perfect knowledge of both source and target languages
  • The translator should avoid word-for-word rendering — it destroys the meaning and ruins the beauty
  • The translator should use forms of speech in common usage
  • Through choice and order of words, the translator should produce the right overall 'tone'

5. From the 17th Century to the Present

'Les Belles Infideles': 17th–18th-century secular translation showed almost unrestrained freedom — the age of les belles infidèles. Cowley claimed to have 'taken, left out and added what I please' to Pindar (his 'imitation').
Dryden and Tytler
Dryden disapproved of Cowley's extreme freedom and set out three types — metaphrase, paraphrase, imitation — preferring the 'golden mean' of paraphrase: 'It is impossible to translate verbally and well at the same time.' Tytler (1790) set out three principles: complete transcript of the idea; same style and manner; all the ease of the original composition. (Campbell accused Tytler of plagiarism.)
The 19th and 20th centuries: The 19th c. brought pedantic literalism and 'super-sophistication' — the idea that 'nothing worth translating can be translated' (Matthew Arnold wanted to reproduce Homer for 'the competent scholar'; the English Revised Version was painfully literal). The 20th c. brought a radical change: new concepts of communication, a premium on intelligibility, and five developments — structural linguistics, the Summer Institute of Linguistics (Wycliffe), the United Bible Societies, the journal Babel (UNESCO), and machine translation.

6. The Basic Conflicts in Translation Theory

Two sets of conflicting 'poles' persist: (1) literal vs. free translating, and (2) emphasis on form vs. concentration on content. These are closely related but not identical. Terms like 'literal vs. free' and 'words vs. sense' are often mere battle cries, rarely analysed in detail.
Grades between the poles: The literal–free difference is not a positive–negative dichotomy but a polar distinction with many grades between, as seen in successive Bible versions (ASV → RSV → NEB → Phillips) ranging from strict formal renderings to fully idiomatic ones. Form vs. content becomes acute in poetry, where a 'song' must be reproduced as a 'song' (Denham: not just 'language into language, but poesie into poesie').
Special theological problems
Bible translation raises special issues framed as oppositions: inspiration vs. philology (Augustine, who accepted the miraculous inspiration of the Septuagint translators, vs. Jerome, firmly on the side of a philological approach), tradition vs. contemporary authority, and theology vs. grammar.

Exam Q&A · Check Yourself

Q: What are the two basic conflicts Nida identifies across the history of translation?
A: Literal vs. free translating, and emphasis on form vs. concentration on content. They are closely related but not identical, and recur throughout Western translation history.
Q: Summarise Dolet's five principles and their significance.
A: Understand the author's content and intention; have perfect knowledge of both languages; avoid word-for-word rendering; use common speech; produce the right overall tone. They are significant as the first formulation of a theory of translation.
Q: How did Dryden respond to the extreme freedom of his age?
A: He rejected Cowley's very free 'imitation', set out the triad metaphrase / paraphrase / imitation, and advocated the 'golden mean' of paraphrase, since 'it is impossible to translate verbally and well at the same time.'
Q: Why is the literal–free distinction described as a 'polar' one?
A: Because it is not a simple positive–negative dichotomy but a scale with many grades between the extremes, as illustrated by the range of Bible versions from strictly formal to fully idiomatic renderings.
Q: Describe the 'inspiration vs. philology' opposition in Bible translation.
A: Augustine accepted the tradition of the divinely inspired, miraculously identical Septuagint translation; Jerome rejected the divine inspiration of translators and adopted a philological approach — shared by most modern scholarship.
