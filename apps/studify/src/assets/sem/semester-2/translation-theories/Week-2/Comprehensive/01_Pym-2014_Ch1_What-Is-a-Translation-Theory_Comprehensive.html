What Is a Translation Theory?

Study Guide · Anthony Pym, Exploring Translation Theories (2014), Ch. 1
From theorizing to theories to paradigms — and why theory matters

1. Core Argument

Central thesis: All translators theorize all the time. Whenever a translator identifies a problem, generates possible solutions and selects one, they bring into play ideas about what translation is — that is theorizing. Pym distinguishes private theorizing, public theories, and the larger paradigms that group theories, and he organises his whole book around paradigms rather than individual theorists.
Know the chain theorizing → theories → paradigms, the 'Tory' example, and why studying theory is useful. Also note Pym's own terminological choice of 'start text' over 'source text'.

2. From Theorizing to Theories

Theorizing: The internal, ongoing mental activity of generating and selecting solutions to a translation problem — done 'in split seconds', by trained and untrained translators alike

Theory (etymology): From Greek theā (view) + -horan (to see) — 'to look at a view'. A theory 'sets the scene' where the generation and selection of solutions takes place
The 'Tory' example: Translating British 'Tory' offers many legitimate options: keep the English term with a footnote, give 'Conservative Party', name a target-culture equivalent party, or omit it. Choosing between them means bringing ideas about translation into play — i.e. theorizing.
When theorizing becomes public theory
Private theorizing becomes public theory only when there are disagreements over different ways of translating. One translator says 'translation should explain the source culture'; another 'it should make things understandable to the target culture'; a third 're-situate everything in the target culture'. These are competing theoretical positions.
'Start text' not 'source text': Pym deliberately says 'start text' rather than 'source text' because 'source' implies a pristine origin, like a river bubbling up — whereas any text may itself be made of translations and reworked fragments. Names-for-things are never neutral; they conceal guiding ideas, forming scenes coherent enough to be called theories.

3. From Theories to Paradigms

Paradigm: A set of underlying principles shared by a group of theories — general ideas, relations and principles with internal coherence and a shared point of departure (cf. Kuhn 1962)
Example of a paradigm: One set of theories uses the terms source, target and equivalence, sharing the point of departure of comparing start and target texts. People within this paradigm can discuss translation productively together.
When paradigms clash, people use the word 'translation' to mean quite different things, and debate becomes pointless. The equivalence theorist and the descriptive theorist (who compares translations with non-translations) talk past each other. Outcomes: continued tension, revolution (one wins), or mutual ignorance. Pym's aim is to overcome this mutual ignorance.
The paradigms in Pym's book
Equivalence (split into natural and directional), purposes (Skopos), descriptions, uncertainty, localization, and cultural translation. The order is roughly chronological (from the 1960s), except uncertainty, present throughout — the fundamental conflict between uncertainty and equivalence is the basic problem all paradigms respond to. Newer theories do not simply replace older ones (a lot of equivalence lives on in localization and technology).

4. Why Study Translation Theories?

No guarantee of better work: There is no empirical evidence that a translator who knows theories works better. Untrained translators may even work faster — fewer doubts, less time reflecting on the obvious.
  • Theory helps most with problems that have no established solution, where creativity is required — it poses productive questions and suggests novel answers
  • Theories can be agents of change when moved between professional cultures (e.g. the idea of translation as 'saying later')
  • They enhance the public image of translators — showing translation is complex enough to study at university
  • In arguments/with clients, theory gives translators tools to defend positions and understand other positions (e.g. explaining 'compensatory correspondence' to a client)
  • A plurality of theories widens the range of solutions a translator thinks of (generative side) and gives reasons for choosing among them (selective side)
Pym argues for plurality: generative theories (descriptive, deconstructionist, cultural-translation) widen options; selective theories (ethics, purposes) help decide — and the selective kind has fallen unfashionable, which he wants to correct.

5. How Should Translation Theories Be Studied?

Never separate theory from practice: Since all translators are always theorizing, it is wrong to separate theory from practice. The best use of theory is in active discussion of different ways of solving real translation problems. Students should come to theories only when they find they actually need them — activities should come at the beginning of a class, not as an appendage at the end.
The real learning of theory, even for the self-learner, should be in dialogue and debate — working against the book's own fixed written form.

Exam Q&A · Check Yourself

Q: What does Pym mean by claiming that 'translators are theorizing all the time'?
A: Whenever translators identify a problem, generate possible solutions, and select one, they bring ideas about what translation is into play. This internal generation-and-selection is theorizing — done in split seconds by trained and untrained translators alike.
Q: Distinguish theorizing, theories and paradigms.
A: Theorizing is the private, internal activity; it becomes public theory when disagreements over ways of translating arise; a paradigm is a set of underlying principles shared by a group of theories, with internal coherence and a shared point of departure (cf. Kuhn).
Q: Why does Pym prefer 'start text' to 'source text'?
A: Because 'source' wrongly implies a pristine, natural origin, whereas any text may itself be built from translations and reworked fragments. Names-for-things are never neutral — they carry theoretical assumptions.
Q: What happens when paradigms clash, and what is Pym's aim?
A: People use 'translation' to mean different things and debate becomes pointless; outcomes are tension, revolution or mutual ignorance. Pym's aim is to overcome the mutual ignorance by understanding rival views.
Q: Give reasons Pym offers for studying translation theory, despite no proof it makes translators 'better'.
A: It helps with problems lacking established solutions, poses productive questions, acts as an agent of change, raises translators' public image, gives tools to defend and understand positions in arguments, and (with a plurality of theories) widens the range of solutions and reasons for choosing among them.
