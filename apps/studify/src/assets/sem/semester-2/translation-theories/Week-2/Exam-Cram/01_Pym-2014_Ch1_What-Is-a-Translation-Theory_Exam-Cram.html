⚡ What Is a Translation Theory? · EXAM CRAM

Anthony Pym, Exploring Translation Theories (2014), Ch. 1 | Essential Points Only

🎯 Core Argument

Central thesis: All translators theorize all the time. Whenever a translator identifies a problem, generates possible solutions and selects one, they bring into play ideas about what translation is — that is theorizing. Pym distinguishes private theorizing, public theories, and the larger paradigms that group theories, and he organises his whole book around paradigms rather than individual theorists.

📚 Key Terms & Concepts

Theorizing: The internal, ongoing mental activity of generating and selecting solutions to a translation problem — done 'in split seconds', by trained and untrained translators alike
Theory (etymology): From Greek theā (view) + -horan (to see) — 'to look at a view'. A theory 'sets the scene' where the generation and selection of solutions takes place
Paradigm: A set of underlying principles shared by a group of theories — general ideas, relations and principles with internal coherence and a shared point of departure (cf. Kuhn 1962)
When paradigms clash, people use the word 'translation' to mean quite different things, and debate becomes pointless. The equivalence theorist and the descriptive theorist (who compares translations with non-translations) talk past each other. Outcomes: continued tension, revolution (one wins), or mutual ignorance. Pym's aim is to overcome this mutual ignorance.

🗝️ Rapid Q&A

Q: What does Pym mean by claiming that 'translators are theorizing all the time'?
A: Whenever translators identify a problem, generate possible solutions, and select one, they bring ideas about what translation is into play. This internal generation-and-selection is theorizing — done in split seconds by trained and untrained translators alike.
Q: Distinguish theorizing, theories and paradigms.
A: Theorizing is the private, internal activity; it becomes public theory when disagreements over ways of translating arise; a paradigm is a set of underlying principles shared by a group of theories, with internal coherence and a shared point of departure (cf. Kuhn).
Q: Why does Pym prefer 'start text' to 'source text'?
A: Because 'source' wrongly implies a pristine, natural origin, whereas any text may itself be built from translations and reworked fragments. Names-for-things are never neutral — they carry theoretical assumptions.
Q: What happens when paradigms clash, and what is Pym's aim?
A: People use 'translation' to mean different things and debate becomes pointless; outcomes are tension, revolution or mutual ignorance. Pym's aim is to overcome the mutual ignorance by understanding rival views.
Q: Give reasons Pym offers for studying translation theory, despite no proof it makes translators 'better'.
A: It helps with problems lacking established solutions, poses productive questions, acts as an agent of change, raises translators' public image, gives tools to defend and understand positions in arguments, and (with a plurality of theories) widens the range of solutions and reasons for choosing among them.
🔥 All translators theorize; theory becomes public through disagreement; paradigms group theories by shared principles. Study theory not because it guarantees better work, but because it widens your options and lets you defend and understand positions.
