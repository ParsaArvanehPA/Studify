⚡ The Notion of 'Problem' in Translation Studies · EXAM CRAM

Gideon Toury, Descriptive Translation Studies – and beyond, Ch. 2 | Essential Points Only

🎯 Core Argument

Central thesis: Translation Studies rarely reflects on its own concepts. Toury isolates the 'translation problem' and shows it is not one concept but three distinct terms — problem1, problem2, problem3 — each belonging to a different context of expert discourse. The mere recurrence of a word does not guarantee sameness of concept.

📚 Key Terms & Concepts

problem1: Source-oriented, prospective, posited. Concerns source texts and how they constrain an envisaged translation — a matter of translatability, not actual translation. It is utopian, abstract and potential; its complement is solvability (a way to go about solving), and it is 'never really solved'
problem2: Target-oriented, retrospective, reconstructed. Manifested in a concrete translation act that has already been performed; identified only by mapping actual translations onto their assumed sources (coupled pairs of replacing + replaced segments). Concrete and realized; its complement is solution2
problem3: Process-oriented, processual, reconstructed. Factual like problem2 but reconstructed step by step as the act unfolds; a dynamic notion that may change during the act. Requires more than the end-product — interim replacements, reflections, think-aloud protocols, keystroke logs (Translog); its complement is solution3 (final or interim)
Pseudotranslation (fictitious translation): A text composed as if it were a translation but with no genuine source text. It activates the Source-Text Postulate — the tacit assumption that every assumed translation has a source — which here proves impossible to locate

🗝️ Rapid Q&A

Q: Why does Toury argue that 'problem' is not a single concept?
A: Because concepts are embedded in conceptual networks; the same word used across different discourse contexts denotes different terms. He identifies three — problem1, problem2, problem3 — each with different existence conditions and meaning.
Q: Distinguish problem1, problem2 and problem3.
A: problem1 is source-oriented, prospective and posited — a matter of translatability, never really solved. problem2 is target-oriented, retrospective and reconstructed from coupled pairs in an actual translation. problem3 is process-oriented and processual, reconstructed step by step as the act unfolds, allowing interim solutions.
Q: Which concepts belong exclusively to problem1 and problem3 respectively?
A: Translatability and solvability belong only to problem1; the notion of an interim solution belongs only to problem3.
Q: What is a pseudotranslation and which postulate does it activate?
A: A text composed as if it were a translation but with no genuine source text. It activates the Source-Text Postulate — the tacit assumption that an assumed translation must have a source, which here cannot be located.
Q: Why are pseudotranslations significant for Translation Studies?
A: Because the host culture treats them like genuine translations, they reveal the functions translation has in a culture. They provide a safe way to introduce novelties, allow individual reinvention, imply subordination to a prestigious culture, and can be used for deliberate culture planning.
🔥 'Problem' splits into three: problem1 (prospective/translatability), problem2 (retrospective/coupled pairs), problem3 (processual/interim solutions). Pseudotranslations, though sourceless, are legitimate, revealing objects that let cultures import prestige and introduce novelty.
