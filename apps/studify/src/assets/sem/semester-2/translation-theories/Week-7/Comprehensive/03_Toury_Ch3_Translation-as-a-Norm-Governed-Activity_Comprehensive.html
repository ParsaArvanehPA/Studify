Translation as a Norm-Governed Activity

Study Guide · Gideon Toury, Descriptive Translation Studies – and beyond, Ch. 3
Norms, the adequacy–acceptability continuum, and regularities of behaviour

1. Core Argument

Central thesis: Translation is a culturally-determined, norm-governed activity. Because it is inherently variable across and within cultures, its regularities can only be explained by reconstructing the norms that govern it. Norms mediate between the abstract 'competence' and actual 'performance' — they are the central concept for a descriptive-explanatory account.
The exam essentials: the definition of norms (between rules and idiosyncrasies), the two overarching norm-values adequacy vs. acceptability, and Toury's initial/preliminary/operational norms (if included) — plus norms as explanatory hypotheses reconstructed from regularities.

2. From Conventions to Norms

Conventions: The outcome of a group's 'sociability' (Davis) — shared agreements about acceptable actions, creating order and predictability. But conventions are vague and not binding enough to guide behaviour

Norms: The translation of general values (right/wrong, adequate/inadequate) into performance 'instructions' for concrete situations — specifying what is prescribed, forbidden, tolerated and permitted. Unlike conventions, norms involve sanctions (positive or negative)
The semiotic square of normativity: Norms specify what has to be done (obligation), what must not be done (prohibition), what may be done (non-prohibition), and what doesn't have to be done (non-obligation).

3. Norms Between Rules and Idiosyncrasies

A graded continuum: Constraints on behaviour form a scale from general, objective rules at one extreme to idiosyncratic mannerisms at the other. Being intersubjective, norms occupy the central part of the scale — in fact nearly the whole continuum minus the two extremes. Rules = more objective norms; idiosyncrasies = more subjective norms.
Gradation and relativity: Norms are not monolithic: some are more binding than others, and their validity shifts over time. What is a mere preference for a large group may be strongly binding within a subgroup (poetry translators, subtitlers, central vs peripheral translators, etc.). Along the time axis, whims can catch on and become rule-like, and norms can weaken — tied to shifts of status and struggles for domination.

4. Norms as Explanatory Hypotheses

Regularities vs. norms: Norms are not directly observable. What we observe are regularities of behaviour in recurrent situations; the norms must be reconstructed from these regularities as explanatory hypotheses. The researcher's route is a mirror image of the translator's: looking back from the end of the process to what caused it. The correlation is not 1:1 — one strategy may be fed by several norms, or one norm may underlie several strategies.
Norms can be extracted from two main sources: (1) the products — translated texts and their regularities; and (2) normative statements by translators, critics, etc. — but these are 'slanted' and must be handled with circumspection, stripped of the interests they serve.

5. The 'Value' Behind Translation: Adequacy vs. Acceptability

Two principles / poles: Following Wittgenstein's 'family resemblance', there is no single feature all translations share. The 'value' of translation consists of two principles, interwoven:
Adequacy: Subscription to the norms of the source text (and, through it, the source language and culture) — producing a representation of the existing source text

Acceptability: Subscription to the norms of the target culture/language — producing a text designed to occupy a certain position/slot in the host culture
An inevitable compromise: The normal state is a degree of incompatibility between the two: getting closer to one means distancing from the other. A translation is never wholly adequate or wholly acceptable, but a blend — an ad hoc compromise reflecting the influence of the norms. (Note: Toury's 'adequacy' — source-norm adherence — is almost the opposite of Reiss's use of the term.)

6. Norms, Acts and Events; Competing Norms

Act vs. event; freedom of choice: The act (the mental process) is embedded in a socially significant event (its context). The mentalist and socio-cultural approaches are complementary, not opposed. Norms do not deny free choice — 'All is foreseen but freedom of choice is granted': the actual decision is the individual's, and norms intersect with the translator's liberties (and can be related to Bourdieu's habitus).
Competing norms and change: At any moment there tend to be three competing types of norm: those dominating the centre (mainstream), remnants of previous mainstream norms (now marginal, 'old-fashioned'), and rudiments of a possible new set ('avant-garde'). Novice translators tend to play safe with 'respectable' (often dated) norms; large-scale changes are usually made by experienced, prestigious translators who can afford to deviate — and a new norm exists only once others follow.
Translation vs. related activities
Norms differ across translation, translation assessment/criticism (constructing an argument about existing translations) and translator training (which often imports 'respectable' concepts, sometimes clashing with the norms that actually operate, causing novices to 'unlearn' on entering the profession).

Exam Q&A · Check Yourself

Q: What is a norm, and how does it differ from a convention?
A: A norm translates general values (right/wrong, adequate/inadequate) into performance 'instructions' for concrete situations, and crucially involves sanctions. A convention is vaguer and non-binding — norms provide the specificity and force conventions lack.
Q: Why does Toury place norms at the centre of the constraints continuum?
A: Because constraints range from objective rules to subjective idiosyncrasies, and norms — being intersubjective — occupy the central part, effectively the whole continuum minus the extremes. Rules are more objective norms; idiosyncrasies are more subjective ones.
Q: Why are norms called 'explanatory hypotheses'?
A: Because norms are not directly observable. Researchers observe regularities of behaviour and reconstruct the norms behind them as explanatory hypotheses — mirroring, in reverse, the course taken by the translator.
Q: Explain the adequacy–acceptability distinction.
A: Adequacy is adherence to source-text norms; acceptability is adherence to target-culture norms. Every translation is an inevitable compromise/blend of the two — never wholly one or the other — reflecting the influence of the governing norms.
Q: Describe the three types of competing norms in a culture.
A: Norms dominating the centre (mainstream), remnants of previous mainstream norms (marginal, 'dated'), and rudiments of a possible new set ('avant-garde'). Novices tend to follow safe/dated norms; prestigious, experienced translators drive change once others follow them.
