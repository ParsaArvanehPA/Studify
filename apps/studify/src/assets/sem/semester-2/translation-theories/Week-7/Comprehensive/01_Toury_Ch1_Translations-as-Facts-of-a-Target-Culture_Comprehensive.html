Translations as Facts of a 'Target' Culture

Study Guide · Gideon Toury, Descriptive Translation Studies – and beyond, Ch. 1
Target-orientedness, cultural facts, and the notion of 'assumed translation'

1. Core Argument

Central thesis: Translations are facts of the culture that hosts them. Their position, function and form are determined first and foremost by considerations originating in the target culture, not the source. This target-oriented axiom underpins Descriptive Translation Studies (DTS) and leads to the key working concept of the 'assumed translation'.
Know: target-orientedness, translations as cultural facts, the descriptive-explanatory goal (vs Skopos theory's applied goal), and the three postulates of 'assumed translation' (Source-Text, Transfer, Relationship).

2. A Target-Oriented Framework

The crucial step: Toury suggests that translations be regarded as facts of the culture that would host them — their function and systemic status are constituted within the target culture. This was 'somewhat unorthodox' in the 1970s, when the field was strongly source-oriented, preoccupied with protecting the 'SL text rights'.
Toury vs. Skopostheorie
Both are target-oriented and emerged around the same time (Vermeer 1978; Toury 1977). But their goals differ: mainstream Skopos theory is applied — it aims to make 'improvements' in the world; Toury's programme is descriptive-explanatory — it aims to give exhaustive accounts of whatever is regarded as translational, on the way to generalizations about translational behaviour. (Nord's reintroduction of 'loyalty' as an a priori moral principle reopens the gap.)

3. Translations as Cultural Facts

The most normal practice: When a text is offered as a translation, it is 'quite readily accepted bona fide as one, no further questions asked' — which is why fictitious (pseudo) translations can pass as genuine. Adopting culture-internal distinctions avoids imposing alien categories on the object of study.
  • A translation can never inhabit the same space as its source (not even in bilingual editions)
  • Cultures resort to translating to fill gaps — so translation is 'as good as initiated by the target culture'; the initial state is one of deficiency in the recipient culture
  • Translations tend to deviate from the target culture's sanctioned patterns (because of the postulate of retaining 'invariant under transformation' some ST features), so they often constitute special systems, even 'genres', of their own
  • What is unthinkable is a translation hovering in 'limbo between cultures' — Toury rejects Pym's notion of an autonomous 'interculture'

4. Proper Contextualization

The reformulated assumption: Translations are facts of target cultures — on occasion of a peculiar status, sometimes constituting identifiable (sub)systems of their own, but of the target culture in any event. Establishing a text's status as a translation is necessary but not sufficient; proper contextualization — always case-specific — is part of the study itself.
The German train example
One of four versions of a warning against improper use of the emergency brake reads like English, yet fits no English-speaking culture's codified version. The only tenable conclusion is that the English notice is situated within German culture — in a specific sector where English is assigned a role. Don't assume language = culture.

5. The Notion of 'Assumed Translation'

Against restrictive definitions: Restrictive, essentialist definitions of translation are counter-productive: they shrink the field and breed circular reasoning (whatever is selected because it fits the definition reaffirms it). Instead, Toury works with 'assumed translation': all utterances in a target culture that are presented or regarded as translations, on any grounds whatever.
Source-Text Postulate: The assumption that there exists another text, in another culture/language, with chronological and logical priority, which served as a point of departure — even if it cannot actually be located (hence pseudotranslations are legitimate objects of study)

Transfer Postulate: The assumption that the process involved a transfer from the assumed source text of certain features the two now share

Relationship Postulate: The assumption that there are tangible relationships tying the translation to its assumed original, based on the shared/transferred features
Benefits of the working hypothesis: (1) a considerable expansion of the range of objects of study; (2) functional operability even when the basic principle seems inapplicable (e.g. no source text can be found).

6. Discovery vs. Justification Procedures

The method: Study starts with the observables — the translated texts and their constituents — then maps target segments onto assumed source segments, establishing coupled pairs of 'replacing + replaced' segments. From these, translation relationships and a revised notion of equivalence (the relationship that constitutes the norm for the pair) are established. Justification procedures mirror the discovery procedures; the progression is helical, not linear.
Redefined equivalence: Toury does not use equivalence prescriptively. He takes it as a given that a TT is equivalent to its ST, and instead studies the web of relations — equivalence is 'that translation relationship which would have emerged as constituting the norm for the pair of texts under study'.

Exam Q&A · Check Yourself

Q: What does Toury mean by 'translations are facts of the target culture'?
A: That a translation's position, function and systemic status are determined first and foremost by considerations originating in the host (target) culture — not the source — and are constituted within it. This is the target-oriented axiom of DTS.
Q: How does Toury's programme differ from Skopostheorie, though both are target-oriented?
A: Skopos theory is applied — it seeks to make improvements/changes in practice; Toury's is descriptive-explanatory — it seeks exhaustive accounts of what is regarded as translational, aiming at generalizations about translational behaviour.
Q: Why does Toury prefer 'assumed translation' to a fixed definition?
A: Because restrictive, essentialist definitions shrink the field and cause circular reasoning. 'Assumed translation' — anything presented or regarded as a translation — expands the range of objects of study and stays operable even when no source text can be found (e.g. pseudotranslations).
Q: State the three postulates of 'assumed translation'.
A: The Source-Text Postulate (a prior text existed elsewhere), the Transfer Postulate (features were transferred from it), and the Relationship Postulate (tangible relationships tie the translation to its assumed original).
Q: How does Toury redefine equivalence?
A: Not prescriptively: he takes it as given that a TT is 'equivalent' to its ST and instead identifies the web of relations between them. Equivalence is the translation relationship that constitutes the norm for the pair of texts studied.
