⚡ Translation as a Norm-Governed Activity · EXAM CRAM

Gideon Toury, Descriptive Translation Studies – and beyond, Ch. 3 | Essential Points Only

🎯 Core Argument

Central thesis: Translation is a culturally-determined, norm-governed activity. Because it is inherently variable across and within cultures, its regularities can only be explained by reconstructing the norms that govern it. Norms mediate between the abstract 'competence' and actual 'performance' — they are the central concept for a descriptive-explanatory account.

📚 Key Terms & Concepts

Conventions: The outcome of a group's 'sociability' (Davis) — shared agreements about acceptable actions, creating order and predictability. But conventions are vague and not binding enough to guide behaviour
Norms: The translation of general values (right/wrong, adequate/inadequate) into performance 'instructions' for concrete situations — specifying what is prescribed, forbidden, tolerated and permitted. Unlike conventions, norms involve sanctions (positive or negative)
Adequacy: Subscription to the norms of the source text (and, through it, the source language and culture) — producing a representation of the existing source text
Acceptability: Subscription to the norms of the target culture/language — producing a text designed to occupy a certain position/slot in the host culture

🗝️ Rapid Q&A

Q: What is a norm, and how does it differ from a convention?
A: A norm translates general values (right/wrong, adequate/inadequate) into performance 'instructions' for concrete situations, and crucially involves sanctions. A convention is vaguer and non-binding — norms provide the specificity and force conventions lack.
Q: Why does Toury place norms at the centre of the constraints continuum?
A: Because constraints range from objective rules to subjective idiosyncrasies, and norms — being intersubjective — occupy the central part, effectively the whole continuum minus the extremes. Rules are more objective norms; idiosyncrasies are more subjective ones.
Q: Why are norms called 'explanatory hypotheses'?
A: Because norms are not directly observable. Researchers observe regularities of behaviour and reconstruct the norms behind them as explanatory hypotheses — mirroring, in reverse, the course taken by the translator.
Q: Explain the adequacy–acceptability distinction.
A: Adequacy is adherence to source-text norms; acceptability is adherence to target-culture norms. Every translation is an inevitable compromise/blend of the two — never wholly one or the other — reflecting the influence of the governing norms.
Q: Describe the three types of competing norms in a culture.
A: Norms dominating the centre (mainstream), remnants of previous mainstream norms (marginal, 'dated'), and rudiments of a possible new set ('avant-garde'). Novices tend to follow safe/dated norms; prestigious, experienced translators drive change once others follow them.
🔥 Translation is norm-governed. Norms sit between objective rules and idiosyncrasies, are reconstructed from behavioural regularities, and pull between adequacy (source norms) and acceptability (target norms) — every translation being a compromise of the two.
