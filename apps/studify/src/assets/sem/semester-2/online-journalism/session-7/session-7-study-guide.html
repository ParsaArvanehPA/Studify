Translation Strategies in Global News

Session 7: A Foreignised Approach to Translation in the News | Based on Claire Scammell, Translation Strategies in Global News (2018), Ch. 7 | MA Translation Studies

Session Overview

About this session

This chapter presents the book's practical payoff: five proposed updates to the Reuters Handbook of Journalism that together constitute the "foreignised" approach — increasing readers' contact with the foreign source language and culture, and helping journalists stay alert to accuracy issues in translated speech, without abandoning news's need for clarity.

1. Update 1 — Retention of Foreign Language (Culture-Specific Concepts)

The Handbook currently has no guidance on translating culture-specific concepts at all; its only relevant entry discourages foreign language generally, except "where no generally recognised English equivalent exists." Update 1 adds a new sub-paragraph specifically for culture-specific concepts:

foreign language culture-specific concepts: "Consider retaining foreign language where replacing a foreign term with the closest English equivalent would involve losing cultural-specificity important to the reader's understanding. Add an explanation/translation in the first instance. Subsequent uses can be left in the original language without repeating the translation/explanation. Where the foreign cultural concept appears in the headline/lead and there is not space to provide explanatory text, use the foreign term rather than generalising with a translation, and add the explanation at the next opportunity."

Examples of Update 1's impact

LocationOriginalForeignised
DUMMY, para. 12"...a call to rid the Paris suburbs of...""...a call to rid the Paris banlieue — the high-rise residential outskirts of the city — of..."
CRIME POLICIES, para. 12"...want the riot police to leave...""...want the CRS (riot police) to leave..."
SECURITY, headline"...beef up French suburb security""...beef up French banlieue security"
SECURITY, lead"...in France's poor suburbs...""...in France's poor banlieues, residential areas on the outskirts of big cities..."
Was this informed by real Reuters/press practice? Yes — a Factiva analysis of banlieue in headlines/leads found AFP retains it in the headlines of 4 reports and the lead of 1; British "quality press" titles retain it far more (e.g. The Independent: 13 headline, 19 lead instances) — evidence that retaining foreign culture-specific concepts in headlines/leads is a viable strategy in principle, even for agency-style writing.

2. Update 2 — Retention of Foreign Language (Quotations)

Update 2 introduces a new sub-paragraph specifying three cases for considering retention of foreign language within translated quotations:

  1. when using a "broken" quote (only needed when the words are contentious, inflammatory or colourful — meaning translation is likely to be problematic);
  2. to catch distinctions and nuances in important passages of speeches;
  3. to convey some of the flavour of the speaker's language.

Where the exact wording cannot be translated accurately/objectively, journalists should signal this, e.g. "meaning something like X," or offer two possible renderings, e.g. "which most closely translates as X or Y."

Examples of Update 2's impact

LocationOriginalForeignised
THUGOCRACY, lead"...the work of a 'thugocracy' of criminals...""...the work of a 'voyoucratie' (thugocracy) of criminals..."
IMMIGRATION, para. 9Sarkozy said 2005 had been a "break year"...Sarkozy said 2005 had been an "année de rupture (break year)"...
DUMMY, para. 5"...saying: 'He's nice really. He's young'""...saying: 'Pardon! He's nice really. He's young'"

Update 2(B) also revises the 'quotations' entry: it replaces the existing instruction to "give a literal translation if a statement is tendentious" with an instruction to "consider retaining some of the original language" instead — since a literal translation risks unintentionally distancing the speaker (recall Bassnett's Al Qaeda "cars of death" example, Session 3).

3. Update 3 — Paraphrase for Accuracy in Translated Quotations

The Handbook already instructs journalists to "avoid excessive use of direct quotes in English when the speaker has spoken in another language" — Update 3 leaves this intact but adds:

"Translating a direct quote in an idiomatic way can involve moving so far from the original wording that the translation effectively becomes a paraphrase of what the speaker has said; in such cases take care to present the speech as paraphrase rather than as a direct quote, using a 'broken' quote where necessary."

The update doesn't change how much direct quotation is used — it's about ensuring quotation marks are only used where the translation genuinely stays close enough to the original to earn them.

4. Update 4 — Showing Deletions in Translated Direct Quotations

The Handbook already treats quotes as "sacred" — nothing may be altered inside quotation marks except deletions, shown with an ellipsis ("..."). Update 4 extends this existing rule explicitly to translation:

"When translating foreign language quotes as direct speech, be alert to and show (as described above) both any deletions that already exist as a result of intra-lingual editing, and any that are introduced by the translation process."

This update doesn't foreignise Reuters' approach directly — it ensures existing accuracy standards are applied just as carefully when translation, not just editing, is the reason for a deletion.

5. Update 5 — Signalling the Foreign Context of Quotations

A new paragraph, unrelated to any existing Handbook guidance, advising journalists to signal the foreign context of a quotation — so readers know they're reading a translation, not the speaker's original words:

"Where appropriate, signal that the English wording of a direct or paraphrased quote is a translation by making direct reference to the foreign context, e.g. 'Speaking on French radio, …'"
Examples of Update 5's impact: "...said in comments to be published in Tuesday's Le Parisien" → unchanged, already signals the foreign context. "...Sarkozy told local leaders as he unveiled the plan..." → "...Sarkozy told local leaders at the Elysée Palace as he unveiled the plan..." (moderately foreignising, since readers may not recognise the institution). "'Our policy of firmness is paying off' he told a news conference" → "'Our policy of firmness is paying off' he told a French news conference."

6. Discussion: What the Five Updates Add Up To

Only two of the five updates (1 and 2) directly call for readers to come into contact with the foreignness of the source language itself. Updates 3 and 4 address the accuracy of quotations by raising institutional awareness, without necessarily foreignising the resulting text. Update 5 doesn't relate to translation strategy directly — it's about signalling the journalist's intervention to the reader.

The updates are deliberately minimal and grounded: each is derived from wording, guidance or exceptions already present elsewhere in the Handbook (e.g. Update 2's three cases are drawn from existing references to quotation's function; the retention strategy already appears, atypically, in the THUGOCRACY report itself). Where an existing paragraph doesn't conflict with the foreignised approach's aims, it is left completely intact.

On word count: while retention does add words in principle, the analysis shows the impact can be minimal — an AFP example shows banlieue retained in one sentence with its explanation efficiently placed earlier in the same paragraph, adding no real length at all.

✦ Why This Matters for a Translator

This chapter is the concrete deliverable the whole book has been building toward — worth studying as a model of how translation-studies theory becomes an actionable style-guide change.

  • Notice how conservative the updates are: none of them mandate foreignisation everywhere. Update 1 says "consider retaining" — the decision stays with the journalist, case by case, which is exactly what "foreignised to a degree" (Session 1) means in practice.
  • Update 2(B)'s replacement of "give a literal translation" with "consider retaining some of the original language" is a precise fix for the exact failure mode shown by Bassnett's "cars of death" example — literal translation can foreignise accidentally and harmfully; deliberate retention foreignises transparently and on purpose.
  • Updates 3 and 4 are a reminder that "foreignising" isn't only about keeping foreign words — protecting quotation accuracy (showing when a "quote" is really a paraphrase, showing translation-introduced deletions) is just as central to the book's ethical argument as retaining vocabulary.

Why it matters for you: when you evaluate any news translation from now on, you have five concrete, testable questions to ask: does it retain a culture-specific term where needed? does it retain/flag untranslatable quoted language? is a "quote" really a paraphrase in disguise? are translation-introduced omissions marked? is the reader told they're reading a translation at all?

Review Questions

Q&A for Exam Preparation

Q1: What does Update 1 add to the Handbook, and under what specific space constraint does it give special guidance?
A: Update 1 adds a new sub-paragraph calling for retention of foreign language when translating culture-specific concepts, if the closest English equivalent would lose important cultural meaning — with an explanation added at first use. Where the concept appears in a headline or lead with no space for an explanation, it instructs journalists to use the foreign term anyway (rather than a generalising translation) and add the explanation at the next opportunity.
Q2: What are the three cases Update 2 specifies for considering retention of foreign language in quotations?
A: (1) When using a "broken" quote, since a broken quote is only needed for words that are contentious, inflammatory or colourful — meaning translation is likely problematic; (2) to catch distinctions and nuances in important passages of speeches; (3) to convey some of the flavour of the speaker's language.
Q3: What existing Handbook instruction does Update 2(B) revise, and why?
A: It revises the instruction to "give a literal translation if a statement is tendentious and likely to be the subject of close analysis," replacing it with an instruction to consider retaining some of the original language instead. This is because a literal translation can unintentionally distance the speaker from the reader (as in Bassnett's "cars of death" Al Qaeda example), an effect the journalist likely never intended.
Q4: What problem does Update 3 address, and what solution does it propose?
A: It addresses the risk that an idiomatic translation of a direct quote can drift so far from the original wording that it effectively becomes a paraphrase. The proposed solution: in such cases, present the speech as paraphrase rather than as a direct quote (using a "broken" quote where appropriate), rather than misleadingly keeping full quotation marks.
Q5: How does Update 4 extend an existing Handbook rule to translation specifically?
A: The Handbook already requires deletions within quotation marks to be shown with an ellipsis. Update 4 makes explicit that journalists must show deletions in this way whether they result from ordinary intra-lingual editing or from the translation process itself — closing a gap where the existing rule didn't mention translation.
Q6: What does Update 5 call for, and how does the "Elysée Palace" example illustrate a moderately foreignising effect?
A: Update 5 calls for journalists to signal, where appropriate, that a quote's English wording is a translation, by referencing its foreign context (e.g. "Speaking on French radio..."). Naming "the Elysée Palace" (rather than a generic "presidential residence") is moderately foreignising because readers unfamiliar with the institution encounter an unexplained foreign-culture reference — though it is not strongly foreignising since Elysée Palace is a commonly recognised English term for it.
Q7: Which two of the five updates most directly expose readers to the foreignness of the source language itself, and how do the other three differ?
A: Updates 1 and 2 (retention of foreign language for culture-specific concepts and quotations, respectively) most directly expose readers to source-language foreignness. Updates 3 and 4 instead focus on protecting the accuracy of quotations by increasing institutional awareness of translation's impact, without necessarily changing the language readers see. Update 5 focuses on signalling the journalist's intervention rather than on translation strategy itself.
Q8: How does the chapter show that retention does not necessarily make a report longer?
A: Using an AFP example, it shows banlieue retained in the final sentence of a paragraph, with its explanation ("high-immigration neighbourhood") already given efficiently earlier in the same paragraph — meaning the foreign term and its explanation could both be included without meaningfully expanding the report's word count.

Key Points to Review

  • Five updates to the Reuters Handbook constitute the "foreignised" approach
  • Update 1: retain foreign culture-specific concepts where an English equivalent would lose meaning; special headline/lead space-constraint guidance
  • Update 2 (A+B): retain foreign language in quotations in 3 defined cases; replaces "give a literal translation" with "consider retaining original language" for tendentious statements
  • Update 3: use paraphrase, not quotation marks, when an idiomatic translation has drifted too far from the original wording
  • Update 4: show translation-introduced deletions the same way as intra-lingual editing deletions
  • Update 5: signal to readers, where appropriate, that a quote is a translation (foreign-context reference)
  • Only Updates 1 and 2 directly foreignise language; 3 and 4 protect accuracy; 5 signals intervention
  • Updates are deliberately minimal, grounded in existing Handbook wording/exceptions, and largely word-count-neutral in practice
